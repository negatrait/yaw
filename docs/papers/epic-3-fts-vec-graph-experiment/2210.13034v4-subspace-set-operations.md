###### Abstract

In the field of natural language processing (NLP), continuous vector representations are crucial for capturing the semantic meanings of individual words.
Yet, when it comes to the representations of *sets* of words, the conventional vector-based approaches often struggle with expressiveness and lack the essential set operations such as union, intersection, and complement.
Inspired by quantum logic, we realize the representation of word sets and corresponding set operations within pre-trained word embedding spaces.
By grounding our approach in the linear subspaces, we enable efficient computation of various set operations and facilitate the *soft* computation of membership functions within continuous spaces. Moreover, we allow for the computation of the F-score directly within word vectors, thereby establishing a direct link to the assessment of sentence similarity.
In experiments with widely-used pre-trained embeddings and benchmarks, we show that our subspace-based set operations consistently outperform vector-based ones in both sentence similarity and set retrieval tasks.11 1 Our code is publicly available at <https://github.com/yoichi1484/subspace>

## §​ 1 Introduction

Embedding-based word representations have become fundamental in the field of natural language processing (NLP).
Models like word2vec [Mikolov et al. (2013)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib23) and GloVe [Pennington et al. (2014)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib26), along with recent Transformer-based architectures [Vaswani et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib29); [Devlin et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib14), have underscored the significance of embeddings in capturing the complexities of linguistic semantics.

The importance of representing collections of words is pivotal in understanding concepts and relationships within language contexts [Zaheer et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib33); [Zhelezniak et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib35).
For instance, while words like “apple” and “orange” each carry their distinct meanings, together they represent the broader concept of fruits. Another example of important application is a sentence representation [Zaheer et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib33).
The set of words in a sentence captures the overall meaning, allowing for computations such as text similarity [Agirre et al. (2012)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib4).

Against this backdrop, our research recognizes the significance of applying set operations in NLP and explores a new approach.
Set operations enable a richer representation of relationships between collections of words, leading to more accurate semantic analysis based on context.
For example, employing set operations allows for a clearer understanding of shared semantic features and differences among word groups within a text.
This directly benefits tasks like determining semantic similarity and expanding word sets.

In response to these challenges, our study introduces a novel methodology that exploits the principles of *quantum logic* [Birkhoff and Von Neumann (1936)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib9), applied within embedding spaces to define set operations.
Our proposed framework adopts a subspace-based approach for representing word sets, aiming to maintain the intricate semantic relationships within these sets.
We represent a word set as a subspace which is spanned by pre-trained embeddings.
Additionally, it adheres to the foundational laws of set theory as delineated in the framework of quantum logic.
This compliance ensures that our set operations, such as union, intersection, and complement, are not only mathematically robust but also linguistically meaningful when applied them in pre-trained embedding space.

We first introduce a subspace set representation along with basic operations ($\cap$, $\cup$, and $\in$).
Subsequently, to highlight the usefulness of our proposed framework, we introduce two core set computations: text similarity and set membership.
The empirical results consistently point towards the notable superiority of our approach; our straightforward approach of spanning subspaces with pre-trained embedding sets enables a rich set representation, and we demonstrated its consistent performance enhancement in downstream tasks ([Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ §​ 1 Introduction ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities")).
Our research contributions include:

1. 1\.

   The introduction of continuous set representations and a framework for set operations, enabling more effective manipulation of word embedding sets ([§​ 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S4 "§​ 4 Subspace-based Set Representations ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities")).

2. 2\.

   By simply transitioning from a vector set representation to a subspace, and incorporating a subspace-based indicator function, we observe a salient improvement in performance across all text similarity benchmarks ([§​ 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S5 "§​ 5 Semantic Textual Similarity Task ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities")).

3. 3\.

   We apply subspace-based basic operations ($\cap,\cup,$ and $\in$) to set expansion task and achive high performance ([§​ 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S6 "§​ 6 Text Concept Set Retrieval Task ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities")).

## §​ 2 Preliminaries

To make the following discussion clear, we define several symbols.
The sets of tokens in two sentences ($A$ and $B$) are denoted as $A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots\},B=\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots\}$ respectively.
The sets of contextualized token vectors are denoted as $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}=\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2},\dots\},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}=\{\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}_{2},\dots\}$, where $\boldsymbol{a}$ and $\boldsymbol{b}$ are token vectors generated by the pre-trained embedding model such as BERT.
The subspace spanned by $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}$ is denoted as $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}=\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2},\dots)$.
Note that the bases of the subspace is orthonormalized.

## §​ 4 Subspace-based Set Representations

We propose the representations of a word set and set operations based on *quantum logic* [Birkhoff and Von Neumann (1936)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib9).
They hold advantages of geometric properties in an embedding space, and the set operations are guaranteed to hold for the laws of a set defined in quantum logic.

### §​ 4.1 Quantum logic

While word embedding represents a word’s meaning as a vector in linear space, quantum mechanics similarly represents a quantum state as a vector in linear space.
These two intuitively different fields are very close to each other in terms of the representation and the operation of information.

Quantum logic [Birkhoff and Von Neumann (1936)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib9) theory describes quantum mechanical phenomena.
Intuitively, it is a framework for *set operations in a vector space*.
In quantum logic, a set of vectors is represented as a linear subspace in a Hilbert space, and such set operations as union, intersection, and complement are defined as operations on subspaces.
Quantum logic, which employs a complete orthomodular lattice as its system of truth values, guarantees to hold various set operations, such as De Morgan’s laws $\overline{(\mathit{A}\cap\mathit{B})}=\overline{\mathit{A}}\cup\overline{\mathit{B}}$ and $\overline{(\mathit{A}\cup\mathit{B})}=\overline{\mathit{A}}\cap\overline{\mathit{B}}$, idempotent law: $\mathit{A}\cap\mathit{A}=\mathit{A}$, and double complement: $\overline{\overline{\mathit{A}}}=\mathit{A}$.

### §​ 4.2 Set Operations in an Embedding Space

The representations of an element, a set, and such set operations as union, intersection, and complement in quantum logic can be applied directly in a word embedding space because it is a Euclidean space and therefore also a Hilbert space.
However, since set similarity and set membership for a word embedding space are still missing in quantum logic, we propose a novel formulation of those operations using subspace-based representations, which is consistent with quantum logic.
The correspondence between symbolic and subspace-based set operations is shown in [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Basic set operations ‣ §​ 3 Symbolic Set Operations ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities").

#### Basic set operations

The *complement* of set $\mathit{A}$, denoted by $\overline{\mathit{A}}$, is represented by the orthogonal complement of subspace $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$:

|  | $\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{\overline{A}}}\coloneqq(\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}})^{\bot}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\mid\exists\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}},\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}=0\}\text{.}\end{split}$ |  | (7) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | --- |

The *union* of two sets, $\mathit{A}$ and $\mathit{B}$, denoted by $\mathit{A}\cup\mathit{B}$, is represented by the sum space of two subspaces, $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$ and $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}$:

|  | $\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A\cup B}}\!\coloneqq\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}+\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}\!=\!\{\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{b}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{a}\!\in\!\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}},\boldsymbol{b}\!\in\!\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}\}\text{.}\!\!\!\end{split}$ |  | (8) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | --- |

The *intersection* of two sets, $\mathit{A}$ and $\mathit{B}$, denoted by $\mathit{A}\cap\mathit{B}$, is represented by the intersection of two subspaces, $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$ and $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}$:

|  | $\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A\cap B}}\coloneqq\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}\cap\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\mid\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}\}\text{.}\end{split}$ |  | (9) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | --- |

The basis of the intersection can be computed based on singular value decomposition (SVD). The bases are the vectors shared by the two subspaces.

#### Hard membership

The set membership in the embedding space (e.g., $\mathit{boy}\in\mathit{Male}$) can be represented by the inclusion of a vector into a subspace (e.g., $\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{boy}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{Male}}$) and
given by the following indicator function:

|  | $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{hard}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{}},\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}})\coloneqq\begin{cases}1\quad(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}),\\ 0\quad(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{}}\notin\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}).\\ \end{cases}$ |  | (10) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ---- |

However, this binary decision fails to exploit the geometric properties of the word embedding space regarding semantic similarity.
Suppose we quantify the degree of membership of word $\mathit{boy}$ for word set $\mathit{Male}$ consisting of many masculine nouns other than $\mathit{boy}$.
Even if $\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{boy}}$ is located very close to $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{Male}}$ due to its semantic similarity to masculine nouns, $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{hard}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{boy}},\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{Male}})$ must return $0$ because $\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{boy}}$ must not be located *exactly* on subspace $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{Male}}$ defined by $\mathit{Male}$.
It must return $1$ based on the masculine property of word $\mathit{boy}$.
Such *hard* membership defined by Eq. ([10](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S4.E10 "In Hard membership ‣ §​ 4.2 Set Operations in an Embedding Space ‣ §​ 4 Subspace-based Set Representations ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities")) is incompatible with an embedding space.

#### Soft membership: Subspace indicator function

Instead, we define another membership function called *subspace indicator function* $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{subspace}}$ that returns continuous values from $0$ to $1$ depending on the following minimum angle between vector $\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{w}}$ and subspace $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$ (the first canonical angle):

|  | $\begin{split}\!\!\!\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{subspace}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{}},\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}})&\coloneqq\max\left\{\!\left.{\frac{&#124;\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{}}&#124;}{\&#124;\boldsymbol{a}\&#124;\&#124;\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{}}\&#124;}}\right&#124;\boldsymbol{a}\in{\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}}\!\right\}\!\text{.}\!\!\end{split}$ |  | (11) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ---- |

This captures the degree of membership between a word and a word set, represented by the angle between a word vector and a subspace.
It is a natural extension of $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{hard}}$, i.e., $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{subspace}}$ returns $1$ when $\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{w}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$ and $0$ when $\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{w}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{\overline{A}}}$.

The key distinction of our subspace indicator function approach lies in its ability to leverage the comprehensive information encapsulated within pre-trained word embedding space. The subspace indicator function does not simply find the nearest individual word from the set. Instead, we consider the *closeness* of the query word to the entire set as a whole, by projecting the query word into the subspace spanned by the pre-trained embeddings (as illustrated in the figure of the subspace indicator function function in [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Basic set operations ‣ §​ 3 Symbolic Set Operations ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities")). This way, we account not just for the individual word similarities, but also for the overall semantic coherence of the word set.
The detailed process for computing this subspace indicator function can be found in Algorithm [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Weighting by Importance ‣ §​ 4.3 Set Similarity ‣ §​ 4 Subspace-based Set Representations ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities").

### §​ 4.3 Set Similarity

#### Limitation of symbolic set similarities

Suppose we quantify the set similarity between $\mathit{A}=\{$*A*, *boy*, *walks*, *in*, *this*, *park*$\}$ and $\mathit{B}=\{$*The*, *kid*, *runs*, *in*, *the*, *square*$\}$,
which represent semantically similar sentences.
The challenge with traditional symbolic set similarities, such as recall, is that they primarily rely on the exact overlap of words between the sets.
These semantically similar sentences share only one word $\{\mathit{in}\}$ between $\mathit{A}$ and $\mathit{B}$: $&#124;\mathit{A}\cap\mathit{B}&#124;=1$.
To address this shortcoming, it is essential to compute vector-based set similarity, such as BERTScore.

#### Three types of similarity in BERTScore

BERTScore is a method that uses embeddings to approximately calculate $R$, $P$, and the $F$-score:

|  | $\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{BERT}}$ | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{&#124;A&#124;}\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{vectors}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}})\text{,}$ |  | (12) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- | ---- |
|  | $\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{BERT}}$ | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{&#124;B&#124;}\sum_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{vectors}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}})\text{,}$ |  | (13) |
|  | $\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{BERT}}$ | $\displaystyle=2\frac{P_{\mathrm{BERT}}\cdot R_{\mathrm{BERT}}}{P_{\mathrm{BERT}}+R_{\mathrm{BERT}}}\text{,}$ |  | (14) |

where a sentence is represented as a set of token vectors $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}$.$\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{vectors}}$ is the indicator function for vector sets.
Intuitively, this indicator function represents the calculation of *selecting one token from the sentence* and serves as a flexible extension of the binary indicator function $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{set}}$.
It returns a continuous similarity score between $-1$ and $1$ for a token, depending on its similarity with the tokens in the sentence.
Specifically, $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{vectors}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}})$ quantifies to what extent the $i$-th token vector $\boldsymbol{a}_{i}$ in sentence $A$ is semantically included in sentence $B$ by taking the maximum cosine similarity between $\boldsymbol{a}_{i}$ and the token vectors in $B$:

|  | $\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{vectors}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}})=\max_{\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}}\cos(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{b}_{j})\in[-1,1]\text{,}$ |  | (15) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ---- |

where $\cos(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{b}_{j})$ is the cosine similarity between $\boldsymbol{a}_{i}$ and $\boldsymbol{b}_{j}$.

#### Limitations of BERTScore’s Indicator Function

The indicator function $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{vector}}$ lies at the heart of BERTScore, playing a crucial role in the computation of $P_{\mathrm{BERT}}$, $R_{\mathrm{BERT}}$, and $F_{\mathrm{BERT}}$. However, a critical limitation arises from BERTScore’s reliance on maximum cosine similarity for its indicator function, which severely hinders its ability to capture the rich and nuanced meanings conveyed by a sentence. [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ §​ 4.3 Set Similarity ‣ §​ 4 Subspace-based Set Representations ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities") starkly illustrates this limitation through the visualization of BERTScore’s $\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{vectors}}$ calculation. Consider the sentence We are the king and queen, which evokes a broader, more abstract concept of royalty through the co-occurrence of king and queen. When seeking an alignment for royalty, BERTScore’s indicator function heavily favors the token with the highest cosine similarity — in this case, king. This approach leads to a severe alignment bias towards the meaning of a single word, while the complex and implicit meanings conveyed by the entire sentence are overlooked.

#### SubspaceBERTScore

To overcome the limitations of BERTScore regarding the expressiveness of its indicator function, we propose SubspaceBERTScore.
This method extends BERTScore by employing the concept of subspace-based sentence representation and indicator function.

## §​ 6 Text Concept Set Retrieval Task

In this section, we evaluate the capability of our proposed set operations ($\cap$, $\cup$, and $\in$) in effectively representing word sets.

#### Task

We evaluate our set operations by the set expansion task introduced by [Zaheer et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib33).
In this task, the model is given a set of words that share a common concept or theme.
The objective is to expand this set by retrieving relevant words from a vocabulary that fit the same concept.
For instance, if the initial set includes words like “apple”, “banana”, and “peach”, the task would be to identify and add other fruit names (e.g., “orange”) to this set.
For the evaluation, we follow [Zaheer et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib33).
We report recall (R@k) and Median, that indicate whether the words in the test set can be ranked higher.

#### Embeddings

We used the most standard pre-trained word embeddings in all of our experiments: 300-dimensional GloVe66 6 <https://nlp.stanford.edu/projects/glove/> [Pennington et al. (2014)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib26), which was pre-trained with Common Crawl, and 300-dimensional word2vec77 7 <https://code.google.com/archive/p/word2vec/> [Mikolov et al. (2013)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib23), which was pre-trained with Google News.

#### Set Expansion with Subspace Indicator Function

To illustrate our subspace-based set expansion method (Subspace Set), we consider a set of fruit-related words. For example, let’s take $\mathit{S}_{\mathrm{fruit}}=\{\mathit{apple},\mathit{banana},\dots\}$. This set is divided into two subsets: a ’span’ subset used for creating a subspace representation, and a ’test’ subset for evaluation. Let’s assume $\mathit{orange}\notin\mathit{S}_{\mathrm{fruit\_span}}$ is a target word for testing.
(1) From the ’span’ subset, we generate a subspace: $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{Fruit}}=\mathrm{span}(\mathit{S}_{\mathrm{fruit\_span}})$. For instance, if $\mathit{S}_{\mathrm{fruit\_span}}=\{\mathit{apple},\mathit{banana},\dots\}$, then $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{Fruit}}=\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{apple}},\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{banana}},\dots)$.
(2) We define a subspace indicator function, which computes the degree to which a word vector belongs to the subspace. For a word $\mathit{w}$, the membership score is calculated as $\mathrm{score}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{subspace}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{orange}},\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{Fruit}})$. This score reflects the extent to which $\mathit{w}$ aligns with the semantic characteristics of the subspace.
This method effectively expand the set $\mathit{S}_{\mathrm{fruit}}$ by identifying words that share semantic properties with the subspace defined by the initial set.

#### Baselines

We compared several baselines, which don’t require training on word sets, to our method.Random just selects words randomly from the dataset’s vocabulary.
A simple unsupervised baseline with word embeddings uses the nearest neighbors in the embedding space (Near)88 8 While [Zaheer et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib33) does not provide details about this method, we have inferred through our replication experiments that it uses a method based on the cosine similarity between the query word vector and other vectors to obtain the nearest neighbor..
We also compare a method based on fuzzy sets ([Zhelezniak et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib35), Fuzzy set;) with our method.
Similar to our method, their method is designed to exploit both the flexibility of word vectors and rich set operations.
Fuzzy Set represents word set $\mathit{A}$ by max-pooled word vectors $\boldsymbol{s}=\max_{\mathit{w}\in\mathit{A}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{w}}$.
One major difference from our method is that Fuzzy set represents a set of word vectors by compressing them into a vector of fixed dimensions.
Although the Text Concept Set Retrieval task requires computing the degree of a word’s membership for a word set, their method does not provide it.
We instead used cosine similarity $\cos(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{w}},\boldsymbol{s})$ between word vector $\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathit{w}}$ of word $\mathit{w}\in\mathit{V}$ and $\boldsymbol{s}$ as the degree of membership to apply fuzzy sets to the task.

## §​ 7 Related Work

Symbol-based similarities between word sets have been proposed, such as Jaccard coefficient [Jaccard (1901)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib16); [Manning and Schütze (2001)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib21); [Thada and Jaglan (2013)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib28) and TF-IDF-based similarity [Jurafsky (2000)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib17).
Unfortunately, symbol-based methods cannot capture the semantic similarity of similar sets or words when the symbols are different.

While many studies have explored representing word sets in pre-trained embedding spaces [Kusner et al. (2015)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib18); [Yokoi et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib32), they primarily focus on set similarity.
Our approach, however, extends beyond this by developing a comprehensive framework for various set operations within these spaces.
Utilizing subspace properties, our method not only represents word sets but also performs a range of versatile operations, such as calculating textual similarities and membership degrees.

Many methods for learning the representation of sets have been proposed because of the wide range of possible applications [Zaheer et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib33); [Pellegrini et al. (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib25); [Lee et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib19); [Vilnis and McCallum (2015)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib31); [Athiwaratkun and Wilson (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib7).
In contrast, our approach does not require additional training.
This enables us to compute set representations and operations using popular general-purpose language models, which are trained on the general domain [Brown et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib12).

## §​ 8 Conclusion

This study introduces a novel framework for set representation and operations within pre-trained embedding spaces, employing linear subspaces grounded in quantum logic.
This approach extends the scope of conventional embedding set operations by incorporating vector-based representations.

## Ethical Considerations

We recognize the importance of addressing the inherent biases in pre-trained models, such as gender stereotypes. In our experiment, we used RoBERTa, which has gender biases [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib27).
We used this model in its original state to preserve the experimental conditions of BERTScore, acknowledging that such biases may influence our results.
However, we would like to emphasize that the focus of our work, which lies in sentence similarity, does not inherently add to or magnify these ethical concerns.

## Limitations

Our SubspaceBERTScore is built upon the foundation of BERTScore, which presents a limitation in that our results and findings are inherently dependent on the characteristics and performance of BERTScore. While we chose BERTScore due to its robustness and popularity in the field, potential biases or shortcomings intrinsic to BERTScore might be incorporated into our extension. Nevertheless, this constraint also suggests future research possibilities, such as applying our subspace-based approach to other base sentence similarity metrics, further expanding the versatility and applicability of our method.

The experiments we conducted were exclusive to BERT and RoBERTa. Testing our methodology with other pre-trained models, like GPT-3 [Brown et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib12), could broaden its applicability and establish its robustness across various pre-trained models.

We evaluated our methodology primarily using English datasets. This decision was made to streamline our initial explorations rather than due to an inherent language-specific bias in our approach. We expect that our subspace-based methodology will be effective across various languages.

## Appendix A Pseudocodes

The pseudocodes for our SubspaceBERTScore and basic set operations are shown in Algorithms [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#alg3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ Appendix A Pseudocodes ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities"), [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#alg4 "Algorithm 4 ‣ Appendix A Pseudocodes ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities"), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#alg5 "Algorithm 5 ‣ Appendix A Pseudocodes ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities"), and [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#alg6 "Algorithm 6 ‣ Appendix A Pseudocodes ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities").

*Algorithm 3  Union*

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A\cup B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}\cup\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+\ell)\times d}\leftarrow\textsc{concat\_rows}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{B}})$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A\cup B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}\leftarrow\textsc{ortho\_normal}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}})$ $\triangleright$ $r$ is rank of $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}$

return $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A\cup B}}$

*Algorithm 4  Intersection*

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}$ ($\ell\geq k$)

$\alpha$: Threshold below which the cosine of canonical angles is considered zero.

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A\cap B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}\cap\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times\ell}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\top}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times\ell},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}\leftarrow\textsc{SVD}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}})$

$\triangleright$ $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\ell})$ ($\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{\ell}$) has cosines of the canonical angles between $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$ and $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{B}}$.

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mathrm{U}}[:,1:\sigma_{m}]$

$\triangleright$ $m$ is the maximum index that satisfies $\lvert\sigma_{i}-1\rvert\leq\alpha$.

return $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A\cap B}}$

*Algorithm 5  Complement*

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{A}}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{\overline{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-k)\times d}$: Bases of $\mathbb{S}_{\mathit{\overline{A}}}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\leftarrow\textsc{SVD}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\top})$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{\overline{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-k)\times d}\leftarrow(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{U}}[:,k:d])^{\top}$

return $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{\overline{A}}}$

*Algorithm 6  SubspaceBERTScore*

$\{\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}^{(k)}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}$: Token vectors of first sentence

$\{\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}$: Token vectors of second sentence

$P\in\mathbb{R},R\in\mathbb{R},F\in\mathbb{R}$: Similarity score

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}\leftarrow\textsc{stack\_rows}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}^{(k)})$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}\leftarrow\textsc{stack\_rows}(\boldsymbol{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)})$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{\textrm{orth}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\leftarrow(\textsc{ortho\_normal}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}^{\top}))^{\top}$

$\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}_{\textrm{orth}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\leftarrow(\textsc{ortho\_normal}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}^{\top}))^{\top}$ $\triangleright$ Orthonormalize the bases. $n$ and $m$ are the ranks.

$\displaystyle R\leftarrow\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(a_{i})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{subspace}}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}_{\textrm{orth}})\penalty\ \Big/\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(a_{i})$

$\displaystyle P\leftarrow\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}f(b_{i})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{subspace}}(\boldsymbol{b}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{\textrm{orth}})\penalty\ \Big/\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}f(b_{i})$ $\triangleright$ $f(\cdot)$ is a weighting function.

$F\leftarrow 2(P\cdot R)\penalty\ /(P+R)$

return $P,R,F$

## Appendix C Vector vs. Set of Vectors

[Table 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#A3.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix C Vector vs. Set of Vectors ‣ Subspace Representations for Soft Set Operations and Sentence Similarities") presents the comparison results on the STS-B dataset. We utilized embeddings from BERT and SimCSE [Gao et al. (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.13034v4#bib.bib15).
Avg-cos and CLS-cos are based on the cosine similarity of vectors, employing methods that compress information into a single vector. In contrast, BERTScore among other methods is based on sets of vectors.
A key takeaway from these results is the suggestion that methods using sets of vectors outperform those using a single vector in evaluating similarity.
It is particularly interesting that even SimCSE, which aims to optimize average cosine similarity, shows superior score when using set-based similarity.
