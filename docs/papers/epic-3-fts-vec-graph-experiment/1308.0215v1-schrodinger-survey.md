## 1. Introduction

This article is aimed at presenting the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport. We hope that it can be used as a basic user’s guide to Schrödinger problem. We also give a survey of the related literature. In addition, some new results are proved.

We denote by $\mathrm{P}(Y)$ and $\mathrm{M}_{+}(Y)$ the sets of all probability and positive measures on a space $Y.$

In 1931, Schrödinger [[Sch31](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx64), [Sch32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx65)] addressed a problem which is translated in modern terms11 1 Recall that in the early 30’s the axioms of probability theory were not settled and that the notion of random process still relied entirely on physical intuition. as follows.
Let $\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}$ or more generally a complete connected Riemannian manifold without boundary, $\Omega=C({[0,1]},\mathcal{X})$ be the space of all continuous $\mathcal{X}$-valued paths on the unit time interval ${[0,1]}$ and denote $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ the law of the reversible Brownian motion on $\mathcal{X},$ i.e. the Brownian motion with the volume measure as its initial distribution. Remark that $R$ is an unbounded measure on $\Omega$ whenever the manifold $\mathcal{X}$ is not compact. Define the relative entropy of any probability measure $P$ with respect to $R$ by

|  | $H(P&#124;R)=\int_{\Omega}\log\left(\frac{dP}{dR}\right)\,dP\in(-\infty,\infty],\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

if $P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $R$ and the above integral is meaningful, and $H(P&#124;R)=\infty$ otherwise. A precise definition of the relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure $R$ is presented at the Appendix. The *dynamic Schrödinger problem* is

| (S${}_{\textrm{dyn}}$) |  | $H(P&#124;R)\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1},$ |  |
| --------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ are prescribed values of the initial and final time marginals $P_{0}:=P(X_{0}\in\cdot)$ and $P_{1}:=P(X_{1}\in\cdot)$ of $P.$ Here $(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ is the canonical process on $\Omega.$

This is a convex minimization problem since $H(\cdot&#124;R)$ is a convex function and the constraint set $\left\{P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}\right\}=\left\{P\in\mathrm{M}(\Omega):P\geq 0,P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}\right\}$ is a convex subset of the vector space $\mathrm{M}(\Omega)$ of all bounded measures on $\Omega.$ Furthermore, as $H(\cdot&#124;R)$ is *strictly* convex, if ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) admits a solution, it must be unique. Let $\widehat{P}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ be this solution (if it exists). We shall see at Proposition [2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Thmtheorem3 "Proposition 2.3 (Föllmer, []). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport") that it disintegrates as

| (1.1) |  | $\widehat{P}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}R^{xy}(\cdot)\,\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where for all $x,y\in\mathcal{X},$ $R^{xy}:=R(\cdot\mid X_{0}=x,X_{1}=y)$ is the Brownian bridge from $x$ to $y$ and $\widehat{\pi}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})$ is the unique solution to the following *static Schrödinger problem*

| (S) |  | $H(\pi&#124;R_{01})\to\textrm{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\pi_{1}=\mu_{1}.$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Here,

| (1.2) |  | $R_{01}(dxdy):=R((X_{0},X_{1})\in dxdy)\propto\exp(-d(x,y)^{2}/2)\,\textrm{vol}(dx)\textrm{vol}(dy)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

is the joint law of the initial and final positions of the reversible Brownian motion $R$, $d$ is the Riemannian distance and $\pi_{0}:=\pi(\cdot\times\mathcal{X})$ and $\pi_{1}:=\pi(\mathcal{X}\times\cdot)$ are the first and second marginals of $\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}).$

The disintegration formula ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) means that $\widehat{P}$ shares its bridges with $R,$ that is: $\widehat{P}^{xy}=R^{xy}$ for almost all $x,y,$ and that this mixture of bridges is governed by the unique solution $\widehat{\pi}$ to the static Schrödinger problem ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). It also follows from ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) that the values of the dynamic and static problems are equal: $\inf\eqref{sdyn}=\inf\eqref{s}.$

The structure of problem ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is similar to Monge-Kantorovich problem’s one:

| (MK) |  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}c(x,y)\,\pi(dxdy)\to\textrm{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\pi_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| ---- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $c:\mathcal{X}^{2}\to[0,\infty)$ represents the cost for transporting a unit mass from the initial location $x$ to the final location $y$. Both are convex optimization problems, but unlike ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), the linear program ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) might admit infinitely many solutions. Since ([1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) writes as $R_{01}(dxdy)\propto\exp\big(-c(x,y)\big)\,\textrm{vol}(dx)\textrm{vol}(dy)$ with

|  | $c(x,y)=d^{2}(x,y)/2,\quad x,y\in\mathcal{X},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

it happens that the Schrödinger problem ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is connected to the quadratic Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) which is specified by this quadratic cost function. The natural dynamic version of ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is

| (MKdyn) |  | $\int_{\Omega}C\,dP\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| ------- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with

| (1.3) |  | $C(\omega)=\int_{[0,1]}&#124;\dot{\omega}_{t}&#124;_{\omega_{t}}^{2}/2\,dt\in[0,\infty],\quad\omega\in\Omega,$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where we put $C(\omega)=\infty$ when $\omega$ is not absolutely continuous.

Let us comment on the choice of this dynamic version of ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). For all $x,y\in\mathcal{X},$ we have

| (1.4) |  | $c(x,y)=\inf\{C(\omega);\omega\in\Omega:\omega_{0}=x,\omega_{1}=y\}$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and this infimum is attained at the constant speed geodesic path $\gamma^{xy}$ between $x$ and $y,$ which is assumed to be unique for any $(x,y),$ for simplicity. Therefore, the solutions of ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) are in one-one correspondence:

- —

  If $\widehat{P}$ solves ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), then $\widehat{P}_{01}:=\widehat{P}((X_{0},X_{1})\in\cdot)$ solves ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport"));

- —

  If $\widehat{\pi}$ solves ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), then the solution of ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is

    | (1.5) |  | $\widehat{P}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\delta_{\gamma^{xy}}(\cdot)\,\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)$ |  |
    | ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

  where $\delta_{a}$ denotes the Dirac measure at $a$.

Furthermore, we have equality of the values of the problems: $\inf\eqref{mk}=\inf\eqref{mkdyn}\in[0,\infty].$

Again, the dynamic Schrödinger problem ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and the dynamic Monge-Kantorovich problem ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) are similar. Comparing their respective solutions ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E5 "In 2nd item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), we see that the $\widehat{\pi}$’s solve their respective static problem ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), while for each $(x,y)$ the bridge $R^{xy}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ in ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) plays the role of $\delta_{\gamma^{xy}}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ in ([1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E5 "In 2nd item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")).

All the notions pertaining to the Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problems ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) which are going to be invoked below are discussed in great details in C. Villani’s textbook [[Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx71)].

### Displacement interpolations in $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})$

Let $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ denote the set of all probability measures on $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X}):=\left\{p\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}):\int_{\mathcal{X}}d^{2}(x_{0},y)\,p(dy)<\infty\right\}.$ If $\mu_{0},\mu_{1}$ are in $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})$, then ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) admit a solution.
Let $\widehat{P}$ be a solution of ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). We consider

| (1.6) |  | $\mu_{t}:=\widehat{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot)\in\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X}),\quad t\in{[0,1]}$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

the time-marginal flow of $\widehat{P}$. The $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})$-valued path $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ is called a *displacement interpolation* between $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}.$ These interpolations encode *geometric* properties of the manifold $\mathcal{X}:$ although $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})$ is not endowed with a Riemannian metric, Otto [[JKO98](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx30), [Ott01](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx59)] discovered that $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ is a minimizing constant speed geodesic path on $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X}),$ in the length space sense. In particular, ([1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E5 "In 2nd item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) shows that for each $x,y,$ $[\delta_{x},\delta_{y}]=(\delta_{\gamma^{xy}_{t}})_{0\leq t\leq 1}.$ Therefore, displacement interpolations lift the notion of minimizing constant speed geodesic paths from the state space $\mathcal{X}$ up to $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})$.

The common value of the Monge-Kantorovich problems

|  | $W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})/2:=\inf\eqref{mk}=\inf\eqref{mkdyn}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

allows to define the Wasserstein distance $W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ between $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}.$ Saying that $\gamma^{xy}$ has a constant speed means that for all $0\leq s\leq t\leq 1,$ $d(\gamma^{xy}_{s},\gamma^{xy}_{t})=(t-s)d(x,y).$ With ([1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E5 "In 2nd item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), we see that $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ inherits this constant speed property: for all $0\leq s\leq t\leq 1,$ $W_{2}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})=(t-s)W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}).$ It is a remarkable fact that $W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ also admits the following Benamou-Brenier representation [[BB00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx1)]:

| (1.7) |  | $W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\inf_{(\nu,v)}\left\{\int_{[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}}&#124;v_{t}(x)&#124;^{2}_{x}\,\nu_{t}(dx)dt\right\}=\int_{[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}}&#124;\nabla\psi_{t}(x)&#124;^{2}_{x}\,\mu_{t}(dx)dt$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where the infimum is taken over all $(\nu,v)$ such that $\nu=(\nu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}\in C({[0,1]},\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})),$ $v$ is a smooth vector field and these quantities are linked by the following current equation (in a weak sense) with boundary values:

|  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\nu+\nabla\!\cdot\!(\nu\,v)=0,\quad t\in(0,1)\\ \nu_{0}=\mu_{0},\ \nu_{1}=\mu_{1}.\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

The last equality in ([1.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E7 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) states that the infimum is attained at $\nu=\mu$: the displacement interpolation ([1.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E6 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), and some gradient vector field $v=\nabla\psi$ which might not be smooth. The optimal couple $(\mu,\nabla\psi)$ solves the forward-backward coupled system

| (1.8) |  | $(a)\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\mu+\nabla\!\cdot\!(\mu\,\nabla\psi)=0,&t\in(0,1]\\ \mu_{0},&t=0\end{array}\right.\qquad(b)\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\psi+\frac{1}{2}&#124;\nabla\psi&#124;^{2}=0,&t\in[0,1)\\ \psi_{1},&t=1\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

for some measurable function $\psi_{1}$ which is designed for obtaining $\mu_{1}$ at time 1.
The potential $\psi$ is the unique viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation ([1.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E8 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport"))-(b); it admits the Hopf-Lax representation

| (1.9) |  | $\psi_{t}(z)=\inf_{y}\left\{\frac{d^{2}(z,y)}{2(1-t)}+\psi_{1}(y)\right\},\quad 0\leq t<1,y\in\mathcal{X}.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

In particular, if $\psi_{1}$ is bounded, $\psi$ is locally Lipschitz continuous and almost everywhere differentiable.

Based on these properties of the displacement interpolations, F. Otto [[JKO98](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx30), [Ott01](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx59)] developed an informal theory aimed at considering the metric space $(\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X}),W_{2})$ as a Riemannian manifold. This informal approach relies on the idea that, in view of the current equation ([1.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E8 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport"))-(a), $\partial_{t}\mu_{&#124;t=0}=-\nabla\!\cdot\!(\mu\,\nabla\psi_{0})$ is a candidate to be a tangent vector at $\mu_{0}$. Second order calculus necessitates to take also ([1.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E8 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport"))-(b) into account.

The analogue of displacement interpolation exists with ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) replaced by ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). This *entropic interpolation* also enjoys properties which are similar to ([1.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E7 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([1.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E8 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). They are discussed below.

### The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems

It is well-known that taking $R^{k}$ to be the reversible Brownian motion with variance $1/k,$ i.e. the Markov measure associated to the Markov generator

|  | $L^{k}=\Delta/(2k)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with the volume measure as its initial distribution, the bridges of $R^{k}$ converge: for each $(x,y),$ we have

| (1.10) |  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}R^{k,xy}=\delta_{\gamma^{xy}}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ |  |
| ------ | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with respect to the usual narrow topology $\sigma(\mathrm{P}(\Omega),C_{b}(\Omega))$. This result is an easy consequence of Schilder’s theorem which is a large deviation result (as $k$ tends to infinity) whose rate function is precisely the dynamic cost function $C$ given at ([1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), see [[DZ98](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx21)].

In fact, the dynamic and static Monge-Kantorovich problems are respectively the $\Gamma$-limits of sequences of dynamic and static Schrödinger problems associated to the sequence $(R^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ in $\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ of reference path measures [[Mik04](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx48), [Léo12a](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx40)]. More precisely (but still informally), considering the sequence of re-normalized Schrödinger problems

| (S${}^{k}_{\mathrm{dyn}}$) |  | $H(P&#124;R^{k})/k\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1},$ |  |
| --------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

we have

| (1.11) |  | $\Gamma\textrm{-}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\eqref{skdyn}=\eqref{mkdyn}$ |  |
| ------ | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and similarly the re-normalized static version

| (Sk) |  | $H(\pi&#124;R^{k}_{01})/k\to\textrm{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\pi_{1}=\mu_{1},$ |  |
| ---- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

satisfies $\Gamma\textrm{-}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\eqref{sk}=\eqref{mk}$. Recall that this implies that under some compactness requirements, the values converge: $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\inf\eqref{skdyn}=\inf\eqref{mkdyn}$ and any limit point of the sequence of minimizers $(\widehat{P}^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ of ([S k dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex10 "In The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) solves ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). A similar statement holds with the static problems.

In particular, the time-marginal flow

|  | $\mu^{k}_{t}:=\widehat{P}^{k}_{t},\quad t\in{[0,1]},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

of the solution to ([S k dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex10 "In The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) converges as $k$ tends to infinity to the displacement interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ when ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) admits a unique solution, for instance when both $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$ are absolutely continuous.
Denoting and calling the *entropic interpolation* $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]^{k}:=(\mu^{k}_{t})_{t\in{[0,1]}}$ of order $k$, we have

| (1.12) |  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]^{k}=[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}],$ |  |
| ------ | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with respect to the topology of uniform convergence on $C({[0,1]},\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X}))$ where $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})$ is equipped with $W_{2}$.

### The Schrödinger problem is a regular approximation of the Monge-Kantorovich problem

Now, we explain informally why in some sense, ([S k dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex10 "In The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is a regularization of its limiting Monge-Kantorovich problem ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), for each $k\geq 1$, ([S k dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex10 "In The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems ‣ 1. ## 2. Schrödinger’s problem

We begin fixing some notation and describing the general framework. Then, Schrödinger’s problem is stated and its main properties are discussed in a general setting.

### Path measures

Depending on the context, we denote by the same letter the set $\Omega=C({[0,1]},\mathcal{X})$ of all continuous paths from the unit time interval ${[0,1]}$ to the topological state space $\mathcal{X},$ or $\Omega=D({[0,1]},\mathcal{X})$ the set of all càdlàg (right-continuous and left-limited) paths. We furnish $\mathcal{X}$ with its Borel $\sigma$-field and $\Omega$ with the canonical $\sigma$-field $\sigma(X_{t};0\leq t\leq 1)$ which is generated by the time projections

|  | $X_{t}(\omega):=\omega_{t}\in\mathcal{X},\qquad\omega=(\omega_{s})_{0\leq s\leq 1}\in\Omega,\ t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

The mapping $X=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}:\Omega\to\Omega$ which is the identity on $\Omega,$ is usually called the canonical process. We call a *path measure*, any positive measure $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ on $\Omega.$ Its time-marginals are the push-forward measures

|  | $Q_{t}:=(X_{t})_{\#}Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}),\quad t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

This means that for any Borel subset $A\subset\mathcal{X},$ $Q_{t}(A)=Q(X_{t}\in A).$ If $Q$ describes the behaviour of the random path $(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ of some particle, then $Q_{t}$ describes the behaviour of the random position $X_{t}$ of the particle at time $t.$ Remark that the flow $(Q_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})^{{[0,1]}}$ contains less information than the path measure $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega).$ In particular, $(Q_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ doesn’t tell us anything about the correlations between two positions at different times $s$ and $t$ which are encoded in $Q_{st}:=(X_{s},X_{t})_{\#}Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}^{2}).$ We shall be primarily concerned with the endpoint marginal measure

|  | $Q_{01}:=(X_{0},X_{1})_{\#}Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}^{2}),$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

meaning that for any Borel subsets $B\subset\mathcal{X}^{2},$ $Q_{01}(B)=Q((X_{0},X_{1})\in B).$ We also denote

|  | $Q^{xy}=Q(\cdot\mid X_{0}=x,X_{1}=y)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega),$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

the bridge of $Q$ between $x$ and $y$. For each $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega),$ the disintegration formula ([A.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E7 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) with $\phi=(X_{0},X_{1})$ writes as follows:

|  | $Q(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}Q^{xy}(\cdot)\,Q_{01}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

We assume that the topological state space $\mathcal{X}$ is a Polish (separable and complete metric) space and equip $\Omega=D({[0,1]},\mathcal{X})$ with the corresponding Skorokhod topology. It is well-known [[Bil68](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx4)] that this topology turns $\Omega$ into a Polish space and that the corresponding Borel $\sigma$-field is precisely the canonical one: $\sigma(X_{t};0\leq t\leq 1).$ Moreover, in restriction to $C({[0,1]},\mathcal{X}),$ the Skorokod topology is the topology of uniform convergence which also turns $C({[0,1]},\mathcal{X})$ into a Polish space. We still have the coincidence of the Borel $\sigma$-field and the canonical one.

The path space $\Omega$ is furnished with this topology.

### Why unbounded path measures

One may wonder why a random behaviour should be described by an unbounded measure rather than a probability measure. We have in mind as a particular but important application, the *reversible Brownian motion* on $\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}.$ It is the Brownian motion whose forward dynamics is driven by the heat semigroup as usual, but its random initial position $X_{0}$ is uniformly distributed on $\mathbb{R}^{n}.$ Denoting $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ the corresponding path measure on $\Omega=C({[0,1]},\mathbb{R}^{n})$, $R_{0}(dx)=dx$ is the Lebesgue measure33 3 Although this paper is not concerned with the interpretation of such a description, one should note that a “frequencist” interpretation fails unless one introduces an infinite system of independent particles initially distributed according to a Poisson point process with a uniform spatial frequency. An alternate information viewpoint is also relevant: the Lebesgue measure (or any of its positive multiples) is the less informative a priori measure for modelling our complete lack of knowledge about the initial position. Indeed, it is invariant under isometries and translations, and the entropic problems to be considered below are insensitive to homotheties (up to an additive constant). on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $R(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{W}_{x}(\cdot)\,dx$ where $\mathcal{W}_{x}$ is the Wiener probability measure with initial marginal $\delta_{x}$. Clearly, $R$ has the same infinite mass as $R_{0}.$
Similarly, the simple random walk on a countably infinite graph $\mathcal{X}$ admits an unbounded reversing measure $R_{0}$ so that the corresponding reversible simple random walk is described by an unbounded measure $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ with $\Omega=D({[0,1]},\mathcal{X}).$
Considering such reversible path measures $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ as reference measures usually simplifies computations.

### Relative entropy

| (2.1) |  | $H(p&#124;r):=\int_{Y}\log(dp/dr)\,dp\in(-\infty,\infty],\qquad p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

if $p\ll r$ and $H(p&#124;r)=\infty$ otherwise.
The rigorous definition of the relative entropy and its basic properties are recalled at the appendix section [A](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1 "Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport").

| (Sdyn) |  | $H(P&#124;R)\to\emph{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},\ P_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |

###### Definition 2.2 (Schrödinger’s problem).

| (S) |  | $H(\pi&#124;R_{01})\to\emph{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\ \pi_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |

### Some properties of the minimizer $\widehat{\pi}$ of ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex7 "In Definition 2.2 (Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. We give some details about the structure of the unique minimizer $\widehat{\pi}$ of ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex7 "In Definition 2.2 (Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. 2. (ii)

   $\varphi\oplus\psi:\mathcal{X}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}$ is $R_{01}$-measurable.

It is tempting to write that $\widehat{\pi}$ has the following shape

| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

are satisfied. This was already suggested by ([2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.E7 "In The dual problem ‣ 2. Indeed, two obstacles have to be avoided.
Some comments are necessary.

#### Obstacle (i)

Firstly, the identity (i) is only valid $\widehat{\pi}$-almost everywhere and it might happen that it doesn’t hold true $R_{01}$-almost everywhere. Otherwise stated, there exists some measurable subset $S\subset\mathcal{X}^{2}$ such that:

1. (i)’

   $\widehat{\pi}=\mathbf{1}_{S}\exp(\varphi\oplus\psi)\,R_{01}$

and it is not true in general that the set $S\subset\mathcal{X}^{2}$ is the product $S=S_{0}\times S_{1}$ of two measurable subsets of $\mathcal{X},$ see [[FG97](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx23), §2] or [[Léo01b](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx38), §5].

#### Obstacle (ii)

Secondly, statement (ii) does not imply that $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are respectively $R_{0}$ and $R_{1}$-measurable on $\mathcal{X}$. Only the tensor sum $\varphi\oplus\psi$ is $R_{01}$-measurable on the product space $\mathcal{X}^{2}.$ Hence, one is not allowed to consider the conditional expectations in ([2.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.E8 "In Some properties of the minimizer 𝜋̂ of () ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")).

#### Avoiding obstacle (i)

To avoid the obstacle (i), it is enough to slightly modify the prescribed marginals $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$ as follows. It is shown in [[Léo01b](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx38)] that (i) is satisfied $R_{01}\textrm{-a.e.}$ (rather than $\widehat{\pi}\textrm{-a.e.}$) if $(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ is in the intrinsic core: $\icor\mathcal{C},$ of the set of all admissible constraints

|  | $\mathcal{C}:=\{(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})^{2};\inf\eqref{S}_{(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}<\infty\}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Recall that for any convex set $C$, its intrinsic core is defined as
$\icor C:=\left\{y\in C;\exists x,z\in C:y\in]x,z[\right\}$ where $]x,z[:=\left\{(1-t)x+tz;0<t<1\right\}\subset C.$ It is also shown in [[Léo01b](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx38)] that $\mathcal{C}=\{\Lambda^{*}<\infty\}$ where $\Lambda^{*}$ is the convex conjugate of the extended real valued function $\Lambda$ which is defined for any measurable functions $\varphi,\psi$ by $\Lambda(\varphi,\psi)=\log\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}e^{\varphi\oplus\psi}\,dR_{01}\in(-\infty,\infty].$ Therefore $\mathcal{C}$ is a convex subset of $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})^{2}.$ In particular, considering

| (2.9) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mu_{0}^{\epsilon}&:=&(1-\epsilon)\mu_{0}+\epsilon R_{0}^{w}\\ \mu_{1}^{\epsilon}&:=&(1-\epsilon)\mu_{1}+\epsilon R_{1}^{w}\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with $R_{0}^{w},R_{1}^{w}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ the marginals of $R^{w}_{01}=z_{w}^{-1}e^{-w}\,R_{01}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ where the function $w$ is chosen55 5 In the important special case where $R$ is a probability measure, just take $w=0$ in order that $R^{w}_{01}=R_{01}.$ such that $\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}we^{-w}\,dR_{01}<\infty$ for $H(R^{w}_{01}&#124;R_{01})<\infty$ to be satisfied with $\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}w\,dR^{w}_{01}<\infty,$ see ([A.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E4 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), we observe that for any admissible $(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\in\mathcal{C},$ for any arbitrarily small $\epsilon>0$, $(\mu_{0}^{\epsilon},\mu_{1}^{\epsilon})\in\icor\mathcal{C}$. Therefore, $(\mu_{0}^{\epsilon},\mu_{1}^{\epsilon})$ is arbitrarily close to $(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ in total variation norm and the corresponding solution $\widehat{\pi}^{\epsilon}$ of $\eqref{S}_{(\mu_{0}^{\epsilon},\mu_{1}^{\epsilon})}$ satisfies

|  | $\widehat{\pi}^{\epsilon}=\exp(\varphi^{\epsilon}\oplus\psi^{\epsilon})\,R_{01}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

for some functions $\varphi^{\epsilon}$ and $\psi^{\epsilon}$ such that $\varphi^{\epsilon}\oplus\psi^{\epsilon}$ is jointly $R_{01}$-measurable.

###### Proposition 2.6.

We say that the constraint $(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ is *internal* if it is in the intrinsic core of the set of all admissible constraints: $(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\in\icor\mathcal{C}$. In this case, we have

|  | $\widehat{\pi}=\exp(\varphi\oplus\psi)\,R_{01}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

for some jointly $R_{01}$-measurable function $\varphi\oplus\psi$ on $\mathcal{X}^{2}.$

#### Overcoming obstacle (ii)

To overcome the measurability obstacle (ii), it is necessary to impose some restriction on the reference measure $R_{01}.$ It is proved in [[BL92](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx5), Proposition 6.1] that when the function $\varphi\oplus\psi$ is measurable with respect to some product measure $\alpha\otimes\beta$ on the product space $\mathcal{X}^{2},$ the functions $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are respectively $\alpha$-measurable and $\beta$-measurable. Therefore, if it is assumed that

|  | $R_{0}\otimes R_{1}\ll R_{01},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

any $R_{01}$-measurable function is $R_{0}\otimes R_{1}$-measurable. As $\varphi\oplus\psi$ is $R_{01}$-measurable, $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are respectively $R_{0}$ and $R_{1}$-measurable.

Choosing another way,
it is also possible to obtain the desired measurability property when assuming

|  | $R_{01}\ll R_{0}\otimes R_{1}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

To see this, let $\widehat{\pi},$ $\varphi$ and $\psi$ satisfy (i).
It is proved in [[Csi75](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx8)] that $\varphi\oplus\psi$ stands in the $L^{1}(\widehat{\pi})$-closure of $L^{1}(\mu_{0})\oplus L^{1}(\mu_{1}),$ denoted by $\Lambda_{\widehat{\pi}}$. In addition, with [[RT93](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx61), Proposition 2], we know that when $\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ with first and second marginals $\pi_{0}=\mu_{0}$ and $\pi_{1}=\mu_{1},$ is such that$\pi\ll\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1}=\mu_{0}\otimes\mu_{1}$, then for any $\theta\in\Lambda_{\pi},$ there exist two *measurable* functions $\theta_{0}$ and $\theta_{1}$ such that $\theta=\theta_{0}\oplus\theta_{1},$ $\pi\textrm{-a.s.}$ Therefore, if

|  | $\widehat{\pi}\ll\mu_{0}\otimes\mu_{1},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

then $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are respectively $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$-measurable functions.

But this implicit criterion is not easy to check. The following result is more practical.

###### Proposition 2.7.

[[RT93](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx61), Thm. 3] If $R_{01}\ll R_{0}\otimes R_{1},$ then $\varphi$ and $\psi$ in (i): $d\widehat{\pi}/dR_{01}=\exp(\varphi\oplus\psi),\ \widehat{\pi}\textrm{-a.e.}$, can be chosen respectively as $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$-measurable functions.

###### Proof.

By assumption $\widehat{\pi}\ll R_{01}\ll R_{0}\otimes R_{1}$. But $\widehat{\pi}\ll R_{0}\otimes R_{1}$ implies that $\widehat{\pi}\ll\widehat{\pi}_{0}\otimes\widehat{\pi}_{1}=\mu_{0}\otimes\mu_{1}$ and we conclude as above.
∎

Extending the functions $\varphi$ and $\psi$ to their $R_{0}$ and $R_{1}$-measurable versions: $\mathbf{1}_{\{d\mu_{0}/dR_{0}>0\}}\varphi$ and $\mathbf{1}_{\left\{d\mu_{1}/dR_{1}>0\right\}}\psi$, we see that $\varphi$ and $\psi$ can be taken respectively $R_{0}$ and $R_{1}$-measurable.

### The special case where $R$ is Markov

We are going to assume that the reference path measure $R$ is Markov. Under this restriction, we obtain at Theorem [2.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Thmtheorem12 "Theorem 2.12 (The Markov case). ‣ Reversibility ‣ The special case where 𝑅 is Markov ‣ 2. #### Markov property

One says that $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is *Markov* if its time marginal $R_{0}$ (or any other time marginal $R_{t_{o}}$ with $0\leq t_{o}\leq 1$) is $\sigma$-finite77 7 This assumption is necessary for defining conditional versions of $R$, such as $R(\cdot\mid X_{t})$ or $R(\cdot\mid X_{[0,t]})$, see [[Léoc](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx35)]. and for each $0\leq t\leq 1,$

|  | $R(X_{[0,t]}\in\cdot,X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\cdot\mid X_{t})=R(X_{[0,t]}\in\cdot\mid X_{t})R(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\cdot\mid X_{t})$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

signifying that under $R$, for any $t$, conditionally on the present state $X_{t}$ at time $t$, past and future are independent. This is equivalent to the usual forward time-oriented Markov property

|  | $R(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})=R(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot&#124;X_{t}),\quad\forall t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

###### Proposition 2.10.

Suppose that the reference measure $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is Markov.
If it exists, the solution $\widehat{P}$ of ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex6 "In Definition 2.1 (Dynamic Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is also Markov.

###### Proof.

We need some notation. For each $0\leq t\leq 1,$ we set $\Omega_{[0,t]}:=\left\{\omega_{&#124;[0,t]};\omega\in\Omega\right\}$ and $\Omega_{[t,1]}:=\left\{\omega_{&#124;[t,1]};\omega\in\Omega\right\}$ the set of all paths on $[0,t]$ and $[t,1]$ respectively. For any $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega),$ $Q^{t,z}:=Q(\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega),$ $Q^{t,z}_{[0,t]}:=Q(X_{[0,t]}\in\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{[0,t]})$ and$Q^{t,z}_{[t,1]}:=Q(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{[t,1]}).$

###### Claim 2.11.

We fix $0\leq t\leq 1$. Among all the $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ such that $P_{t}=\mu,$ $P^{tz}_{[0,t]}=Q_{<}^{tz}$ and $P^{tz}_{[t,1]}=Q_{>}^{tz}$, $z\in\mathcal{X},$ where $\mu\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),$ $Q_{<}^{tz}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{[0,t]}\cap\left\{X_{t}=z\right\})$ and $Q_{>}^{tz}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{[t,1]}\cap\left\{X_{t}=z\right\})$ are prescribed, the relative entropy $H(\cdot&#124;R)$ attains its unique minimum at $P^{*}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q_{<}^{tz}\otimes Q_{>}^{tz}(\cdot)\,\mu(dz).$ In particular, $P^{*}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})=P^{*}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}).$

Accept this claim for a while and suppose, ad absurdum, that $\widehat{P}$ is not Markov. Then, there exists some $0\leq t\leq 1$ such that $\widehat{P}(\cdot&#124;X_{t})\not=\widehat{P}_{[0,t]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t})\otimes\widehat{P}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}).$ Choosing $\mu=\widehat{P}_{t},$ $Q_{<}^{tz}=\widehat{P}^{tz}_{[0,t]}$ and $Q_{>}^{tz}=\widehat{P}^{tz}_{[t,1]}$ in the above claim, we see that the time marginals are unchanged: $P^{*}_{s}=\widehat{P}_{s},$ for all $s\in{[0,1]}$ and that $H(P^{*}&#124;R)<H(\widehat{P}&#124;R):$ $\widehat{P}$ is not the solution to ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex6 "In Definition 2.1 (Dynamic Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), a contradiction.

It remains to prove the claim. With ([A.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E8 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), we see that

|  | $H(P&#124;R)=H(\mu&#124;R_{t})+\int_{\mathcal{X}}H(P^{tz}&#124;R^{tz})\,\mu(dz)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and

|  | $H(P^{tz}&#124;R^{tz})=H(Q_{<}^{tz}&#124;R^{tz}_{[0,t]})+\int_{\Omega_{[0,t]}}H(P^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})&#124;R^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]}))\,dQ_{<}^{tz}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Noting that $R^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})=R^{tz}_{[t,1]}$ and $Q_{>}^{tz}=\int_{\Omega_{[0,t]}}P^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})\,dQ_{<}^{tz}$, we obtain with Jensen’s inequality that

|  | $\displaystyle H\big(Q_{>}^{tz}\big&#124;R^{tz}_{[t,1]}\big)$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle H\Big(\int_{\Omega_{[0,t]}}P^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})\,dQ_{<}^{tz}\Big&#124;R^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})\Big)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle\leq$ | $\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{[0,t]}}H(P^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})&#124;R^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]}))\,dQ_{<}^{tz}$ |  |

with equality if and only if $P^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})=Q^{tz}_{>},$ $Q^{tz}_{<}\textrm{-a.e.}$ Since this holds for $\mu$-almost every $z,$ this amounts to say that $P(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q_{<}^{tz}\otimes Q_{>}^{tz}(\cdot)\,P_{t}(dz)$ and it also means that $P^{tz}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{[0,t]})=P^{tz}_{[t,1]},$ $P^{tz}_{[0,t]}\textrm{-a.e.}$ which is the desired forward Markov property at time $t$. #### Reversibility

A path measure $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is said to be *reversible* with $m\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ as its reversing measure ($m$-reversible for short), if $R_{0}=m$ and for any $0\leq u\leq v\leq 1,$ $R$ is invariant with respect to the time reversal mapping $\mathrm{rev}^{uv}$ defined by: $\mathrm{rev}^{uv}_{t}:=X_{(u+v-t)^{+}},u\leq t\leq v$, meaning that $(\mathrm{rev}^{uv})_{\#}R=(X_{[u,v]})_{\#}R.$

Clearly, this implies that $R_{u}=R_{v}$ for any $u,v$. In other words, $R$ is $m$-*stationary* i.e. $R_{t}=m,$ for all $0\leq t\leq 1.$

This notion is invoked at statement (c) of the following result.

## 3. $(f,g)$-transform of a Markov measure

Motivated by Theorems [2.9](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Thmtheorem9 "Theorem 2.9. ‣ The solution 𝑃̂ of () ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport") and [2.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Thmtheorem12 "Theorem 2.12 (The Markov case). ‣ Reversibility ‣ The special case where 𝑅 is Markov ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport"), we introduce the transform $f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1})\,R$ of a Markov measure $R$ and call it an $(f,g)$-transform. It was already noticed by Föllmer [[Föl88](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx25), [FG97](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx23)] and Nagasawa [[Nag89](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx55)], that it is a time symmetric version of Doob’s usual $h$-transform [[Doo57](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx19), [Doo00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx20)].

We are going to assume for simplicity that the reference path measure $R$ is reversible. Let us recall the definition of this notion.

### $(f,g)$-transform of a reversible Markov measure

Let us first state an assumption which will hold for the remainder of the paper.

###### Assumption 3.1.

The reference path measure $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is Markov and $m$-reversible with $m\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$.

The Markov property of the reference measure will turn out to be crucial for the description of the dynamics of the solution $\widehat{P}$ of ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex6 "In Definition 2.1 (Dynamic Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). Indeed, we have already seen at Proposition [2.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Thmtheorem10 "Proposition 2.10. ‣ Markov property ‣ The special case where 𝑅 is Markov ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport") that $\widehat{P}$ inherits the Markov property from $R.$ It follows that its dynamics is characterized by its stochastic derivatives. On the other hand, reversibility is only assumed for simplicity.

###### Definition 3.2 ($(f,g)$-transform).

Let $f_{0}$, $g_{1}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)$ be two nonnegative measurable functions such that $E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1}))=1$. The path measure

| (3.1) |  | $P:=f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1})\,R\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

is called an $(f,g)$-transform of $R.$

###### Remark 3.5.

It follows with ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E4 "In Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. ###### Proof of Theorem [3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. The Markov property of $P$ is a direct consequence of Theorem [3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3.3. ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport") and Proposition [2.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Thmtheorem10 "Proposition 2.10. ‣ Markov property ‣ The special case where 𝑅 is Markov ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport").

We propose an alternate simple proof.
To prove that $P$ is Markov, we show that for each $0\leq t\leq 1$ and any bounded measurable functions $a\in\sigma(X_{[0,t]})$, $b\in\sigma(X_{[t,1]})$, we have

|  | $E_{P}(ab\mid X_{t})=E_{P}(a\mid X_{t})E_{P}(b\mid X_{t}).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Indeed, we have

|  | $\displaystyle E_{P}(ab\mid X_{t})$ | $\displaystyle\overset{\textrm{(i)}}{=}$ | $\displaystyle\frac{E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})abg_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})}\overset{\textrm{(ii)}}{=}\frac{E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})a\mid X_{t})E_{R}(bg_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})\mid X_{t})E_{R}(g_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle\overset{\textrm{(iii)}}{=}$ | $\displaystyle E_{P}(a\mid X_{t})E_{P}(b\mid X_{t}),\quad P\textrm{-a.e.},$ |  |

which is the desired result. Equality (i) is a general result about conditioning; note that we do not divide by zero $P\textrm{-a.s.}$ Equality (ii) uses crucially the assumed Markov property of $R$ and one obtains (iii) by considering separately the cases when $b\equiv 1$ and $a\equiv 1$ in the just obtained identity to see that $E_{P}(a\mid X_{t})=\frac{E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})a\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})\mid X_{t})}$ and $E_{P}(b\mid X_{t})=\frac{E_{R}(bg_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(g_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})}$.

Finally, to prove ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E4 "In Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), remark that

|  | $\displaystyle\frac{dP_{t}}{dm}(X_{t})$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{dP_{t}}{dR_{t}}(X_{t})=E_{R}\left(\frac{dP}{dR}\mid X_{t}\right)=E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle\overset{\checkmark}{=}$ | $\displaystyle E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})\mid X_{t})E_{R}(g_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t})=:f_{t}(X_{t})g_{t}(X_{t})$ |  |

where we used the Markov property of $R$ at the marked equality.
∎

### Forward and backward generators

Let $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ be a Markov measure. Its *forward stochastic derivative* $\partial+\overrightarrow{L}^{Q}$ is defined by

|  | $[\partial_{t}+\overrightarrow{L}^{Q}_{t}](u)(t,x):=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}h^{-1}E_{Q}\big(u(t+h,X_{t+h})-u(t,X_{t})\mid X_{t}=x\big)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

for any measurable function $u:{[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}$ in the set $\dom\overrightarrow{L}^{Q}$ for which this limit exists $Q_{t}\textrm{-a.e.}$ for all $0\leq t<1$. In fact this definition is only approximate, we give it here as a support for understanding the relations between the forward and backward generators. For a precise statement see [[Léod](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx36), §2]. Since the time reversed $Q^{*}$ of $Q$ is still Markov, $Q$ admits a *backward stochastic derivative* $-\partial+\overleftarrow{L}^{Q}$ which is defined by

|  | $[-\partial_{t}+\overleftarrow{L}^{Q}_{t}]u(t,x):=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}h^{-1}E_{Q}\big(u(t-h,X_{t-h})-u(t,X_{t})\mid X_{t}=x\big)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

for any measurable function $u:{[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}$ in the set $\dom\overleftarrow{L}^{Q}$ for which this limit exists $Q_{t}\textrm{-a.e.}$ for all $0<t\leq 1$. Remark that$\overleftarrow{L}^{Q}_{t}=\overrightarrow{L}^{Q^{*}}_{1-t},$ $0\leq t\leq 1.$

It is proved in [[Léod](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx36), §2] that these stochastic derivatives are extensions of the extended forward and backward generators of $Q$ in the sense of semimartingales. In particular, they offer us a natural way for computing generators. Later on, we shall call $\overrightarrow{L}^{Q}$ and $\overleftarrow{L}^{Q}$ generators, rather than stochastic derivatives.

For simplicity, we denote $\overrightarrow{L}^{R}=\overleftarrow{L}^{R}=L$ without the superscript $R$ and without the time arrows, since $R$ is assumed to be reversible. We also write $\overrightarrow{A}=\overrightarrow{L}^{P}$ and $\overleftarrow{A}=\overleftarrow{L}^{P}$ the generators of the $(f_{0},g_{1})$-transform $P$ defined by ([3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E1 "In Definition 3.2 ((𝑓,𝑔)-transform). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")).

Stochastic derivatives have been introduced by E. Nelson in [[Nel67](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx57)] while studying the dynamical properties of the Brownian motion. The above definition (more precisely the one of [[Léod](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx36)]), which is an extension of Nelson’s one, is necessary for technical reasons.

### The dynamics of the $(f,g)$-transform

To give the expressions of the generators $\overrightarrow{A}$ and $\overleftarrow{A},$ we need to introduce the carré du champ of $R$. It is defined for any functions $u,v$ on $\mathcal{X}$ such that $u,v$ and $uv$ are in $\dom L$, by

|  | $\Gamma(u,v):=L(uv)-uLv-vLu.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

In general, the forward and backward generators $(\partial_{t}+\overrightarrow{A}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ and $(-\partial_{t}+\overleftarrow{A}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ of $P$ depend explicitly on $t$. The following informal statement is known for long in specific situations. In the important examples which are discussed below at Section [4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S4 "4. Standard examples ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport") below, these claims are easy consequences of Itô’s formula (for instance see [[RY99](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx63), Ch. 8,§ 3] in the continuous diffusion case).

###### Informal statement 3.6.

Under some hypotheses on $R$, the forward and backward generators of $P$ are given for any function $u:{[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}$ belonging to some class $\mathcal{U}_{R}$ of regular functions, by

| (3.5) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcll}\overrightarrow{A}_{t}u(x)&=&\displaystyle{Lu(x)+\frac{\Gamma(g_{t},u)(x)}{g_{t}(x)}},&(t,x)\in[0,1)\times\mathcal{X}\\ \overleftarrow{A}_{t}u(x)&=&\displaystyle{Lu(x)+\frac{\Gamma(f_{t},u)(x)}{f_{t}(x)}},&(t,x)\in(0,1]\times\mathcal{X}\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $f_{t},g_{t}$ are defined at ([3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E2 "In (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")).

Because of ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E4 "In Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), for any $t$ no division by zero occurs $P_{t}\textrm{-a.e.}$

Rigorous statement and proof are given in [[Léod](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx36)] for instance.

###### Idea of proof of Statement [3.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.Thmtheorem6 "Informal statement 3.6. ‣ The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport").

To obtain the forward generator of $P$, we are going to compute the stochastic derivative $\overrightarrow{A}_{t}u(x):=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}h^{-1}E_{P}[u(X_{t+h})-u(X_{t})\mid X_{t}=x].$ Let us denote for simplicity $F_{0}=f_{0}(X_{0}),$ $G_{t}=g_{t}(X_{t})$ and $U_{t}=u(X_{t})$. We have

|  | $\displaystyle E_{P}[U_{t+h}-U_{t}\mid X_{t}=x]$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{E_{R}[F_{0}(U_{t+h}-U_{t})G_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]}{E_{R}[F_{0}G_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{E_{R}[F_{0}\mid X_{t}=x]E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})G_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]}{E_{R}[F_{0}\mid X_{t}=x]E_{R}[G_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]}$ |  |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})G_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]}{g_{t}(x)},$ |  |

where the Markov property of $R$ is used at second identity.
But,

|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})G_{1}\mid X_{t}=x]$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})G_{t+h}\mid X_{t}=x]$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle g_{t}(x)E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})\mid X_{t}=x]+E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})(G_{t+h}-G_{t})\mid X_{t}=x],$ |  |

where the first equality is a martingale identity.
We conclude by means of the definition of $L$:$\lim_{h\downarrow 0}h^{-1}E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})\mid X_{t}=x]=:Lu(x)$, and with the following identity $\lim_{h\downarrow 0}h^{-1}E_{R}[(U_{t+h}-U_{t})(G_{t+h}-G_{t})\mid X_{t}=x]=\Gamma(u,g_{t})(x).$ ∎

One sees that it is necessary that the functions $f_{t}$ and $g_{t}$ are regular enough to be in the domains of the carré du champ operators. For instance, choosing$f_{0},g_{1}\in\dom L$ insures that $f\in\dom(-\partial+L)$ and $g\in\dom(\partial+L)$ and also that $f$ and $g$ are *classical solutions* of the following parabolic PDEs

| (3.6) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-\partial_{t}+L)f(t,x)=0,&0<t\leq 1,\\ f_{0},&t=0,\end{array}\right.\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\partial_{t}+L)g(t,x)=0,&0\leq t<1,\\ g_{1},&t=1.\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Even better, since $R$ is assumed to be $m$-reversible, its Markov generator $L$ is self-adjoint on $L^{2}(m)$ and for any $f_{0},g_{1}$ in $L^{2}(m)$ we have $f\in\dom(-\partial+L)$ and $g\in\dom(\partial+L)$.

It is worthwhile describing the dynamics ([3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E5 "In Informal statement 3.6. ‣ The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) in terms of

| (3.7) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi:=\log f,\\ \psi:=\log g.\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Remark that because of ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E4 "In Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. | (3.8) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-\partial_{t}+B)\varphi(t,x)=0,&0<t\leq 1,\quad P_{t}\textrm{-a.e.}\\ \varphi_{0}=\log f_{0},&t=0,\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and

| (3.9) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\partial_{t}+B)\psi(t,x)=0,&0\leq t<1,\quad P_{t}\textrm{-a.e.}\\ \psi_{1}=\log g_{1},&t=1,\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

|  | $Bu:=e^{-u}Le^{u}$ |  |

## 4. Standard examples

We present two well-known reference processes: the reversible Brownian motion and a reversible random walk on a graph. We also apply the above general results to these important examples.

### Reversible Brownian motion

The reversible Brownian motion $R$ on $\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is specified by

| (4.1) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}L=\Delta/2,\\ R_{0}(dx)=m(dx)=dx\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where the Markov generator $L=\Delta/2$ is defined on $\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$. It is easily checked that $R$ is $m$-reversible.

Let $P=f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1})\,R\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ be any $(f,g)$-transform of $R$.
By the regularity improving property of the heat kernel, $(f_{0},g_{1})$ is such that $f\in\dom(-\partial_{t}+\Delta/2)$ for $t\in(0,1]$ and $g\in\dom(\partial_{t}+\Delta/2)$ for $t\in[0,1).$ We have$Bu=\Delta u/2+&#124;\nabla u&#124;^{2}/2,$ $\Gamma(u,v)=\nabla u\cdot\nabla v$ for any $u,v\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{X}).$ The expressions ([3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E5 "In Informal statement 3.6. ‣ The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. | (4.2) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\overrightarrow{A}_{t}&=&\Delta/2+\nabla\psi_{t}\cdot\nabla\\ \overleftarrow{A}_{t}&=&\Delta/2+\nabla\varphi_{t}\cdot\nabla\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

|  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\partial_{t}-\Delta/2)\mu_{t}(x)+\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}\nabla\psi_{t})(x)=0,&(t,x)\in(0,1]\times\mathcal{X}\\ \mu_{0},&t=0,\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\psi$ solves ([3.9](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E9 "In The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")):

| (4.3) |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\partial_{t}+\Delta/2)\psi_{t}(x)+&#124;\nabla\psi_{t}(x)&#124;^{2}/2=0,&(t,x)\in[0,1)\times\mathcal{X}\\ \psi_{1}=\log g_{1},&t=1,\end{array}\right.$ |  |

### Reversible random walk on a graph

Let $R$ be a random walk on a countable connected graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim)$ where $x\sim y$ means that $x$ and $y$ are next neighbours. Its generator is given for any finitely supported function $u$ by

| (4.10) |  | $Lu(x)=\sum_{y:y\sim x}[u(y)-u(x)]\,J_{x}(y),\quad x\in\mathcal{X}$ |  |
| ------ | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $J_{x}(y)>0$ for all $x\sim y$ is interpreted as the average frequency of jumps from $x$ to $y.$ Its dual formulation is the current equation

|  | $\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)=\sum_{y:y\sim x}[\rho_{t}(y)J_{y}(x)-\rho_{t}(x)J_{x}(y)],\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},0<t<1$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\rho_{t}(x):=R(X_{t}=x).$ Clearly, the path measure $R$ admits the stationary measure $m\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ if and only if $\sum_{y:y\sim x}[m_{y}J_{y}(x)-m_{x}J_{x}(y)]=0,$ for all $x\in\mathcal{X}.$ It admits $m$ as a reversing measure if this global equilibrium property is reinforced into the following detailed one:

|  | $m_{x}J_{x}(y)=m_{y}J_{y}(x),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},x\sim y.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

The special case where the jump measure

|  | $J_{x}:=\sum_{y:y\sim x}J_{x}(y)\,\delta_{y}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

is equal to $J^{o}_{x}=\frac{1}{n_{x}}\sum_{y:y\sim x}\delta_{y}$ where

|  | $n_{x}:=\#\left\{y:y\sim x\right\}<\infty$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

is the number of neighbours of $x$ which is assumed to be finite for all $x,$ corresponds to the *simple random walk* on the graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim).$ It is easily checked that its reversing measure is $m^{o}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}n_{x}\delta_{x}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ which is unbounded when $\mathcal{X}$ is infinite.

For simplicity we assume in the general case that

|  | $\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}J_{x}(\mathcal{X})<\infty$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $J_{x}(\mathcal{X}):=\sum_{y:y\sim x}J_{x}(y)$ is the global average frequency of jumps at $x$. This ensures that for any initial marginal $R_{0}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}),$ there exists a Markov measure $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ with generator $L$. Moreover, any bounded function $u:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ is in the domain of $L.$
Viewing $L=(L_{xy})_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}$ as a matrix with $L_{xy}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}J_{x}(y),&\textrm{if }x\sim y;\\ -J_{x}(\mathcal{X}),&\textrm{if }x=y;\\ 0,&\textrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right.$ and the functions as column vectors indexed by $\mathcal{X},$ we observe that the solutions of the heat equations ([3.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E6 "In The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. | (4.11) |  | $f_{t}=e^{tL}f_{0}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad g_{t}=e^{(1-t)L}g_{1},\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.$ |  |
| ------ | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

It follows that for any couple $(f_{0},g_{1})$ of nonnegative functions and any $0\leq t\leq 1,$ $f_{t}\in\dom(-\partial_{t}+L)$ and $g_{t}\in\dom(\partial_{t}+L).$

| ------ | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

$\Gamma(u,v)(x)=\sum_{y:y\sim x}[u(y)-u(x)][v(y)-v(x)]\,J_{x}(y),$ for any bounded functions $u,v$ and any $x\in\mathcal{X}.$ Let $P=f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1})\,R$ be any $(f,g)$-transform of the random walk $R.$ Applying ([3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E5 "In Informal statement 3.6. ‣ The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. where $R^{x}_{1}:=R(X_{1}\in\cdot&#124;X_{0}=x)\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ and $(\pi^{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})^{\mathcal{X}}$ is any measurable Markov kernel.

## 5. Slowing down

In this section, we describe an efficient way to recover optimal transport as a limit of Schrödinger problems. The main idea consists in slowing the reference process down to a no-motion process. In the following lines, we present some heuristics and refer the reader to [[Léo12a](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx40)] for a rigorous treatment in the case where $\mathcal{X}$ is a real vector space and [[Léoa](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx33)] in the alternate case where $\mathcal{X}$ is a discrete graph. The specific case of the reversible Brownian motion has been investigated by T. Mikami in [[Mik04](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx48)] with a stochastic control approach which differs from what is presented below.

Let $R$ be Markov with generator $L$. The slowed down process is represented by the sequence $(R^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ in $\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ of Markov measures associated with the generators

|  | $L^{k}:=L/k,\qquad k\geq 1.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Remark that slowing the process down doesn’t modify its reversible measure $m$; one converges more slowly towards the same equilibrium. Suppose also that the sequence $(R^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ in $\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ obeys the large deviation principle in $\Omega$ with speed $\alpha_{k}$ and rate function $C$, meaning approximately that for a “large class” of measurable subsets $A$ of $\Omega,$ we have

| (5.1) |  | $R^{k}(A)\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-\alpha_{k}\inf_{\omega\in A}C(\omega)\right].$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

For instance, in the case ([4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S4.E1 "In Reversible Brownian motion ‣ 4. Standard examples ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) when $R$ is the reversible Brownian motion on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, Schilder’s theorem tells us that $C$ is the kinetic action ([1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and $\alpha_{k}=k.$ In the case ([4.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S4.E10 "In Reversible random walk on a graph ‣ 4. Standard examples ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) when $R$ is a reversible random walk, it is proved in [[Léoa](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx33)] that $\alpha_{k}=\log k$ and the rate function is

| (5.2) |  | $C(\omega):=\sum_{0\leq t\leq 1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{t^{-}}\not=\omega_{t}\}},\qquad\omega\in\Omega,$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where in this situation $\Omega$ is the space of all right-continuous paths with finitely many jumps. Remark that $C$ is simply the total number of jumps of the path.

At a heuristic level, the $\Gamma$-convergence of

| (S${}^{k}_{\textrm{dyn}}$) |  | $H(P&#124;R^{k})/\alpha_{k}\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| --------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

as $k$ tends to infinity to

| (MK${}_{\textrm{dyn}}$) |  | $\int_{\Omega}C\,dP\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| ---------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

is best seen with the dual problems. Without getting into the details, to show this $\Gamma$-convergence, it is enough to prove that the objective functions of the dual problems converge pointwise, see [[Léo12a](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx40)] for the details. Let us check this pointwise convergence. Recall that the dual problem of ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is

| (D) |  | $\Delta_{R}(\varphi_{0},\psi_{1})\to\textrm{max};\qquad\varphi_{0},\psi_{1}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X})$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with $\Delta_{R}(\varphi_{0},\psi_{1}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}-\log\int_{\Omega}e^{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})}\,dR.$ Consequently, for each $k\geq 1,$ the dual problem of ([S k dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex10 "In The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) consists in maximizing $\alpha_{k}^{-1}\Delta_{R^{k}}(\varphi_{0},\psi_{1})$ or equivalently: $\alpha_{k}^{-1}\Delta_{R^{k}}(\alpha_{k}\varphi_{0},\alpha_{k}\psi_{1}).$ This leads us to

| (Dk) |  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}-\alpha_{k}^{-1}\log\int_{\Omega}e^{\alpha_{k}[\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})]}\,dR^{k}\to\textrm{max};\quad\varphi_{0},\psi_{1}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X})$ |  |
| ---- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

The pointwise limit, as $k$ tends to infinity, of $\alpha_{k}^{-1}\Delta_{R^{k}}(\alpha_{k}\varphi_{0},\alpha_{k}\psi_{1})$ is a direct consequence of the large deviation principle ([5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S5.E1 "In 5. Slowing down ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and the Laplace-Varadhan integral lemma [[DZ98](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx21), Thm. 4.3.1], which provide us with

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{k}^{-1}\log\int_{\Omega}e^{\alpha_{k}[\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})]}\,dR^{k}=\sup_{\Omega}\left\{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\right\}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Here, we took advantage of $\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})\in C_{b}(\Omega)$ to apply the Laplace-Varadhan lemma. We see that the pointwise limit of the objective function of ([Dk](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S5.Ex5 "In 5. Slowing down ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{k}^{-1}\Delta_{R^{k}}(\alpha_{k}\varphi_{0},\alpha_{k}\psi_{1})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}-\sup_{\Omega}\left\{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\right\}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and the limit dual problem is (this is informal)

|  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}-\sup_{\Omega}\left\{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\right\}\to\textrm{max};\qquad\varphi_{0},\psi_{1}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

But this problem is equivalent to

| (D∞) |  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}\to\textrm{max};\qquad\varphi_{0},\psi_{1}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}):\varphi_{0}\oplus\psi_{1}\leq c$ |  |
| ---- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where

| (5.3) |  | $c(x,y):=\inf\left\{C(\omega);\omega\in\Omega:\omega_{0}=x,\omega_{1}=y\right\},$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

recall ([1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). To see this, first remark that for any $\lambda\in\mathbb{R},$ transforming $\varphi_{0}\oplus\psi_{1}$ into $\varphi_{0}\oplus\psi_{1}+\lambda$ doesn’t modify the value of $\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}-\sup_{\Omega}\left\{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\right\}$. Hence, when $\sup_{\Omega}\left\{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\right\}<\infty,$ we can normalize $\varphi_{0}\oplus\psi_{1}$ so that $\sup_{\Omega}\left\{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\right\}=0$ and we obtain the equivalent problem

|  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}\to\textrm{max};\qquad\varphi_{0},\psi_{1}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}):\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\leq 0.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

But, $\varphi_{0}(X_{0})+\psi_{1}(X_{1})-C\leq 0$ on $\Omega$ if and only if $\varphi_{0}\oplus\psi_{1}\leq c$ on $\mathcal{X}^{2}.$
We have informally shown that $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\eqref{dk}=\eqref{dinf}$ (in some insufficiently specified sense) which is the usual Kantorovich problem, dual to ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). Consequently, we must have $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\eqref{skdyn}=\eqref{mk}$ in some sense.
Similarly, the static analogue of ([S k dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex10 "In The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) which is

| (Sk) |  | $H(\pi&#124;R^{k}_{01})/\alpha_{k}\to\textrm{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\pi_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| ---- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

converges to

| (MK) |  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}c\,d\pi\to\textrm{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\pi_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| ---- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with $c$ as above.

It happens that this convergence is in terms of $\Gamma$-convergence.

#### $\Gamma$-convergence

Recall that $\Gamma\textrm{-}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}=f$ on the metric space $Y$ if and only if for any $y\in Y,$

1. (a)

   $\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}(y_{k})\geq f(y)$ for any convergent sequence $y_{k}\to y,$

2. (b)

   $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}(y^{o}_{k})=f(y)$ for some sequence $y^{o}_{k}\to y.$

A function $f$ is said to be coercive if for any $a\geq\inf f,$ $\left\{f\leq a\right\}$ is a compact set.

The sequence $(f^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ is said to be equi-coercive if for any real $a$, there exists some compact set $K_{a}$ such that $\cup_{k}\left\{f^{k}\leq a\right\}\subset K_{a}.$
If in addition to $\Gamma\textrm{-}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}=f$, the sequence $(f^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ is equi-coercive,
then:

- —

  $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\inf f^{k}=\inf f,$

- —

  if $\inf f<\infty,$ any limit point $y^{*}$ of a sequence $(y^{*}_{k})_{k\geq 1}$ of approximate minimizers i.e.: $f^{k}(y^{*}_{k})\leq\inf f^{k}+\epsilon_{k}$ with $\epsilon_{k}\geq 0$ and $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\epsilon_{k}=0,$ minimizes $f$ i.e.: $f(y^{*})=\inf f.$

For more details about $\Gamma$-convergence, see [[DM93](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx17)] for instance.

The convex indicator $\iota_{A}$ of any subset $A,$ is defined to be equal to $0$ on $A$ and to $\infty$ outside $A.$ We denote for each $k\geq 2,$ (we drop $k=1$ not to divide by $\log(1)$ below),

|  | $I^{k}(P):=H(P&#124;R^{k})/\log k+\iota_{\{P:P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}\}},\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega),$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

so that ([S k dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S5.Ex2 "In 5. Slowing down ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is simply: ($I^{k}\to\textrm{min}).$ We also define

|  | $I(P)=E_{P}C+\iota_{\{P:P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}\}},\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with $C$ given at ([5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S5.E2 "In 5. Slowing down ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). The dynamic Monge-Kantorovich problem ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S5.Ex3 "In 5. Slowing down ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) rewrites as $(I\to\textrm{min})$.

Similarly, we denote for each $k\geq 2,$

## 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem

We consider a large number $n$ of independent (non-interacting) moving random particles in the state space $\mathcal{X}.$ They are described by the independent stochastic processes $Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n}$ taking their random values in $\Omega$ with the laws

| (6.1) |  | $\mathrm{Law}(Y^{i})=R(\cdot\mid X_{0}=y^{i}_{0})\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is a path measure and $y^{i}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}$ is the deterministic initial position of the $i$-th particle. It is also assumed that the particles are indistinguishable. Therefore, one doesn’t loose information considering the empirical probability measure

|  | $L^{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\delta_{Y^{i}}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

which is a random element of $\mathrm{P}(\Omega).$ At each time $t,$ the empirical measure of the particle system is the following random element of $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),$

|  | $L^{n}_{t}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\delta_{Y^{i}_{t}},\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Suppose that the initial positions are close to a profile $\mu_{0}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),$ i.e.

|  | $L^{n}_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\delta_{y^{i}_{0}}\ \underset{n\to\infty}{\rightarrow}\ \mu_{0}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with respect to the narrow topology $\sigma(\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),C_{b}(\mathcal{X})).$ The law of large numbers tells us that, as $n$ tends to infinity, $L^{n}$ converges in law to the deterministic limit ${}^{\mu_{0}}R:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}R(\cdot\mid X_{0}=x)\,\mu_{0}(dx)$ in $\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ and in particular that at time $t=1,$

|  | $L^{n}_{1}(dy)\ \underset{n\to\infty}{\rightarrow}\ (^{\mu_{0}}R)_{1}:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}R(X_{1}\in dy\mid X_{0}=x)\,\mu_{0}(dx).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Schrödinger addressed the following problem. Suppose that at the final time $t=1,$ you observe the system in a profile $L^{n}_{1}$ far away from the expected profile $(^{\mu_{0}}R)_{1}:$ for all large enough $n,$ $L^{n}_{1}$ is in a very small neighbourhood of some $\mu_{1}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ which doesn’t contain $(^{\mu_{0}}R)_{1}.$ This may happen since $n$ is finite, but this is a very rare event, i.e. with an exponentially small probability, see ([6.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E5 "In Solving the problem without getting into details ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) below. *Nevertheless, conditionally on this rare event, what is the most likely dynamical behaviour of the whole random system described by $L^{n}?$*

Before stating this rigorously at Problem [6.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.Thmtheorem1 "Problem 6.1 (Schrödinger’s question []). ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport") below, take a metric $\mathrm{d}$ on $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ compatible
with the narrow topology and
denote $B(\mu,\epsilon)=\{\nu\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X});\mathrm{d}(\nu,\mu)<\epsilon\}$ the open ball centred at $\mu$ with radius $\epsilon>0.$

###### Problem 6.1 (Schrödinger’s question [[Sch32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx65)]).

Let $\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ be given.
What is the limit

| (6.2) |  | $\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in\cdot&#124;L^{n}_{1}\in B(\mu_{1},\epsilon))$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

in $\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{P}(\Omega))?$

### Solving the problem without getting into details

Schrödinger’s approximate proof contains the main ideas. It is
based on a statistical physics approach. The main tool for obtaining
the limiting behavior as $n$ tends to infinity of the combinatoric
terms is Stirling’s formula. As pointed out by Föllmer in [[Föl88](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx25)], its modern counterpart, which is
available in a much more general setting, is
Sanov’s theorem.

###### Informal statement 6.2 (Informal statement of Sanov’s theorem).

Let $Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n},\dots$ be a sequence of independent identically distributed $\Omega$-valued random variables with common law $R\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$99 9 We take a probability measure, rather than $R\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega),$ for the simplicity of exposition.. Define $L^{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y^{i}}$ its empirical measure. Then, for a “large class” of measurable subsets $A$ of $\mathrm{P}(\Omega),$ we have

| (6.3) |  | $\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in A)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-n\inf_{P\in A}H(P&#124;R)\right].$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

The rigorous statement of this result is in
terms of a large deviation principle. It is valid for a general class of spaces $\Omega$, not necessarily a path space. For a comprehensive
introduction to the theory of large deviations including Sanov’s theorem, a good textbook is [[DZ98](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx21)]. One says that $H(\cdot&#124;R)$ is the rate
function of the large deviations of$\{L^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}$ as $n$ tends to infinity.

###### Idea of proof (a hint to agree with this statement).

We consider informally the situation where $\Omega$ is replaced by a three-point set.
Take $\Omega=\{a,b,c\},$ $R=\alpha\delta_{a}+\beta\delta_{b}+\gamma\delta_{c}$ and$P=p\delta_{a}+q\delta_{b}+r\delta_{c}$ with$\alpha,\beta,\gamma,p,q,r>0$ and $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=p+q+r=1.$ Then,

|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\approx P)$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}(a)\approx p,L^{n}(b)\approx q,L^{n}(c)\approx r)$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle\approx$ | $\displaystyle\frac{n!}{(np)!(nq)!(nr)!}\alpha^{np}\beta^{nq}\gamma^{nr}$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle\approx$ | $\displaystyle\exp[n\log n-np\log(np)-nq\log(nq)-nr\log(nr)+np\log\alpha+nq\log\beta+nr\log\gamma]$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\exp[-n(p\log(p/\alpha)+q\log(q/\beta)+r\log(r/\gamma))]$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\exp[-nH(P&#124;R)]$ |  |

where we used Stirling’s formula: $k!\approx\exp[k\log k-k]$ as $k$ tends to infinity.
∎

This hint is very much in the spirit of Schrödinger’s derivation
in [[Sch32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx65)].

Since $P\mapsto H(P&#124;R)\in[0,\infty]$ is
strictly convex and $H(P&#124;R)=0$ if and only if $P=R$, see ([A.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E2 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), one observes that if $R\in A,$ ([6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E3 "In Informal statement 6.2 (Informal statement of Sanov’s theorem). ‣ Solving the problem without getting into details ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) leads to the law of large
numbers: $\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}L^{n}=R,$ with an exponential rate of convergence.

We need a slight modification of Sanov’s theorem.

###### Informal statement 6.3 (Informal statement of Sanov’s modified theorem).

Let $Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n},\dots$ be the sequence of independent $\Omega$-valued random variables specified by ([6.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E1 "In 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). Define $L^{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y^{i}}$ its empirical measure. Then, for a “large class” of measurable subsets $A$ of $\mathrm{P}(\Omega),$ we have

| (6.4) |  | $\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in A)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-n\inf_{P\in A:P_{0}=\mu_{0}}\Big\{H(P&#124;R)-H(\mu_{0}&#124;R_{0})\Big\}\right].$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

This statement is proved by D. Dawson and J. Gärtner in [[DG87](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx14), Thm. 3.5] where the Schrödinger problem, when $R$ is a diffusion process, is re-discovered1010 10 No reference to the original papers by Schrödinger is given in [[DG87](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx14)]. and investigated by means of large deviations of large non-interacting particle systems. More precisely, [[DG87](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx14), Thm. 3.5] only states a variational formula in the spirit of for the rate function ([A.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E6 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). An alternate proof is given in [[CL95](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx7), Thm. 2.1] which states that the rate function is $H(P&#124;\mu_{0}R)+\iota_{\left\{P:P_{0}=\mu_{0}\right\}},$ where $\mu_{0}R:=\frac{d\mu_{0}}{dR_{0}}(X_{0})R(\cdot&#124;X_{0}).$ But it is easily seen with the additive property ([A.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E8 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) of the relative entropy that for any $P$ such that $P_{0}=\mu_{0},$ we have$H(P&#124;\mu_{0}R)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}H\big(P(\cdot&#124;X_{0}=x)&#124;R(\cdot&#124;X_{0}=x)\big)\,\mu_{0}(dx)$ and$H(P&#124;R)=H(\mu_{0}&#124;R_{0})+\int_{\mathcal{X}}H\big(P(\cdot&#124;X_{0}=x)&#124;R(\cdot&#124;X_{0}=x)\big)\,\mu_{0}(dx),$ which implies that $H(P&#124;\mu_{0}R)=H(P&#124;R)-H(\mu_{0}&#124;R_{0}),$ the desired result.

The conditional probability in ([6.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E2 "In Problem 6.1 (Schrödinger’s question []). ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) has the form$\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in A&#124;L^{n}\in C_{\epsilon})$ where

|  | $C_{\epsilon}=\{P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega);P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}\in B(\mu_{1},\epsilon)\}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and $\epsilon>0$ is introduced to guarantee
that $\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in C_{\epsilon})$ doesn’t vanish. With
([6.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E4 "In Informal statement 6.3 (Informal statement of Sanov’s modified theorem). ‣ Solving the problem without getting into details ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) one sees that for each $\epsilon>0$ and “all”$A\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega),$

|  | $\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in A&#124;L^{n}\in C_{\epsilon})\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-n\Big\{\inf_{P\in A\cap C_{\epsilon}}H(P&#124;R)-\inf_{P\in C_{\epsilon}}H(P&#124;R)\Big\}\right]$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Some analytical work (formally, think of $A$ as an arbitrarily
small neighbourhood of a generic $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$) allows us to show that this
implies that$\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in\cdot&#124;L^{n}\in C_{\epsilon})=\delta_{\widehat{P}_{\epsilon}}$ where $\widehat{P}_{\epsilon}$ is the solution of the convex minimization
problem

|  | $H(P&#124;R)\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in C_{\epsilon}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Note that this problem admits a unique solution since $H(\cdot&#124;R)$ is a *strictly* convex function on the convex set$C_{\epsilon}.$ Existence is obtained as usual showing that$H(\cdot&#124;R)$ has compact sublevel sets. Finally, as $\epsilon$ decreases to zero, $C_{\epsilon}$ decreases to$C=\{P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega);P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}\}$ and the
objective functions of the minimization problems on $C_{\epsilon}:$ $H(P&#124;R)+\iota_{C_{\epsilon}}(P)$ where$\iota_{C_{\epsilon}}(P)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\textrm{if }P\in C_{\epsilon}\\ \infty&\textrm{otherwise}\\ \end{array}\right.$ increase towards $H(P&#124;R)+\iota_{C}(P).$ Together with some compactness, this
monotonicity allows to prove easily that$\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\widehat{P}_{\epsilon}=\widehat{P}$ where $\widehat{P}$ is the
unique solution to the limiting minimization problem:

|  | $H(P&#124;R)\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in C.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Therefore, we have informally obtained the answer to Schrödinger’s
question.

###### Informal statement 6.4 (The answer to Schrödinger’s question).

The limit ([6.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E2 "In Problem 6.1 (Schrödinger’s question []). ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is

|  | $\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}\in\cdot&#124;L^{n}_{1}\in B(\mu_{1},\epsilon))=\delta_{\widehat{P}}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{P}(\Omega))$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\widehat{P}$ is the unique solution to the entropy minimization
problem

| (S${}_{\textrm{dyn}}$) |  | $H(P&#124;R)\to\emph{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}.$ |  |
| --------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Loosely speaking, this means that conditionally on$L^{n}_{0}\approx\mu_{0}$ and $L^{n}_{1}\approx\mu_{1},$ the whole system $L^{n}$ tends in law as $n$ tends to infinity towards $\widehat{P}.$ In fact, the
rigorous proof of this theorem [[Léo10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx39), Thm. 7.3] uses large deviation principles and
shows that this convergence is exponentially fast. Therefore,
Borel-Cantelli lemma allows us to state an almost sure version of
this conditional law of large numbers.

The same line of reasoning leads to the following evaluation of the probability that the system evolves spontaneously from the prepared initial profile $\mu_{0}$ to the unexpected profile final profile $\mu_{1}:$

| (6.5) |  | $\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathrm{Prob}(L^{n}_{1}\in B(\mu_{1},\epsilon)&#124;L^{n}_{0}\in B(\mu_{0},\epsilon))=-\big\{\inf\eqref{sdyn}-H(\mu_{0}&#124;m)\big\}.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

These considerations show that solving Schrödinger’s problem amounts to solve
the convex minimization problem ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex6 "In Definition 2.1 (Dynamic Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) which, in statistical physics,
enters the class of Boltzmann-Gibbs conditioning principles.

For a variation on this theme, with killed particles, see [[DGW90](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx15)].

### The lazy gas experiment

In his textbook [[Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx71), pp. 445-446], C. Villani writes in a section entitled *“A fluid mechanics feeling for Ricci curvature - The lazy gas experiment”*, the following sentences.

*Take a perfect gas in which particles do not interact, and ask him to move from a certain prescribed density field at time $t=0,$ to another prescribed density field at time $t=1.$ Since the gas is lazy, he will find a way to do so that needs a minimal amount of work (least action principle). Measure the entropy1111 11 Here, the entropy is standard Boltzmann’s one: $p\mapsto-H(p&#124;\mathrm{vol}),$ which is a concave function. of the gas at each time, and check that it always lies *above* the line joining the final and initial entropies. If such is the case, then we know that we live in a nonnegatively curved space.*

This is clearly Schrödinger’s thought experiment. As [[Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx71)] is only concerned with optimal transport, this lazy gas experiment must be understood at the level of the displacement interpolations. It refers to the important discovery by K.T. Sturm and M. von Renesse [[Sv05](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx68)] that entropy along displacement interpolations enjoys convexity properties related to Ricci lower bounds1212 12 The decisive milestones on the way towards this result are [[McC97](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx44), [OV00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx60), [CEMS01](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx6)].. Namely, Otto’s heuristic calculus (see [[JKO98](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx30), [Ott01](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx59)] and [[Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx71), Ch. 15]) allows us to guess that, along the displacement interpolations $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ with respect to quadratic optimal transport on a Riemannian manifold $\mathcal{X},$ the second derivative of the entropy as a function of time: $t\in{[0,1]}\mapsto h_{[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]}(t):=H(\mu_{t}&#124;\mathrm{vol})$ satisfies

| (6.6) |  | $h_{[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\langle\Gamma_{2}(\psi_{t}),\mu_{t}\rangle,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\psi$ solves ([1.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E8 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and $\Gamma_{2}(\psi):=L\Gamma(\psi)-2\Gamma(\psi,L\psi)$, with $L=\Delta/2,$ is the iterated carré du champ.
Bochner’s formula, relates $\Gamma_{2}$ and the Ricci curvature:

|  | $\Gamma_{2}(\psi)=\&#124;\nabla\psi\&#124;^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}+\mathrm{Ric}(\nabla\psi).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

This is the starting point of the Lott-Sturm-Villani theory [[Stu06a](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx66), [Stu06b](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx67), [LV09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx43)].

Schrödinger problem suggests a slight (more realistic :-) variant of this thought experiment where displacement interpolations are replaced with entropic interpolations, see ([1.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E12 "In The Monge-Kantorovich problem is a limit of Schrödinger problems ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). This is really a lazy gas experiment, while in some sense, the above mentioned lazy gas experiment in [[Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx71)] is a *very* lazy gas experiment. Indeed, in the displacement interpolation setting, not only the particles need to find a cooperative lazy behaviour (the transport mapping $x\mapsto y$) but also each individual particle must find an economic way to travel (the minimizing geodesic path) as a result of its intrinsic laziness: it is very slow and at the limit $k\to\infty,$ it doesn’t want to move at all, recall Statement [5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S5.Thmtheorem1 "Informal statement 5.1 (See []). ‣ 5. Slowing down ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport").

It is interesting to know that, without slowing down, along the entropic interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}],$

| (6.7) |  | $h_{[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\Gamma_{2}(\varphi_{t})+\Gamma_{2}(\psi_{t}),\mu_{t}\rangle,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where the functions $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are given at ([3.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E7 "In The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")): $\varphi_{t}(z)=\log E_{R}(f_{0}(X_{0})\mid X_{t}=z)$, $\psi_{t}(z)=\log E_{R}(g_{1}(X_{1})\mid X_{t}=z)$ with $f_{0},g_{1}$ such that $\mu_{t}=(f_{0}(X_{0})g_{1}(X_{1})\,R)_{t}$. This is proved by the author in [[Léob](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx34)]. Remark that ([6.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E7 "In The lazy gas experiment ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is an extension of ([6.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E6 "In The lazy gas experiment ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) where $\psi=-\varphi.$ Unlike this asymptotic case $(k=\infty)$ where the convexity estimate ([6.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E6 "In The lazy gas experiment ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is obtained informally by means of Otto’s heuristic calculus, the entropic interpolation formula ([6.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E7 "In The lazy gas experiment ‣ 6. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is rigorous.

In the general setting of a reference reversible measure $R$, and in particular with a reversible random walk on a graph, it is shown in [[Léob](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx34)] that stochastic calculus for the $(f,g)$-transforms as developed at Statement [3.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.Thmtheorem6 "Informal statement 3.6. ‣ The dynamics of the (𝑓,𝑔)-transform ‣ 3. | (6.8) |  | $h_{[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\Theta_{2}(\varphi_{t})+\Theta_{2}(\psi_{t}),\mu_{t}\rangle,\quad 0<t<1,$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where

| (6.9) |  | $\Theta_{2}\psi:=L\Theta\psi+e^{-\psi}\Gamma\left(e^{\psi},\Theta\psi\right)+e^{-\psi}\Gamma(e^{\psi},\psi)B\psi-e^{-\psi}\Gamma(e^{\psi}B\psi,\psi)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with

|  | $\Theta\psi:=e^{-\psi}\Gamma(e^{\psi},\psi)-B\psi+L\psi.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

In the special case where $L$ is a diffusion operator, then $\Theta=\Gamma$ and $\Theta_{2}=\Gamma_{2}.$ One may expect that formulae ([6.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E8 "In The lazy gas experiment ‣ 6. The statistical physics motivation of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([6.9](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S6.E9 "In The lazy gas experiment ‣ 6. ## 7. A short history of Schrödinger’s problem and related literature

Schrödinger’s problem was first addressed by E. Schrödinger in a German written article [[Sch31](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx64)] which was published in 1931 and entitled *“Über die Umkehrung der Naturgesetze”* 1313 13 On the reversibility of the laws of nature., then in a French written article [[Sch32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx65)] which was published in 1932 and entitled *“Sur la théorie relativiste de l’électron et l’interprétation de la mécanique quantique*1414 14 On the relativistic theory of the electron and the interpretation of quantum mechanics.. The entropy minimization problem appears at the last section VII of the 1932 article which can be read independently of the preceding sections and is entitled: *“Une analogie entre la mécanique ondulatoire et quelques problèmes de probabilités en physique classique*1515 15 An analogy between wave mechanics and some probabilistic problems in classical physics..

Let us quote Schrödinger’s introduction to this section.

 *Il s’agit d’un problème classique : problème de probabilités dans la théorie du mouvement brownien. Mais en fin de compte, il *ressortira* une analogie avec la mécanique ondulatoire, qui fut si frappante pour moi lorsque je l’eus trouvée, qu’il m’est difficile de la croire purement accidentelle.

À titre d’introduction, je voudrais citer une remarque que j’ai trouvée dans les “Glifford lectures” de A.S. Eddington (Cambridge, 1928, p. 216 et sqq). Eddington, en parlant de l’interprétation de la mécanique ondulatoire, fait dans une note en bas de page la remarque suivante : 1616 16 This is a classical problem: a probability problem in the theory of Brownian motion. But eventually an analogy with the wave mechanics will appear. This analogy stroke me so hard once I discovered it, that it is difficult for me to believe that it is purely accidental.
As an introduction, let me quote a remark that I found in the “Glifford lectures” of A. S. Eddington (Cambridge, 1928, p. 216 et sqq). Discussing the interpretation of wave mechanics, Eddington writes in a footnote the following remark: “The whole interpretation is very obscure, …”
“The whole interpretation is very obscure, but it seems to depend on wether you are considering the probability *after you know what has happened* or the probability for the purposes of prediction. The $\psi\bar{\psi}$ is obtained by introducing two symmetrical systems of $\psi$ waves travelling in opposite directions in time; one of these must presumably correspond to probable inference from what is known (or is stated) to have been the condition at a later time.”*

In 1931, wave mechanics is newly born and many physicists are puzzled by its possible interpretations. Based on Eddington’s remark, one may wonder at first sight if in the quantum world knowledge from the far future is available. Of course, this is not so, but why ? In his 1931-32 papers, Schrödinger solves this paradox by providing an amazingly close analogue of the quantum wave function propagation in the classical world, by means of the entropy minimization problem ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex6 "In Definition 2.1 (Dynamic Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). In particular, formula ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E4 "In Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) in Theorem [3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport") : $P_{t}(dx)=f_{t}(x)g_{t}(x)\,m(dx),$ must be interpreted as the classical analogue of Born’s formula : $P_{t}(dx)=\psi_{t}(x)\bar{\psi}_{t}(x)\,dx.$ Let us quote [[Sch32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx65)] again (this quotation also appears in [[Föl88](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx25)]) to emphasize that, although derived in a heuristic manner in [[Sch31](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx64), [Sch32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx65)], the system ([2.11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.E11 "In Theorem 2.8. ‣ Summing up ‣ Some properties of the minimizer 𝜋̂ of () ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and Born’s formula ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S3.E4 "In Theorem 3.4 (Euclidean analogue of Born’s formula). ‣ (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a reversible Markov measure ‣ 3. (𝑓,𝑔)-transform of a Markov measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) are motivated by the following question of large deviations in the framework of the lazy gas experiment :

 *Imaginez que vous observez un système de particules en diffusion, qui soient en équilibre thermodynamique. Admettons qu’à un instant donné $t_{0}$ vous les ayez trouvées en répartition à peu près uniforme et qu’à $t_{1}>t_{0}$ vous ayez trouvé un écart spontané et *considérable* par rapport à cette uniformité. On vous demande de quelle manière cet écart s’est produit. Quelle en est la manière la plus probable ? 1717 17 Imagine that you observe a system of diffusing particles which is in thermal equilibrium. Suppose that at a given time $t_{0}$ you see that their repartition is almost uniform and that at $t_{1}>t_{0}$ you find a spontaneous and *significant* deviation from this uniformity. You are asked to explain how this deviation occurred. What is its most likely behaviour?*

As a concluding comment in his 1932 article, Schrödinger writes :

 *La fonction [d’onde] complexe $\psi$ correspond à *deux* fonctions réelles, de sorte qu’il suffit de définir les conditions aux limites en se donnant la valeur de $\psi$ à *un seul* instant déterminé ; c’est la façon de voir généralement admise en mécanique quantique. Est-elle la seule admissible ? Dans notre problème, cela reviendrait à regarder comme données les valeurs de $f$ et $g$1818 18 With the notation of the present article. à un instant déterminé (au lieu des valeurs de leur produit à deux instants différents), chose inadmissible et absolument dénuée de sens.

Doit-on interpréter la remarque d’Eddington, citée plus haut, comme signalant la nécessité de modifier cette manière de voir en mécanique ondulatoire et prendre comme conditions aux limites les valeurs d’une seule probabilité réelle à deux instants différents ? 1919 19 The complex [wave] function $\psi$ corresponds to *two* real functions. Therefore, it is enough to define the limit conditions by prescribing the value of $\psi$ at a *unique* given time. This is the regular practice in quantum mechanics. Is it the only admissible one? In our problem, this would correspond to considering that the values of $f$ and $g$ [with the notation of the present article] are prescribed at a given time (instead of the values of their product at two distinct times). This is inadmissible and meaningless.
Should one interpret the previously quoted remark of Eddington, as a hint for the necessity of modifying our usual way of looking at quantum mechanics by defining the limit conditions in terms of the values of a single real probability at two distinct times?*

This has been performed in 1942 by R. Feynman in his PhD thesis [[Fey05](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx22)], without knowing Schrödinger’s contribution. Feynman’s thesis is entitled : *The principle of least action in quantum mechanics*. Based on a seminal article by Dirac [[Dir33](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx16)], entitled *The Lagrangian in quantum mechanics* (also reproduced in [[Fey05](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx22)]), and in contrast with the regular Hamiltonian approach, Feynman’s thesis proposes a Lagrangian approach to quantum mechanics which will be further developed in several directions, see [[FH65](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx24)].

Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and ([S](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Ex7 "In Definition 2.2 (Schrödinger’s problem). ‣ Statement of Schrödinger’s problem ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). Their explicit formulation is due to H. Föllmer in his Saint-Flour lecture notes [[Föl88](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx25), pp. 154-167]. ### Early mathematical developments

Although this part of Schrödinger’s work has been forgotten for some decades, it had influenced leading mathematicians soon after its publishing.

#### Reciprocal processes, 1932

Very soon after Schrödinger’s 1931 article, S. Bernstein published in 1932 an article [[Ber32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx2)] about the general problem of deriving limit theorems for sequences of dependent random variables. Among other notions, he explored the Markov property and, motivated by [[Sch31](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx64)], proposed a type of time-correlation which is less restrictive than the Markov property and is still symmetric with respect to time reversal2020 20 It is not clear that Bernstein was aware of the time-symmetry of the Markov property. This symmetry has clearly been identified twenty years later by J.L. Doob in his textbook [[Doo53](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx18)].. He suggested that such stochastic processes could be called reciprocal process. While a Markov measure $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ satisfies for any $0\leq s\leq t\leq 1,$

|  | $Q(X_{[0,s]}\in\cdot,X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\cdot\mid X_{[s,t]})=Q(X_{[0,s]}\in\cdot\mid X_{s})Q(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\cdot\mid X_{t}),$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

a path measure $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is reciprocal if for any $0\leq s\leq t\leq 1,$

|  | $Q(X_{[s,t]}\in\cdot\mid X_{[0,s]},X_{[t,1]})=Q(X_{[s,t]}\in\cdot\mid X_{s},X_{t}).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Any Markov measure is reciprocal, but the converse is false.

The theory of reciprocal processes has been forgotten for a while after Bernstein’s article and was eventually developed by B. Jamison in 1974, [[Jam74](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx28), [Jam75](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx29)]. A significant contribution of Jamison to the theory of Schrödinger problem was that its solution $\widehat{P}$ is not only reciprocal, but also Markov and it is indeed an $h$-transform of the Markov reference process. This is performed without any entropy, but solely by means of reciprocal transitions. Föllmer recovered these results in [[Föl88](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx25)] using the entropy minimization problem ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). For more information about the relations between reciprocal and Markov measures, see [[LRZ](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx42)].

#### Time-reversal, 1936

In the very first lines of his celebrated paper [[Kol36](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx32)] about Markov processes and time-reversal, A. Kolmogorov quotes Schrödinger’s 1931 paper as a motivation. This has been surprisingly forgotten afterwards.

#### Schrödinger system, 1940

Schrödinger had left open the problem of finding criteria for the system ([2.11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.E11 "In Theorem 2.8. ‣ Summing up ‣ Some properties of the minimizer 𝜋̂ of () ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) to have a solution $(f_{0},g_{1}).$ In 1940, R. Fortet [[For40](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx26)] proposed a partial solution and in 1960, A. Beurling [[Beu60](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx3)] gave a solution close to the statement of Theorem [2.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S2.Thmtheorem8 "Theorem 2.8. ‣ Summing up ‣ Some properties of the minimizer 𝜋̂ of () ‣ 2. Schrödinger’s problem ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport"). Beurling’s proof also relies upon an entropy argument. Beurling’s result was improved by Jamison in [[Jam75](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx29)] who obtained the complete solution of Schrödinger’s system.

### Stochastic deformations of mechanics

The aim of Euclidean quantum mechanics (EQM), which is mainly developed by J.-C. Zambrini since 1986 [[Zam86](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx72), [CZ91](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx10), [CWZ00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx9), [CZ08](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx11)], is to transfer by analogy, known results from quantum mechanics to the theory of stochastic processes and the other way round2121 21 Unlike Nelson’s stochastic mechanics [[Nel85](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx58)] or Nagasawa’s interpretation of quantum mechanics [[Nag00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx56)], EQM is not aimed at giving a stochastic interpretation of quantum mechanics. Such a project still remains an open problem eighty years after the advent of this theory.. The starting paper [[Zam86](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx72)] of this program relies on Schrödinger’s discovery and adapts Jamison’s results for an appropriate class of reciprocal processes (unlike Zambrini, Jamison doesn’t use the time-reversed filtration in his construction of reciprocal processes). Then the EQM program was extended to the derivation of rigorous results about various kind of stochastic processes which are suggested by the textbook *Quantum mechanics and path integrals* by Feynman and Hibbs [[FH65](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx24)]. This textbook presents, indeed, a time-symmetric (Lagrangian) approach to quantum mechanics which extends Feynman’s early works and in particular his PhD thesis [[Fey05](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx22)].

Feynman’s approach is an enlightening, efficient and intuitive guideline for physicists, but unfortunately it is impossible to put it on a rigorous mathematical ground : it is proved that Feynman’s integral is an oddly defined object.
However, replacing Feynman’s integration by stochastic calculus suggests interesting results about diffusion processes. The first of these results was the celebrated Feynman-Kac’s formula [[Kac49](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx31)]. EQM viewpoint, however, is that there is much more in Feynman’s method than this time-asymmetric measure theoretic perturbative formula. EQM uses Kac’s strategy in a systematic manner and its basic program is to obtain rigorous stochastic analogues of several intuitive statements from [[FH65](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx24)] ; intuitive, but highly efficient since they are corroborated by experiments. In EQM, the natural stochastic processes to work with are reciprocal processes. However, in several important situations, it appears that the critical (solving some variational problem) reciprocal processes are Markov. In this case, it is sufficient to work with $(f,g)$-transforms of Markov reference processes (see [[Jam75](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx29), [Föl88](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx25)] for an $h$-transform representation) and their extensions : $f_{0}(X_{0})\exp\big(\int_{0}^{1}U(X_{t})\,dt\big)g_{1}(X_{1})\,R$ with the additional Feynman-Kac integral term $(x,y)\mapsto\exp\big(\int_{0}^{1}U(X_{t})\,dt\big)\,R^{xy}$ which is the classical analogue of Feynman’s propagator. These extensions of $h$-transforms are also used by M. Nagasawa in [[Nag89](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx55), [Nag00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx56)] who also explores connections between stochastic processes and quantum physics which are highly inspired by the Schrödinger problem.

It is also possible to stochastically deform all the mathematical tools of classical mechanics to derive new results about diffusion processes. For instance, M. Thieullen designed a second order calculus for reciprocal processes in [[Thi93](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx69)] and without referring to ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) or entropy in general, M. Thieullen and J.-C. Zambrini have obtained a stochastic deformation of Noether theorem [[TZ97](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx70)].

#### An interesting problem

This suggests that it would be also interesting to derive a type of Noether theorem for the Monge-Kantorovich dynamical problem. Let us give some hint of what is meant. In the Euclidean case, the displacement interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ is a solution of ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S5.Ex3 "In 5. Slowing down ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) with $C=C_{\textrm{kin}}:=\int_{[0,1]}&#124;\dot{X}_{t}&#124;^{2}/2\,dt$, the kinetic action. It has a constant speed ; this means that twice the average kinetic energy $\int_{\Omega}&#124;\dot{X}_{t}&#124;^{2}\,d\widehat{P}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}&#124;\nabla\psi_{t}(x)&#124;^{2}\,\mu_{t}(dx),$ with the notation of the Benamou-Brenier formula ([1.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.E7 "In Displacement interpolations in P_2⁢(𝒳) ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), doesn’t depend on time $t$. What happens when considering, instead of $C_{\textrm{kin}}$, the action functional $C=\int_{[0,1]}\big(&#124;\dot{X}_{t}&#124;^{2}/2+U(X_{t})\big)\,dt$ which should be connected with some Newton equation ? What are the quantities that are conserved along the minimizer $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}],$ in terms of the symmetries of the potential $U?$

### Stochastic optimal control

Optimal transport can be deformed into a stochastic optimal control problem. This is mainly the contribution of T. Mikami, see [[Mik09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx51)] for an overview of this approach and some of its main developments. With ([4.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S4.E7 "In item iii ‣ Proof. ‣ Minimal action ‣ Reversible Brownian motion ‣ 4. Standard examples ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), one obtains that the Brownian Schrödinger problem ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")), i.e. taking $R$ to be the reversible Brownian motion, is also expressed as follows :

| (7.1) |  | $E_{P^{u}}\int_{0}^{1}L(u_{t})\,dt\to\textrm{min};\quad u\in\mathcal{A}:P^{u}_{0}=\mu_{0},P^{u}_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set (of admissible controls) which consists of all the $\mathbb{R}^{n}$-valued progressively measurable processes $u$ and $P^{u}$ (if it exists) is the law of the semi-martingale

|  | $X^{u}_{t}=X^{u}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}\,ds+W_{t},\quad 0\leq t\leq 1$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $W$ is a standard Brownian motion starting from 0 and

|  | $L(u)=&#124;u&#124;^{2}/2,\quad u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

This is a stochastic version of the quadratic Monge-Kantorovich problem ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) :

|  | $E_{P^{u}}\int_{0}^{1}L(u_{t})\,dt\to\textrm{min};\quad u\in\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{MK}}:P^{u}_{0}=\mu_{0},P^{u}_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

which is obtained by replacing $\mathcal{A}$ with $\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{MK}},$ the set of all controls $u\in L^{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}({[0,1]})$ and taking $P^{u}$ to be the law of

|  | $X^{u}_{t}=X^{u}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}\,ds,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

a process with a random initial position and a deterministic evolution.

This theory extends naturally to the case where $L$ is strictly convex, regular and coercive enough : $\lim_{&#124;u&#124;\to\infty}L(u)/&#124;u&#124;^{p}=\infty,$ for some $p>1.$ But results close to optimal transport are obtained with $L$ admitting a quadratic growth, i.e. in harmony with the Brownian motion $W.$

When $L$ is quadratic, if the Brownian motion $W$ is replaced with $\sqrt{\epsilon}W$ and $\epsilon$ tends to zero, then Mikami
shows in [[Mik04](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx48)] that ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) tends to ([MKdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). Unfortunately, this type of convergence remains unclear unless $L$ is quadratic, i.e. unless the stochastic optimal control problem corresponds to ([S dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S1.Ex2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")).

T. Mikami and M. Thieullen have proved a Kantorovich-type dual equality in [[MT06](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx53)] for ([7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#S7.E1 "In Stochastic optimal control ‣ 7. A short history of Schrödinger’s problem and related literature ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) and recovered related optimal transport results in [[MT08](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx54)]. T. Mikami has intensively studied the connections between stochastic control and optimal transport. In particular, soon after the discovery by Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto [[JKO98](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx30)] of the relation between gradient flows, Wasserstein distance and dissipative evolution equations, he proposed in [[Mik00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx46)] a stochastic approach to the JKO approximation scheme. In addition to the already cited articles by Mikami, several other works by the same author are related to a probabilistic approach to optimal transport : [[Mik02](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx47), [Mik06](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx49), [Mik08](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx50), [Mik12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx52)].
Let us also quote the early contributions of Mikami [[Mik90](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx45)] and P. Dai Pra [[Dai91](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx12)] where the Schrödinger problem is translated in terms of stochastic control.

## Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure

This appendix section is a short version of [[Léoc](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#bib.bibx35), § 2] which we refer to for more details.
Let $r$ be some $\sigma$-finite positive measure on some space $Y$. The relative entropy of the probability measure $p$ with respect to $r$ is loosely defined by

| (A.1) |  | $H(p&#124;r):=\int_{Y}\log(dp/dr)\,dp\in(-\infty,\infty],\qquad p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

if $p\ll r$ and $H(p&#124;r)=\infty$ otherwise.

More precisely, when $r$ is a probability measure, we have

|  | $H(p&#124;r)=\int_{Y}h(dp/dr)\,dr\in[0,\infty],\qquad p,r\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with $h(a)=a\log a-a+1\geq 0$ for all $a\geq 0,$ (take $h(0)=1).$ Hence, the definition ([A.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E1 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) is meaningful. It follows from the strict convexity of $h$ that $H(\cdot&#124;r)$ is also strictly convex. In addition, since $h(a)=\inf h=0\iff a=1,$ we also have for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y),$

| (A.2) |  | $H(p&#124;r)=\inf H(\cdot&#124;r)=0\iff p=r.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

If $r$ is unbounded, one must restrict the definition of $H(\cdot&#124;r)$ to some subset of $\mathrm{P}(Y)$ as follows. As $r$ is assumed to be $\sigma$-finite, there exist measurable functions $W:Y\to[1,\infty)$ such that

| (A.3) |  | $z_{W}:=\int_{Y}e^{-W}\,dr<\infty.$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Define the probability measure $r_{W}:=z_{W}^{-1}e^{-W}\,r$ so that $\log(dp/dr)=\log(dp/dr_{W})-W-\log z_{W}.$ It follows that for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ satisfying $\int_{Y}W\,dp<\infty,$ the formula

| (A.4) |  | $H(p&#124;r):=H(p&#124;r_{W})-\int_{Y}W\,dp-\log z_{W}\in(-\infty,\infty]$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

is a meaningful definition of the relative entropy which is coherent in the following sense. If $\int_{Y}W^{\prime}\,dp<\infty$ for another measurable function $W^{\prime}:Y\to[0,\infty)$ such that $z_{W^{\prime}}<\infty,$ then $H(p&#124;r_{W})-\int_{Y}W\,dp-\log z_{W}=H(p&#124;r_{W^{\prime}})-\int_{Y}W^{\prime}\,dp-\log z_{W^{\prime}}\in(-\infty,\infty]$.

Therefore, $H(p&#124;r)$ is well-defined for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ such that $\int_{Y}W\,dp<\infty$ for some measurable nonnegative function $W$ verifying ([A.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E3 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). It can be proved that

| (A.5) |  | $\displaystyle H(p&#124;r)$ | $\displaystyle\overset{(\textrm{i})}{=}$ | $\displaystyle\sup\left\{\int_{Y}u\,dp-\log\int_{Y}e^{u}\,dr;u:Y\to[-\infty,\infty),\int_{Y}e^{u}\,dr<\infty\right\}$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
| (A.6) |  |                                | $\displaystyle\overset{(\textrm{ii})}{=}$ | $\displaystyle\sup\left\{\int_{Y}u\,dp-\log\int_{Y}e^{u}\,dr;u\in C_{W}(Y)\right\},$ |  |

where

1. (i)

   identity (i) is valid when $p$ is assumed to be absolutely continuous with respect to $r;$

2. (ii)

   identity (ii) is meaningful when $Y$ is a topological space equipped with its Borel $\sigma$-field since we have set $C_{W}(Y)$ to be the space of all continuous functions $u:Y\to\mathbb{R}$ such that $\sup&#124;u&#124;/W<\infty$, where $W$ is any nonnegative function satisfying ([A.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E3 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")). In this case, it follows that, being the supremum of affine continuous functions, $H(\cdot&#124;r)$ is a convex lower semi-continuous function with respect to the weak topology $\sigma(\{p\in\mathrm{P}(Y);\int_{Y}W\,dp<\infty\},C_{W}(Y))$.

Clearly, identity (i) entails that $H(p&#124;r)=\infty$ whenever $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ is such that $\int_{Y}W\,dp=\infty$
It follows from the strict convexity of $H(\cdot&#124;r_{W})$ and ([A.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E4 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) that $H(\cdot&#124;r)$ is also strictly convex.

Let $Y$ and $Z$ be two Polish spaces equipped with their Borel $\sigma$-fields. For any measurable function $\phi:Y\to Z$ and any measure $q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(Y)$ we have the disintegration formula

| (A.7) |  | $q(dy)=\int_{Z}q(dy&#124;\phi=z)\,\phi_{\#}q(dz)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $z\in Z\mapsto q(\cdot&#124;\phi=z)\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ is measurable, and the following additive property

| (A.8) |  | $H(p&#124;r)=H(\phi_{\#}p&#124;\phi_{\#}r)+\int_{Z}H\Big(p(\cdot\mid\phi=z)&#124;r(\cdot\mid\phi=z)\Big)\,\phi_{\#}p(dz),$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

is valid for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ and any $\sigma$-finite $r\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(Y).$ In particular, as $r(\cdot\mid\phi=z)$ is a probability measure for each $z$, with ([A.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0215v1#A1.E2 "In Annexe A Relative entropy with respect to an unbounded measure ‣ A survey of the Schrödinger problem and some of its connections with optimal transport")) we see that

| (A.9) |  | $H(\phi_{\#}p&#124;\phi_{\#}r)\leq H(p&#124;r),\quad\forall p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| ----- | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with equality if and only if

| (A.10) |  | $p(\cdot\mid\phi=z)=r(\cdot\mid\phi=z),\quad\forall z,\phi_{\#}p\textrm{-a.s.}$ |  |
