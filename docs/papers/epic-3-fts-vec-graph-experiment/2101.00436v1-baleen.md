###### Abstract

Multi-hop reasoning (i.e., reasoning across two or more documents) at scale is a key step toward NLP models that can exhibit broad world knowledge by leveraging large collections of documents. We propose Baleen, a system that improves the robustness and scalability of multi-hop reasoning over current approaches. Baleen introduces a per-hop *condensed retrieval* pipeline to mitigate the size of the search space, a *focused late interaction* retriever (FliBERT) that can model complex multi-hop queries, and a weak supervision strategy, *latent hop ordering*, to learn from limited signal about which documents to retrieve for a query. We evaluate Baleen on the new many-hop claim verification dataset HoVer, establishing state-of-the-art performance.

## 1 Introduction

In *open-domain* reasoning, a model is tasked with retrieving evidence from a large corpus to answer questions, verify claims, or otherwise exhibit broad world knowledge. In practice, such open-domain tasks often further require *multi-hop* reasoning, where the evidence must be extracted from two or more documents. To do this effectively, a model must learn to use partial retrieved evidence to bridge its way to additional documents leading to an answer. In this vein, HotPotQA [Yang et al. (2018)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib25) contains complex questions that multi-hop models answer by retrieving two passages from Wikipedia, while HoVer [Jiang et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib9) contains claims that can only be verified (or disproven) by extracting multiple facts from up to four Wikipedia passages.

To illustrate, consider the claim *“The MVP of [a] game Red Flaherty umpired was elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame”* from the HoVer validation set. To correctly verify this claim, a HoVer model must identify facts spread across three Wikipedia pages: *Red Flaherty* umpired in the world series of 1955, 1958, 1965, and 1970. The MVP of the *1965 World Series* was *Sandy Koufax*. Koufax was later elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame.

This three-hop claim illustrates three major challenges in multi-hop retrieval. First, *multi-hop queries encompass multiple information needs*; the claim above referenced facts from three disparate passages. Second, *retrieval errors in each hop propagate to subsequent hops*. This can happen if the model decides to directly retrieve information about the Baseball Hall of Fame, confuses Red Flaherty with, say, Robert Flaherty, or singles out the MVP of, say, the *1958* World Series, which Flaherty also umpired. Third, due to the dependency between the hops, *multi-hop retrievers must learn to follow an effective sequence of hops*, where previously-retrieved clues lead to other relevant passages. Such effective orders are not always obvious, especially in *many-hop* settings with three or more passages, and it can be expensive to expect human annotators to identify one (or every) sequence in which facts could be retrieved to provide training data for this task.

The above challenges call for models that have highly expressive query representations, that are robust to retrieval errors, and that scale to many hops over massive document collections. Existing systems fall short on one or more of these criteria. For instance, many state-of-the-art systems rely on bag-of-words [Qi et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib17) or single-vector dot-product [Xiong et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib24) retrievers, whose capacity to model a single open-domain question is limited [Khattab et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib11), let alone to encode complex multi-hop queries. Furthermore, existing systems embed trade-offs when it comes to sequencing: they employ brittle greedy search, which reduces recall per hop; or they employ extensive beam search over an exponential space, which reduces scalability; or they assume explicit links that connect every passage with related entities, which limits them to link-structured corpora. Lastly, to enforce an effective order of hops, many systems use fragile supervision heuristics specific to the two-hop question-answering pattern of HotPotQA that cannot be applied to settings with more hops.

To tackle these problems, we propose Baleen,11 1 In marine biology, the term “baleen” refers to the filter-feeding system that baleen whales use to capture small organisms in their mouths, filtering out seawater. So too does our system seek to capture relevant facts from a sea of documents. a new multi-hop reasoning system that improves both robustness and scalability.
First, to address the complexity of retrieval for multi-hop reasoning, we propose *focused late interaction*, an effective learned-retrieval mechanism for multi-hop queries. We instantiate this mechanism in our retriever, which we call ‘Focused late interaction over BERT’ (FliBERT). FliBERT follows the late interaction paradigm of ColBERT [Khattab and Zaharia (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib12) but explicitly permits the encoding of multiple, disparate information needs for each query. Second, to control the search-space growth after every hop, we introduce a two-stage *condenser*. The condenser summarizes the retrieved information over all previous hops into a short context that preserves high recall of relevant facts. Third, to discover an effective order of hops for training with many-hop examples, we propose *latent hop ordering*, an iterative weak-supervision strategy that uses the retriever being trained to select the best hop sequences for training.

In this short report, we apply Baleen to the many-hop claim verification dataset HoVer [Jiang et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib9). We report preliminary results on the HoVer validation set and will submit a future version of our system to the official leaderboard.22 2 <https://hover-nlp.github.io/>33 3 Our code and other model information will be uploaded at <https://github.com/stanford-futuredata/Baleen>. Against the official baseline, Baleen improves passage retrieval accuracy by over 46 points (absolute), raising it to about 90% at $k=100$ passages. It also improves the evidence extraction F1 score by over 30 points, outperforming even the official *Oracle Retrieval + BERT* results by [Jiang et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib9).

## 2 Background & Related Work

### 2.1 Open-Domain Reasoning

There is significant recent interest in NLP models that can solve tasks by retrieving evidence from a large corpus. The most popular such task is arguably open-domain question answering (OpenQA; [Chen et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib2)), which extends the well-studied machine reading comprehension (MRC) problem over supplied question–passage pairs to answering questions given only a large text corpus like Wikipedia. Other open-domain tasks span claim verification (e.g., FEVER; [Thorne et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib19)), question generation (e.g., with Jeopardy as in [Lewis et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib14)), and open dialogue (e.g., Wizard of Wikipedia; [Dinan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib6)), among others. Many of these datasets are compiled in the recently-introduced KILT benchmark [Petroni et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib16) for *knowledge-intensive* tasks.

Among models for these tasks, the most relevant to our work are OpenQA models that include *learned* retrieval components. These include ORQA [Lee et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib13), REALM [Guu et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib7), and DPR [Karpukhin et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib10). [Lewis et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib14) introduced RAG, which by virtue of a seq2seq architecture can tackle OpenQA as well as other open-domain problems, including claim verification and question generation. Recently, [Khattab et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib11) introduced ColBERT-QA, which uses the ColBERT [Khattab and Zaharia (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib12) retriever, both closely related works that we discuss in §[2.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S2.SS4 "2.4 ColBERT: Late Interaction Paradigm ‣ 2 Background & Related Work ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval").

### 2.2 Multi-Hop Open-Domain Reasoning

With a few exceptions, the examples in the open-domain tasks from §[2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S2.SS1 "2.1 Open-Domain Reasoning ‣ 2 Background & Related Work ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval") can be solved by finding *one* relevant passage in the corpus, often by design. In contrast, a number of recent works explore multi-hop reasoning over multiple passages. These include QAngaroo [Welbl et al. (2018)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib22) and 2WikiMultiHopQA [Ho et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib8), among others. However, these tasks supply the relevant passages for each example (possibly among other “distractors”), and thus do not require retrieval from a large corpus.

To our knowledge, HotPotQA was the first large-scale open-domain multi-hop task, particularly in its open-domain “fullwiki” setting. This dataset catalyzed much follow-up research. However, HotPotQA is limited to only two-hop questions, many of which may not require strong multi-hop reasoning capabilities (see [Chen and Durrett 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib3); [Wang et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib21)). This softens many of the challenges of multi-hop reasoning discussed §[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval").

Very recently, [Jiang et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib9) introduced the HoVer many-hop verification dataset, which contains two-, three-, and four-hop examples. HoVer contains just over 18,000 training examples. This is about 5$\times$ smaller than HotPotQA, which adds to the challenge posed by the dataset.

### 2.3 Multi-Hop Open-Domain Models

To conduct the “hops” of retrieval, many prior multi-hop systems (e.g., [Asai et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib1); [Zhao et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib26)) assume explicit links that connect every passage with related entities. We argue that this risks tying systems to link-structured knowledge bases like Wikipedia and producing brittle architectures built around datasets like HotPotQA [Yang et al. (2018)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib25) that are constructed by following hyperlinks. Recently, [Xiong et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib24) and [Qi et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib17) introduce MDR and IRRR, state-of-the-art systems that assume no explicit link structure. Instead, they use an *iterative retrieval* paradigm that retrieves passages relevant to the question, reads these passages, and then formulates a new query for another hop if necessary. In this work, we adopt this iterative retrieval formulation and tackle three unique challenges for multi-hop retrieval.

### 2.4 ColBERT: Late Interaction Paradigm

The current state-of-the-art systems for OpenQA use learned retrieval. While most such systems encode every query and passage into a single dense vector, [Khattab and Zaharia (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib12) introduced late fine-grained interaction, where every query and every document is represented using a vector *for each constituent token*. Later, [Khattab et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib11) found that fine-grained interaction performs better for OpenQA and proposed an iterative strategy for weakly-supervising such retrievers.

In ColBERT, a query is tokenized and the tokens fed into a BERT encoder, which embeds the query into a matrix of $N$ vectors (given $N$ tokens). Similarly, every passage in the corpus is tokenized and embedded into a matrix of $M$ vectors (for $M$ tokens). Since the passage representations do not depend on any query, they are computed offline and indexed for fast retrieval.

For retrieval, ColBERT assigns a score to a passage (given a query) via late interaction. In particular, every query vector is matched against *all* the vectors in one passage, and the maximum-similarity score is kept. [Khattab and Zaharia (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib12) refer to this operator as MaxSim. For every passage, the MaxSim scores of all query vectors are added. [Khattab and Zaharia (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib12) describe how this MaxSim-decomposed late interaction enables scaling ColBERT to massive collections with millions of passages, using vector-similarity indexes. In this work, we propose a retriever, namely FliBERT, that devises an improved late interaction mechanism that targets complex multi-hop queries.

## 3 Baleen

We now introduce Baleen, which uses an iterative retrieval paradigm to find relevant facts in $T\geq 1$ success hops.

During inference, the input to Baleen is a textual *query* $Q_{0}$—for instance, a question to answer or a claim to verify. The $t^{th}$ hop begins with query $Q_{t-1}$, which includes the initial $Q_{0}$ in addition to relevant facts extracted from earlier hops, if any. First, $Q_{t-1}$ is supplied to Baleen’s retriever, namely FliBERT (§[3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S3.SS1 "3.1 FliBERT: Focused Late Interaction ‣ 3 Baleen ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval")), which selects $K$ relevant passages from the full corpus. Second, these passages are fed into a two-stage *condenser* model (§[3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S3.SS2 "3.2 Condenser: Per-Hop Fact Extraction ‣ 3 Baleen ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval")), which reads these passages and extracts the *facts* (i.e., individual sentences) that are most relevant to $Q_{t-1}$. Third, the selected facts are *condensed* (or gathered from their source passages into a single sequence) and added to $Q_{t}$ for the subsequent hop, if any. Once all of the hops are complete, Baleen’s task-specific *reader* (§[3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S3.SS3 "3.3 Reader: Task-Specific Processing ‣ 3 Baleen ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval")) processes $Q_{T}$, which contains the query and the condensed facts, allowing it to evaluate the original query (e.g., verify a claim) given sufficient background information.

Baleen’s retrievers, condensers, and readers are implemented as Transformer encoders [Vaswani et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib20). In particular, FliBERT uses BERT-base [Devlin et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib5) and the condensers use ELECTRA-large [Clark et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib4). To supervise FliBERT (and the condensers) for multi-hop retrieval, we introduce a simple yet effective *latent hop ordering* scheme (§[3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S3.SS4 "3.4 Supervision: Latent Hop Ordering ‣ 3 Baleen ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval")), which trains a weakly-supervised retriever for each hop and then uses this retriever to establish a preference over the passages to be retrieved in every training hop.

### 3.1 FliBERT: Focused Late Interaction

Multi-hop queries are often complex, often requiring information from multiple passages with little in common. Existing retrievers, whether traditional (e.g., TF-IDF) or neural ([Lee et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib13); [Karpukhin et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib10); [Khattab and Zaharia, 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib12)), seek passages that match *all* of the query, whereas this setting calls for a retriever that can match the same query effectively against disparate contexts.

To do so, we introduce a *focused late interaction* mechanism over BERT, which we refer to as FliBERT. The FliBERT encoders and interaction mechanism are shown in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.4 ColBERT: Late Interaction Paradigm ‣ 2 Background & Related Work ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval"). FliBERT inherits the scalability and fine-grained interaction of ColBERT (§[2.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S2.SS4 "2.4 ColBERT: Late Interaction Paradigm ‣ 2 Background & Related Work ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval")) while modifying its matching mechanism to better accommodate multi-hop queries.

Given a query $Q_{t}$ in the $t^{th}$ hop, the query encoder outputs a vector representation of every token in the input. Each query embedding interacts with all passage embeddings via a MaxSim operator. Whereas ColBERT sums all the MaxSim outputs indiscriminately, FliBERT considers only the strongest-matching query embeddings for evaluating each passage, by adding only the top-$\hat{N}$ partial scores from $N$ scores, with $\hat{N}<N$.

We refer to this top-$k$ filter as a “focused” interaction. Intuitively, it allows the same query to match multiple relevant passages that are contextually unrelated, by aligning—during training and inference—a different subset of the query embeddings with different relevant passages. FliBERT applies this focused interaction mechanism over the representation of the query and the facts separately, keeping the top-$\hat{N}$ and top-$\hat{L}$ from $N$ and $L$ embeddings, respectively.

More formally, the FliBERT score $S_{q,d}$ of a passage $d$ given a query $q$ is the summation

|  | $S_{q,d}=S^{Q}_{q,d}+S^{F}_{q,d}$ |  | (1) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | --- |

over the scores corresponding to the original query and to the facts. Define $M^{Q}_{i}$ as

|  | $M^{Q}_{i}=\max_{j=1}^{m}E_{q_{i}}\cdot E_{d_{j}}^{T}$ |  | (2) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | --- |

that is, the maximum similarity of the $i^{th}$ query embedding against the document embeddings. Then, the partial score $S^{Q}$ is computed as

|  | $S^{Q}_{q,d}=\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{N}}\textrm{top}_{\hat{N}}\left\{M^{Q}_{i}:i\in[N]\right\}$ |  | (3) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | --- |

where $\textrm{top}_{\hat{N}}$ is an operator that keeps the largest $\hat{N}$ values in a set. We define $S^{F}_{q,d}$ similarly, using the fact matches $M^{F}_{i}$ instead of $M^{Q}_{i}$.

### 3.2 Condenser: Per-Hop Fact Extraction

Baleen must maintain high recall of the facts from every hop, but it must also scale well. To this end, Baleen introduces a *condensed retrieval* pipeline in every hop, which summarizes the pertinent facts retrieved in the current and previous hops, condensing this information into a relatively short context with high recall. By repeating this at the end of every hop, the total amount of context remains bounded by a constant while spanning facts from multiple passages within and across hops.

Given the retrieved top-$K$ passages of the $t^{th}$ hop, the condenser proceeds in two stages: (a) passage-level fact ranking and (b) cross-passage fact extraction. The first stage feeds the condenser $Q_{t}$, which includes the original query and any retrieved facts from the previous hop, and each retrieved passage. Every passage is split into its constituent sentences, and every sentence is prepended with a special token. The output embeddings corresponding to the special tokens are scored via a linear layer, assigning a score to each sentence in each retrieved passage. We train the first-stage condenser with a cross-entropy loss over the sentences of two passages, one positive and one negative.

Across all $K$ passages, the top-$k$ facts are identified and concatenated into the second-stage condenser model. As in the passage-level case, we include special tokens between the sentences. The second-stage condenser assigns a score to every fact while attending jointly over all of the top-$k$ facts, allowing a direct comparison and mutual conditioning among all. Every fact whose score is positive is selected to proceed, and the other facts are discarded. We train the second-hop condenser over a set of 7–9 facts, some positive and other (sampled) negatives, using a linear combination of cross-entropy loss for each positive fact (against all negatives) and binary cross-entropy loss for each individual fact.

### 3.3 Reader: Task-Specific Processing

After all hops are conducted, a reader is used to extract answers or verify the claim. We use a standard Transformer encoder, in particular ELECTRA-large ([Clark et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib4)), which we feed the final output $Q_{t}$ of our multi-hop retrieval and condensing pipeline. We train for question answering similar to [Khattab et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib11) or claim verification similar to [Jiang et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib9).

### 3.4 Supervision: Latent Hop Ordering

For every training example, we need a *sequence* or order over the relevant passages in order to train the retriever and condensers to use $Q_{t-1}$ to produce $Q_{t}$ during hop $t$. We seek a generic mechanism for identifying the best passage(s) to learn to retrieve next at every hop.

To train a FliBERT model for the $t^{th}$ hop, we proceed in two rounds of training. To begin with, we train the model to use $Q_{t-1}$ and retrieve every relevant passage *not* already represented with facts in $Q_{t-1}$. In particular, we create triples of the query $Q_{t-1}$, positive passages (as described), and negative passages sampled from the top-1000 passages of the $(t-1)^{th}$ hop retrieval over the training set.

Once this round of training is complete, we use this weakly-supervised retriever over every query $Q_{t-1}$ in the training set, and retain only the positives ranked highly by this model. We then proceed to the second round of supervision, where another FliBERT model is trained using these positives and using negative passages sampled from the (harder) top-1000 passages of the first-round retriever.

The above procedure is applied for $t\geq 2$. For the first hop (i.e., with $t=1$), we use a pretrained ColBERT retriever to identify the highest-ranking positives over the training set. We supervise the first-round retriever (for the first-hop) with these positives, and conduct a second round similar to the general procedure above. Our supervision for the first hop thus shares similarities with GoldEn [Qi et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib18), which uses a bag-of-words model to identify the “more easily retrieved” passage (among two) for HotPotQA.

## 4 Development-Set HoVer Evaluation

In this section, we apply Baleen on the many-hop claim verification dataset HoVer, following the evaluation protocol of the authors [Jiang et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib9). First, we study the retrieval performance of Baleen in §[4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S4.SS3 "4.3 Evaluating Retrieval Effectiveness ‣ 4 Development-Set HoVer Evaluation ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval"). ### 4.1 Task Description

The input to the model is a claim, which is often one or a few sentences long. The model is to attempt to verify this claim, outputting “Supported” if the corpus contains enough evidence to verify the claim, or otherwise “Unsupported” if the corpus contradicts—or otherwise fails to support—this claim. Alongside this binary label, the model must extract facts (i.e., sentences) that justify its prediction. In particular, HoVer contains two-, three-, and four-hop claims that require fact from 2–4 different Wikipedia pages, and the model must find and extract one or more supporting facts from *each* of these pages for every claim. The results of the authors [Jiang et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib9) indicate that it is this retrieval of relevant passages and extraction of facts that poses the largest challenge in HoVer. The example we reviewed in §[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval") is a three-hop claim.

### 4.2 Baleen Implementation

We implement Baleen using Python 3.7 and Pytorch 1.6 and rely extensively on the HuggingFace Transformers library [Wolf et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib23). Our implementation of FliBERT is an extension of the ColBERT [Khattab and Zaharia (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib12) open-source code,55 5 <https://github.com/stanford-futuredata/ColBERT> where we primarily modify the retrieval modeling components.

For FliBERT, we fine-tune a BERT-base model, initialized from a ColBERT model previously trained on the MS MARCO Passage Ranking task [Nguyen et al. (2016)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#bib.bib15)). We train FliBERT in two iterative supervision rounds for each hop, and use a different FliBERT model for the first, second, and third hops. During inference, we restrict the retrieval of the second-round FliBERT models (for second- and third-hop retrieval) to the top-1000 results of the first-round models. The condensers read the top-20 passages of FliBERT in each hop, for a total of 60 passages for the full three-hop Baleen setting. For the two-stage condenser, we train two ELECTRA-large models, one per stage.

We show Baleen’s performance after one, two, and three hops—where Baleen’s FliBERT retrieves 100, 50+50, and 34+33+33 passages in each hop, respectively. As Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.00436v1#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.3 Reader: Task-Specific Processing ‣ 3 Baleen ‣ Baleen: Robust Multi-Hop Reasoning at Scale via Condensed Retrieval") shows, Baleen achieves strong results, outperforming the official baseline model by 46.2 points in Retrieval@100, 47.3 points in Passage EM, and 28.3 points in Passage F1. ## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we propose Baleen, a robust and scalable system for multi-hop reasoning. Baleen tackles the complexity of multi-hop queries with the *focused late interaction* mechanism for retrieval. Further, it mitigates the exponential search space problem by employing an aggressive *condensed retrieval* pipeline in every hop, which uses high-recall retrieval and consolidates the pertinent information retrieved throughout the current and previous hops into a relatively short context while preserving the high recall. Moreover, Baleen deals with the difficulty of ordering passages for multi-hop supervision via *latent hop ordering*. By employing a strong retriever, incorporating a condenser, and avoiding brittle heuristics, Baleen can robustly learn from limited training signal. We evaluated Baleen on the many-hop HoVer claim verification dataset and found that it greatly outperforms the baseline models.
