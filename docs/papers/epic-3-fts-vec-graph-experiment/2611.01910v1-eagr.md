**title**: EAGR: Evidence-Guided Adaptive Graph Retrieval

**author**: \\textbf{Sonam Gupta}\\textsuperscript{1}\\thanks{Corresponding author.},

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for multi-hop reasoning requires retrieval to adapt as intermediate evidence reveals new information needs. Existing approaches support such retrieval through explicit graph structures or iterative query reformulation, introducing graph-construction or query-time overhead. We present Evidence-Guided Adaptive Graph Retrieval (EAGR), a framework in which accumulated evidence dynamically determines which semantic directions to explore next, and which requires no dedicated graph database and no explicit graph materialization. EAGR operates over an implicit semantic graph induced by compact passage metadata pre-ingested into a standard VectorDB. Starting from dense-retrieval seed passages, an LLM iteratively assesses accumulated evidence, identifies promising semantic directions, expands the retrieval frontier through metadata-based neighborhood lookup, and terminates once sufficient evidence has been collected. By precomputing semantic metadata, EAGR enables evidence-conditioned graph exploration without materializing an explicit graph, maintaining a dedicated GraphDB, or iteratively rewriting the query. Across three widely used multi-hop QA benchmarks, EAGR achieves the highest LLM-based answer accuracy among evaluated methods, outperforming the vanilla RAG by 17% and strongest graph-aware baseline by 4% percentage points averaged across three popular benchmarks HotPotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and MuSiQue.

# Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounds Large Language Models (LLMs) in external knowledge\~lewis2020rag. Conventional RAG retrieves a fixed set of passages using dense similarity and delegates reasoning to the LLM\~karpukhin2020dpr. This works for localized evidence, but often struggles when an answer requires combining information across documents through dependent retrieval steps\~xiong2021mdr,trivedi2023ircot. Such questions require retrieval to evolve as evidence accumulates.

The full retrieval path is rarely apparent from the original query. For example, a question about a person’s *mother-in-law* may require first finding a passage about their *spouse*, then using that evidence to retrieve information about the *mother*. The next useful retrieval direction thus emerges from intermediate evidence, rather than from the query alone.

Recent work tackles multi-hop retrieval using graph-based structures, including hierarchical summary graphs, knowledge graphs, associative memory graphs, and relation-free passage graphs\~graphrag,gutierrez2024hipporag,gutierrez2025hipporag2,memgraphrag,wang2024kgp,li2025gnnret. These methods demonstrate the value of semantic connectivity, but commonly add a graph representation or indexing stage to the passage index. Another line of work treats retrieval over explicit knowledge graphs as a planning problem. Systems such as StructGPT, Think-on-Graph, and Plan-on-Graph use LLMs to explore graph neighborhoods and refine their decisions\~structgpt,tog,pog. They assume a symbolic graph whose entities and relations are known *a priori*.

[li2026s2g]

We study a different setting: evidence-conditioned traversal over unstructured passages\~li2026s2g. Enterprise VectorDBs typically store passages and metadata, not explicit symbolic graphs. We derive passage connections from shared keyword and semantic descriptors in the existing index, then use metadata lookups to retrieve connected passages. This enables GraphRAG-style traversal without a separate graph store or a separately materialized graph. The central challenge is to discover which semantic directions to explore as evidence accumulates, rather than to execute a plan over a known graph.

[pog]

[logicrag]

We propose **Evidence-Guided Adaptive Graph Retrieval (EAGR)**, which views retrieval as an adaptive search process. Each passage is associated with LLM-generated semantic descriptors stored as metadata; shared descriptors induce implicit connections without explicit graph edges. Retrieval starts with dense-retrieved seed passages. At each iteration, an LLM assesses the accumulated evidence, selects promising directions, and expands the retrieval frontier through metadata-based neighborhood lookup. Unlike planning-based approaches, EAGR neither executes an explicit reasoning plan\~pog,logicrag nor repeatedly rewrites the query: its traversal adapts to the evidence gathered so far.

Our contributions are:

1. **Evidence-guided adaptive retrieval.** We formulate retrieval decisions around both the original query and accumulated evidence, enabling iterative LLM-guided exploration without repeated query rewriting.
2. **GraphRAG over a standard VectorDB.** We derive implicit passage connections from LLM-generated semantic descriptors stored as metadata, enabling neighborhood lookup without materialized graph edges or a separate graph store.
3. **EAGR traversal strategy.** We introduce an iterative procedure that selects semantic directions, expands the retrieval frontier, and stops when sufficient supporting context has been collected.

**Overview of EAGR. ## Problem Formulation

sec:problem_formulation

Let $\mathcal{C}=\{p_1,\ldots,p_N\}$ denote a corpus of passages and

$q$ denote an information-seeking query. Standard retrieval in RAG systems

typically formulates retrieval as a mapping from the query to a ranked set of

passages,

$$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{std}}(q,\mathcal{C}) \rightarrow \mathcal{P},$$

where $\mathcal{P}$ denotes the retrieved passages. Under this formulation,

the query serves as the primary conditioning signal throughout retrieval.

For multi-hop queries, however, the evidence needed to answer $q$ is spread across several passages, and what earlier retrieval uncovers often determines what must be retrieved next. For example, the passage that identifies a film’s director is what reveals which biography to retrieve. Query-only retrieval does not model this evolving context.

We therefore treat {adaptive retrieval as a first-class retrieval

problem}, in which each retrieval step is conditioned jointly on the original

query $q$ and the evidence accumulated so far. Let $\mathcal{M}_t$ denote the

evidence accumulated after $t$ retrieval iterations. The passages retrieved

next depend on both:

$$\mathcal{P}_{t+1} = \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{adapt}}(q,\mathcal{M}_t,\mathcal{C}),$$

$$\mathcal{M}_{t+1} = \mathcal{M}_t \cup \operatorname{Evidence}(\mathcal{P}_{t+1}),$$

where $\operatorname{Evidence}(\cdot)$ extracts the facts in the newly

retrieved passages that bear on $q$. Retrieval thus alternates between

retrieving passages and updating the evidence state, and

$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{adapt}}$ need not search all of $\mathcal{C}$ at every

step: in EAGR it searches only the neighbourhood of the passages explored so

far.

The objective is to acquire sufficient evidence to answer $q$ while

avoiding unnecessary retrieval. Given a retrieval budget $B$, defined as the

maximum number of nodes that may be explored, the process terminates when the

accumulated evidence is sufficient to answer $q$, or when the

[yao2023react]

exploration budget is exhausted, and returns a relevance-based reordered list of passages. Unlike iterative methods yao2023react that issue new model-generated queries at each step, our formulation keeps the

original query $q$ fixed throughout retrieval. Adaptation happens instead

through the evolving evidence state $\mathcal{M}_t$, which determines what

to seek next without altering the original information need.

In the following sections, we instantiate this formulation through an

[sec:offline_gc]

[sec:eagr]

implicit semantic graph abstraction sec:offline_gc and an evidence-guided adaptive graph retrieval procedure sec:eagr.

## Offline Stage: Implicit Graph Construction (Figure\~fig:eagra)

sec:offline_gc

To enable adaptive retrieval beyond the initial set of retrieved passages,

we introduce an *implicit semantic graph abstraction* over the corpus.

Rather than explicitly constructing and storing a graph, we attach to each passage $p_i \in \mathcal{C}$ a set of semantic descriptors $K_i = \{k_{i1}, \ldots, k_{im_i}\}$, the entities and key concepts it mentions, and store them as metadata alongside the passage in the VectorDB. Each passage is thus represented as $v_i = (p_i, K_i)$.

Two passages are connected if they share a

[wang2024kgp]

[li2025gnnret]

descriptor\~wang2024kgp,li2025gnnret, which defines the implicit graph

$\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{E})$ with

$$(p_i,p_j) \in \mathcal{E} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad K_i \cap K_j \neq \emptyset .$$

$\mathcal{G}$ is never materialized: EAGR stores no edges, triples,

hierarchical summaries or auxiliary graph index. Instead, the neighbourhood associated with a selected set of descriptors (K’) is retrieved at query time through a metadata lookup in the VectorDB,

$$\mathcal{N}(K') = \{\, p_i \in \mathcal{C} \mid K_i \cap K' \neq \emptyset \,\},$$

[app:graph-construction]

where descriptors are matched as phrases (Appendix\~app:graph-construction).

The graph is therefore carried entirely by passage metadata, so graph-based

retrieval needs no separate graph construction, storage or indexing pipeline.

This simplicity is *architectural* rather than computational: EAGR still

issues LLM calls for offline descriptor extraction and online traversal, which

[sec:results]

we profile in Section\~sec:results.

#### Descriptor Extraction and Adjacency.

A single LLM call per passage extracts the named entities it mentions (people,

organizations, locations, titles of works, events, products) and a few key

concepts. Descriptors are lowercased, stripped of accents and punctuation, and

kept only if they occur in the passage, which removes hallucinated

descriptors. Dates are not used as descriptors, since they connect many

unrelated passages. Finally, because the same person is often named

differently across passages (e.g., *Clarence Brown* in a film’s passage

and *Clarence Leon Brown* in his biography), a corpus-level step links

shorter forms of a person’s name to the full name, so that such passages

[app:graph-construction]

become neighbours. Appendix\~app:graph-construction gives the prompt and

the complete procedure.

## Online Stage: Evidence-Guided Frontier Search (Figure\~fig:overviewb)

sec:eagr

Given a query $q$, EAGR first retrieves the top-$k$ passages by dense passage

retrieval (DPR). Each passage is represented as $v_i=(p_i,K_i)$, with content

$p_i$ and semantic descriptors $K_i$. These seed passages form the initial

frontier $V_0$, and the accumulated evidence is initialized as

$\mathcal{M}_0=\emptyset$. EAGR then proceeds frontier by frontier: it

assesses every node of the current frontier $V_t$, adding the evidence they

contain to $\mathcal{M}_t$, and uses this evidence to decide whether to stop

and, if not, which directions of the implicit graph to explore next. Each

iteration comprises five stages: *Node Assessment and Evidence Accumulation*, {Edge

Selection}, *Sufficiency Check*, {Evidence-Guided Edge

Reranking} and *Graph Expansion*. Node Assessment and Edge Selection

are performed jointly in a single LLM call per node; the prompts are given in

[app:prompts]

Appendix\~app:prompts.

#### Node Assessment and Evidence Accumulation.

Nodes of the frontier are assessed in order of their similarity to $q$. For

each node $v_i$, EAGR decides whether it is relevant to the query and

extracts the evidence it contributes, conditioned on the query and the

evidence accumulated so far:

$$(r_i,E_i) = \operatorname{Assess}(q,v_i,\mathcal{M}_t).$$

A node is relevant ($r_i=1$) if it helps answer any step of the query: it

states information the query asks for, or identifies an entity on the path to

the answer, such as the director of a film named in the query. It need not

contain the final answer. $E_i$ is the set of

facts from $p_i$ that fill a step of the query, each written as a short,

self-contained sentence; facts about the same entities that would not change

the answer are left out. Since the LLM may introduce information that is not

in the passage, a fact is kept only if it is grounded in $p_i$, and facts

[app:prompts]

already in $\mathcal{M}_t$ are skipped (Appendix\~app:prompts). The

evidence of relevant nodes is accumulated,

$$\mathcal{M}_t \leftarrow \mathcal{M}_t \cup E_i \quad \text{if } r_i=1,$$

so each subsequent node, within the same frontier or a later one, is assessed

against all evidence found so far. Irrelevant nodes contribute neither

evidence nor directions for exploration.

#### Edge Selection.

For each relevant node $v_i=(p_i,K_i)$, EAGR considers its unvisited

descriptors as candidate directions for further exploration and selects those

most likely to lead to evidence that is still missing, conditioned on the

query, the current passage and the accumulated evidence:

$$K_i' = \operatorname{Select}(q,v_i,K_i,\mathcal{M}_t), \qquad K_i' \subseteq K_i .$$

Descriptors are thus not chosen solely for their association with the current

passage; their utility is judged against what the query still requires given

the evidence gathered so far. Each descriptor in $K_i'$ defines a direction of

traversal, with the passages sharing it forming candidate neighbours in the

implicit graph. By selecting directions rather than exploring all descriptors

in $K_i$, EAGR focuses expansion on connections expected to yield useful

evidence.

Unlike methods that traverse a knowledge graph through predefined entities

and relations, EAGR selects edges using the full content of the current

passage together with the accumulated evidence. This lets it distinguish

directions that are structurally connected but differ substantially in their

usefulness for the query.

#### Sufficiency Check.

Once all nodes of the frontier have been assessed, and if they added new

evidence, EAGR checks whether $\mathcal{M}_t$ is sufficient to answer $q$:

$$s_t = \operatorname{Sufficient}(q,\mathcal{M}_t).$$

Rather than asking for a bare yes/no judgement, which we found prone to

premature termination, the LLM writes out the chain of steps the query

requires, each as an (*entity*, *attribute*, *value*) triple

with the value taken from $\mathcal{M}_t$, or left empty if the evidence does

not state it. For example, for {“Which team did Joe Buck’s father

broadcast for?”}, the chain is (Joe Buck, father, Jack Buck) followed by

(Jack Buck, team, St.\\ Louis Cardinals). EAGR accepts $s_t=1$ only if the LLM

judges the evidence sufficient, every value is filled, and every value

appears in $\mathcal{M}_t$, which rejects answers the LLM fills in from its

own knowledge. If $s_t=1$, retrieval terminates; otherwise EAGR continues with

the edges selected in this frontier. Retrieval also terminates when the

number of explored passages reaches the budget $B$.

#### Evidence-Guided Edge Reranking.

If the evidence is insufficient, EAGR pools the descriptors selected from relevant nodes on the current frontier into a candidate set:

$$\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{cand}} = \bigcup_{v_i \in \mathcal{V}_t} K_i',$$

where $\mathcal{V}_t \subseteq V_t$ denotes the relevant nodes of the

frontier. It then ranks these candidates by their usefulness for the query,

conditioned on the accumulated evidence:

$$K^{*} = \operatorname{Rank}\left(q,\mathcal{M}_t,\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{cand}}\right).$$

Unlike Edge Selection, which evaluates each node’s descriptors independently in its local context, reranking compares candidates proposed by different nodes against the same accumulated evidence. This enables EAGR to prioritize directions that address unresolved evidence gaps and adapt its focus as new evidence is gathered.

#### Graph Expansion.

Given the ranked descriptors $K^{*}$, EAGR materializes the corresponding

neighbourhood $\mathcal{N}(K^{*})$ through a metadata lookup in the VectorDB

[sec:offline_gc]

(sec:offline_gc), retrieving up to $N_{\max}$ passages per descriptor.

Already visited passages are excluded, and the remaining passages form the

next frontier $V_{t+1}$, ordered by the rank of the descriptor that reached

them; since the budget may run out partway through a frontier, higher-ranked

directions are explored first. Implementation details of candidate edges,

such as following other spellings of a selected name, are given in

[app:prompts]

Appendix\~app:prompts. The process then iterates, so that the evidence

accumulated at each step shapes the directions explored next, without an

explicit graph ever being constructed or stored.

#### Termination and output.

Traversal stops when the Sufficiency Check succeeds or when the number of

explored passages reaches the budget $B$. The number of passages explored

therefore varies across queries: EAGR retrieves only as much additional

evidence as each query needs, within a fixed upper bound on retrieval cost.

The explored passages are then ranked as described in

## Retrieval Reordering

sec:retrieval_reordering

After traversal terminates, EAGR orders the explored passages using the

evidence collected along the way, in three groups:

(i)\~relevant passages whose extracted facts mention the entities or values in

the reasoning chain of the last Sufficiency Check, i.e., the evidence for the

answer;

(ii)\~other relevant passages that contributed facts; and

(iii)\~all remaining passages, with those judged irrelevant last.

Within each group, passages keep their order from the traversal.

This ordering matters most for multi-hop queries, where passages reached

after one or more hops (e.g., the biography of a film’s director) are needed

to establish the answer but may share little vocabulary with the query.

Ranked by query similarity alone, such passages would often fall outside the

top-ranked results; ranking by the evidence chain instead places them first,

while passages judged irrelevant are moved to the end.

# Results and Analysis

sec:results

#### Generation accuracy (Q1).

[tab:main_results]

Table\~tab:main_results shows that EAGR achieves the highest average

accuracy, 67.1 LLM-Acc., 3.9 points above the strongest baseline (LinearRAG,

63.2) and 7.3 above vanilla RAG. Its main strength is consistency: the

strongest graph-based baselines each excel on some benchmarks but fall below

vanilla RAG on others (HippoRAG2 and LinearRAG on MuSiQue, LinearRAG also on

HotpotQA), whereas EAGR improves over vanilla RAG on all three. It matches

the best baseline on HotpotQA (77.4 vs.\\ 77.9) and 2Wiki (78.1 vs.\\ 78.2) and

gives the best result on MuSiQue (45.8 vs.\\ 44.2), the benchmark with the

longest reasoning chains. Giving the genertor the accumulated evidence instead of

passages raises MuSiQue accuracy further to 51.7, suggesting that the evidence

collected during traversal is a useful condensed context for long chains.

#### Retrieval (Q2).

[tab:retrieval_recall5_single]

Table\~tab:retrieval_recall5_single shows the largest retrieval gain on 2Wiki, where EAGR

reaches 95.5 Recall@5 against 91.0 for HippoRAG2 and 76.7 for dense

retrieval; its questions mostly follow explicit entity chains (e.g., a film’s

director, then the director’s birthplace), which descriptor edges follow

directly. On HotpotQA, EAGR is on par with HippoRAG2 (95.4 vs.\\ 95.9). On

MuSiQue, its recall equals dense retrieval and trails HippoRAG2 (71.5 vs.\\

76.2), yet its answer accuracy is 7.1 points higher, indicating that recall of

[fig:early_stopping]

all gold passages alone does not fully determine downstream accuracy. Figure\~fig:early_stopping shows how early stopping criteria in EAGR makes a significant number of queries stop at less than 5 retrieved chunks, this filtering out noisy context and providing better QA accuracy.

#### Adaptive retrieval.

Because traversal stops once the accumulated evidence is verified to answer

the query, EAGR spends retrieval effort where it is needed

[fig:early_stopping]

(Figure\~fig:early_stopping). On HotpotQA and 2Wiki, about 81% of

queries stop within 10 passages, and on HotpotQA 71% need at most 5, i.e.,

the four seed passages and at most one more; only about 4% reach the budget.

MuSiQue, whose questions require up to four hops, shows a different profile:

about 46% of queries stop within 10 passages, while 31% explore the full

budget. The traversal thus scales with question difficulty rather than

retrieving a fixed number of passages per query, which keeps the average

cost low on benchmarks dominated by short reasoning chains.

## Ablation Study

sec:ablation

Each ablation removes one component while keeping the rest of the pipeline

[tab:ablation]

unchanged (Table\~tab:ablation). Two components matter consistently.

Removing accumulated evidence, so that assessment, edge selection and

reranking see only the query and the current passage and traversal never

stops early, lowers average accuracy by 4.3 points, and by 6.3 and 5.4 on 2Wiki

and MuSiQue: conditioning on evidence is the main source of EAGR’s gains.

Removing retrieval reordering costs 3.9 points (7.9 on 2Wiki), showing that

passages found through multi-hop expansion need to be ranked by the evidence

chain to reach the reader. Edge reranking has a small average effect (0.8) but

matters on MuSiQue (3.2), where longer chains make the order of exploration

more consequential. Relevance assessment has the smallest effect: it helps on

2Wiki (1.7) and is neutral on HotpotQA and MuSiQue.

Edge selection is the most critical component: following every descriptor of

a relevant passage instead of the selected ones lowers average accuracy by

23.3 points, below even vanilla RAG. Without selection, the budget is spent

within the first expansion on passages that share incidental descriptors

with the seeds, so the passages of later hops are rarely reached, and the

many loosely related passages explored compete with the actual evidence for

the top-ranked positions.

# Conclusion

sec:concl

We presented EAGR, an adaptive retrieval approach in which accumulated

evidence, rather than a rewritten query, determines which directions to

explore next. EAGR traverses an implicit graph induced from descriptors stored

as ordinary VectorDB metadata, selecting and ranking edges by the evidence

collected and stopping once a verified reasoning chain answers the query, all

without materializing a graph or rewriting the query. Across HotpotQA,

2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue, it achieves the highest average answer accuracy,

3.9 points above the strongest graph-based baseline, and is the only

graph-based method that improves over dense retrieval on all three

benchmarks. Ablations show that conditioning on accumulated evidence drives

these gains, supporting a view of multi-hop retrieval as an adaptive search process in which accumulated evidence and not the original query alone, determines how the retrieval frontier evolves.

# Limitations

All components of EAGR are currently used off-the-shelf without

task-specific training. Future work could explore component-specific

fine-tuning and stronger foundation models to further improve both retrieval

quality and practical viability.

# Appendix

## Evidence-Guided Adaptive Graph Retrieval (EAGR) Algorithm

[appendix:alg-eagr]

Algorithm\~appendix:alg-eagr provides the complete procedural description of

EAGR. The algorithm consists of an offline semantic graph abstraction stage

and an online evidence-guided retrieval stage. During the offline stage, each

passage is associated with a set of semantic descriptors $K_i$, which are

stored as metadata in the VectorDB. During inference, EAGR first retrieves

$k$ seed passages using DPR and initializes the retrieval queue and accumulated

evidence with these seeds and an empty evidence context, respectively.

The online retrieval procedure follows the four stages described in

[sec:eagr]

Section\~sec:eagr: Node Assessment, Edge Selection,

Evidence-Guided Edge Reranking, and Graph Expansion. Relevant but insufficient

nodes contribute evidence and selected semantic descriptors to the traversal,

while irrelevant nodes are excluded from further expansion. Candidate

descriptors proposed across the explored nodes are subsequently reranked using

the query and accumulated evidence, after which at most $N_{\max}$ neighboring

passages are retrieved through metadata lookup. Already explored nodes are

excluded from subsequent expansion.

The procedure terminates when a relevant node is assessed as sufficient or

when the exploration budget $B$ is exhausted. Finally, the retrieved passages

are reordered using the relevance decisions made during traversal, while

preserving their relative retrieval order.

# Implicit Graph Construction

app:graph-construction

This appendix specifies how descriptors are extracted and normalized and how

neighbourhoods are looked up, so that the offline stage can be reproduced

given the same LLM (temperature\~0).

#### Step 1: Descriptor extraction.

Each passage, prefixed with its title, is sent to

Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct with the prompt in

[fig:eagr-extraction-prompt]

Figure\~fig:eagr-extraction-prompt. The prompt asks for every named

entity and at most five key concepts, as a JSON array of typed items

(*person*, *organization*, *location*, *work*,

*event*, *product*, *other*, *concept*, *date*).

Names are requested in their most complete form in the text; partial names

and nationality adjectives are excluded, and no minimum count is imposed.

#### Step 2: Parsing, cleaning and grounding.

We parse the first well-formed JSON array in the response. If none is found

(e.g., a missing closing bracket, or a response cut off at the token limit),

the complete entity objects are read individually. Each name is lowercased, hyphens are replaced by spaces, accents are removed

and whitespace is collapsed. A name is kept only if it occurs as a whole-word

phrase in the passage (title included), compared after the same

normalization with punctuation removed; this discards names the LLM invented

or paraphrased. Duplicates are collapsed. Dates are kept in the passage

metadata but excluded from the descriptor set $K_i$.

#### Step 4: Person-name normalization.

Using the extracted entity types, we collect person names across the corpus and group them by last name and suffix (e.g., *Jr.*, *II*). Within each group, a name is considered a *shorter form* of another if its words occur in the same order in the longer name, with initials matching words that begin with the corresponding letter (e.g., *Clarence Brown* and *Clarence Leon Brown*; *Robert A. Stemmle* and *Robert Adolf Stemmle*). First names must match, suffixes must be identical, and the shorter name’s middle part must match the first middle part of the longer name. Thus, *George W. Bush* is not merged with *George H. W. Bush*. Names containing particles or epithets (e.g., *de*, *van*, *the*, *of*) are excluded from merging. If a shorter form matches at most three full names, all matching full names are added as descriptors to every passage containing that shorter form. If it matches more than three, it is considered ambiguous and left unlinked. Finally, single-word fragments of a person’s name (e.g., *Clarence*, *Brown*) are removed from passages containing the full name to prevent spurious connections between different people.

#### Step 4: Neighborhood lookup.

At query time, the neighbours along a selected descriptor $k$ are the

passages with at least one descriptor that contains $k$ as a contiguous word

sequence, matched case- and punctuation-insensitively by a phrase query on the

descriptor metadata. A descriptor therefore also reaches passages whose

descriptors extend it (e.g., *the goose woman* matches

*the goose woman remake*). Each lookup

returns at most $N_{\max}$ matching passages per descriptor, without further

ranking among the matches.

#### Implementation details.

- *Candidate edges.* The Edge Labels shown for a passage are its descriptors that have not yet been expanded. When a person’s name is selected, other spellings of the same name in that passage (e.g., *robert a.\\ stemmle* and *robert stemmle*) are expanded as well, since a neighbouring passage may use only one of them.
- *Parsing.* Selected labels are matched to the candidates ignoring case and whitespace, and labels that are not among the candidates are discarded. If the joint prompt’s output cannot be parsed, the passage contributes no evidence or edges, and its relevance is treated as unknown during reordering.
- *Fact grounding.* Since the LLM may introduce information that is not in the passage, a fact is kept only if every number in it appears in the passage and at least 60% of its content words do (words of five or more letters may match on their first five letters, so that, e.g., *married* matches *marriage*). A fact is skipped if an existing fact in $\mathcal{M}_t$ already states it, i.e., contains its numbers and at least 85% of its content words.
- *Sufficiency verification.* $s_t=1$ only if the LLM answers `Yes`, the chain has at least one step, and every value (every item of a list value) appears in $\mathcal{M}_t$: its numbers must match exactly, and either the whole value or all of its content words must occur.
- *Reranking fallback.* Labels missing from the reranker’s output are appended in their selection order; if the output cannot be parsed, the selection order is kept.
- *Evidence chain for reordering.* A passage supports the reasoning chain if one of its facts contains a chain entity or value as a phrase, or contains all its numbers and at least 75% of its content words.
