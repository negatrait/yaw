###### Abstract.

This paper is about the construction of displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph. Our approach is based on the approximation of any optimal transport problem whose cost function is a distance on a discrete graph by a sequence of Schrödinger problems associated with random walks whose jump frequencies tend down to zero. Displacement interpolations are defined as the limit of the time-marginal flows of the solutions to the Schrödinger problems.
This allows to work with these interpolations by doing stochastic calculus on the approximating random walks which are regular objects, and then to pass to the limit in a slowing down procedure.
The main convergence results are based on $\Gamma$-convergence of entropy minimization problems.

As a by-product, we obtain new results about optimal transport on graphs.

###### Key words and phrases:

Displacement interpolation, discrete metric graph, optimal transport, Schrödinger problem, random walks, entropy minimization, $\Gamma$-convergence.

## Introduction

Displacement interpolations on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ were introduced by McCann in [[McC94](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx18)] and extended later to a geodesic space $(\mathcal{X},d)$ where they are defined as minimizing geodesics on the space of all probability measures on $\mathcal{X}$ equipped with the Wasserstein pseudo-distance of order two. They appeared to be a basic and essential notion of the Lott-Sturm-Villani theory of lower bounded curvature of geodesic spaces, see [[LV09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx16), [Stu06a](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx26), [Stu06b](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx27), [Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx29)]. Indeed, as discovered by Otto, Villani [[OV00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx22)], Cordero-Erausquin, McCann, Schmuckenschläger [[CEMS01](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx3)], Sturm and von Renesse in [[Sv05](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx28)] in the Riemannian setting, lower bounded curvature is intimately linked to convexity properties of the relative entropy with respect to the volume measure along displacement interpolations. It happens that these convexity properties admit natural analogues on a geodesic space.

It is tempting to try to implement a similar approach in a discrete setting. But,
little is known in this case since a discrete space fails to be a length space. Indeed, any regular enough path on a discrete space is piecewise constant with instantaneous jumps, so that no speed and a fortiori no constant speed geodesic exist.

This paper is about the construction of displacement interpolations on a discrete11 1 The epithet *discrete* is important since the standard definition of a (non-discrete) metric graph allows for continuous mass transfer along the edges. In the present paper, only pure jumps occur. metric graph. It permits us to propose, in this discrete setting, natural substitutes for the constant speed geodesics. As a by-product of our approach, we also obtain new results about the optimal transport on a graph.

To recover some time regularity of the paths without allowing any mass transfer along the edges of a graph, one is enforced to do some averaging on ensembles of discontinuous sample paths. This means that, for defining instantaneous speeds and accelerations, one is obliged to consider expected values of random walks. So doing, one lifts the paths on the discrete state space $\mathcal{X}$ up to the continuous space $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ of probability measures on $\mathcal{X}.$ This lifting from $\mathcal{X}$ to $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ has already been successfully used in the Lott-Sturm-Villani theory where minimizing geodesics on the length space $\mathcal{X}$ are embedded in the set of all minimizing geodesics on the Wasserstein space of order two $(\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),W_{2})$, i.e. $W_{2}$-displacement interpolations. As usual, the pseudo-distance $W_{2}$ is defined as the square root of the optimal value of a transport problem with cost function $d^{2},$ the square of a distance $d$ on $\mathcal{X}$.

The author already proposed in [[Léo12a](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx13)] a construction of the $W_{2}$-displacement interpolations in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ as limits of solutions of Schrödinger problems when the reference processes are slowed down to a no-motion process. In the present paper, we stay as close as possible to this strategy. It will lead us to a natural notion of $W_{1}$-displacement interpolation on a discrete metric graph.
The main idea lies in the following thought experiment.

*The lazy gas experiment*.
Suppose you observe at time $t=0$ a large collection of particles that are distributed with a profile close to the probability measure $\mu_{0}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ on the state space $\mathcal{X}.$ As in the thought experiment proposed by Schrödinger in 1931 [[Sch31](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx24), [Sch32](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx25)] and also described in Villani’s textbook [[Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx29), p. 445], ask them to rearrange into a new profile close to some $\mu_{1}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ at some later time $t=1.$ Since the particles are able to create mutual optimality (Gibbs conditioning principle), they will find an optimal transference plan between the endpoint profiles $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}.$ Now, suppose in addition that the typical speed of these particles is close to zero (the particles are lazy), then each particle will decide to travel at the lowest possible cost, meaning that it chooses an almost *geodesic* path. Indeed, with a very high probability each particle is very slow, so that it is typically expected that its final position is close to its initial one. But it is required by the optimal transference plan that it must reach a distant final position. Hence, *conditionally on the event that this target is finally attained*, each particle follows an almost geodesic path with a high probability.

With this thought experiment in mind, one can guess that a slowing down procedure enforces the appearance of individual geodesics. A displacement interpolation is a mixture of these geodesics which is specified by some optimal transport plan between the endpoint profiles $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}.$ We will see that displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d)$ are minimizing geodesics on the Wasserstein space of order one $(\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),W_{1})$ where the pseudo-distance $W_{1}$ on $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ is defined as the optimal value of a transport problem with the distance $d$ as its cost function.

### Notation

Before going on we need some general notation.
We denote by $\mathrm{P}(Y)$ and $\mathrm{M}_{+}(Y)$ the sets of all probability and positive measures on a measurable set $Y.$

Let $\Omega\subset\mathcal{X}^{{[0,1]}}$ be a set of paths from the time interval ${[0,1]}$ to the measurable state space $\mathcal{X}.$ The canonical process $X=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ is defined for all $\omega=(\omega_{s})_{0\leq s\leq 1}\in\Omega$ by $X_{t}(\omega)=\omega_{t}\in\mathcal{X}$ for each $0\leq t\leq 1$. The set $\Omega$ is endowed with the $\sigma$-field generated by $(X_{t};t\in{[0,1]}).$ For any $t\in{[0,1]}$ and any $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega),$ the push-forward

|  | $Q_{t}:=(X_{t})_{\#}Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ |  | (0.1) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

of $Q$ by the measurable mapping $X_{t}$ is the law of the random position $X_{t}$ at time $t$ if $Q$ describes the behaviour of the random path. More specifically $Q_{0}$, $Q_{1}$ are the initial and final time-marginal projections of $Q.$ Also

|  | $Q_{01}:=(X_{0},X_{1})_{\#}Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ |  | (0.2) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

is the joint law of the random endpoint position $(X_{0},X_{1}).$ The $xy$-bridge of $Q$ is the conditional probability measure

|  | $Q^{xy}:=Q(\cdot&#124;X_{0}=x,X_{1}=y)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{X}.$ |  | (0.3) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

As a general result, we have the disintegration formula

|  | $Q(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}Q^{xy}(\cdot)\,Q_{01}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

If $P$ is a probability measure on $\Omega,$ we sometimes use the probabilistic convention: $E_{P}(u):=\int_{\Omega}u\,dP.$

### Implementing the slowing down procedure

As a consequence of the large deviation theory,
the mathematical translation of the lazy gas experiment is in terms of some entropy minimization problems: the Schrödinger problems. More precisely, we are going to investigate the limit as a slowing down parameter $k$ tends to infinity (for instance, the average number of jumps of each individual particle is of order $1/k$) of the sequence of minimizing problems

|  | $H(P&#124;R^{k})\rightarrow\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where the relative entropy of a probability measure $p$ with respect to a reference measure $r$ is defined by $H(p&#124;r):=\int\log(dp/dr)\,dp,$ $R^{k}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ describes the random behaviour of a slow walker (a single typical particle) on the graph and $P$ is the unknown path probability measure which is subject to have initial and final prescribed marginal measures: $P_{0}=\mu_{0}$ and $P_{1}=\mu_{1}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),$ see notation ([0.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#Sx1.E1 "In Notation ‣ Introduction ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

By means of $\Gamma$-convergence, we shall prove that the unique minimizer $\widehat{P}^{k}$ of this entropy problem admits a limit $\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{P}^{k}=\widehat{P}$ in $\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ that will be characterized as a specific solution of a new auxiliary variational problem. It is the law of a random walk whose sample paths are piecewise constant geodesics with respect to a given distance $d$ on the graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim)$. Moreover, the joint law $\widehat{P}_{01}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$, see notation ([0.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#Sx1.E2 "In Notation ‣ Introduction ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), of the couple of its endpoint positions under $\widehat{P}$ is a singled out solution of the Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem

|  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(x,y)\,\pi(dxdy)\rightarrow\textrm{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}),\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\pi_{1}=\mu_{1},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\pi_{0}$ and $\pi_{1}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ are the first and second marginals of $\pi.$ The optimal value of this problem is the Wasserstein distance of order one$W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}).$ This specific random walk $\widehat{P}$ is a geodesic bridge between $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}.$ We shall call it a *displacement interpolation random walk* and its time-marginal flow $(\widehat{P}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})^{{[0,1]}}$, see notation ([0.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#Sx1.E1 "In Notation ‣ Introduction ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), will be defined as a *displacement interpolation* between $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}.$ This definition will be justified because of several analogies with the standard displacement interpolations on a geodesic space.

### A discrete metric measure graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d,m)$

Let us take a distance $d$ on $\mathcal{X}$ which is compatible with the graph structure $\sim$, in the sense of Hypothesis [2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E1 "Hypotheses 2.1. ‣ The underlying hypotheses ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")-($d$), and a possibly unbounded positive measure $m$ on $\mathcal{X}.$ It will be shown that the sequence of reference Markov random walks $(R^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ can be chosen such that for any $0\leq t\leq 1$ and any $k\geq 1,$ the $t$-marginal $R^{k}_{t}=m$, the Markov generator $L^{k}_{t}$ is self-adjoint in $L^{2}(\mathcal{X},m)$ and the limiting displacement interpolation $\mu_{t}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{P}_{t}^{k}$ is a minimizing constant speed geodesic in $(\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),W_{1})$ where the Wasserstein distance $W_{1}$ is built upon the distance $d$.

Although what follows is not treated in the present paper, it seems plausible that, in the perspective of deriving displacement convexity properties of the entropy, it is worthwhile studying the functions $t\in{[0,1]}\mapsto H\big((X_{t})_{\#}\widehat{P}^{k}&#124;(X_{t})_{\#}R^{k}\big)=H(\widehat{P}^{k}_{t}&#124;m)$ since the dynamics of $R^{k}$ and $\widehat{P}^{k}$ are well understood, see [[Léob](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx12)], and $\lim_{k\to\infty}H(\widehat{P}^{k}_{t}&#124;m)=H(\mu_{t}&#124;m).$ This will be explored elsewhere.

### Alternate approaches

Let us write a few words about already existing strategies in view of deriving displacement convexity properties of the entropy on a discrete space.

#### Midpoint interpolations

In the special case of the hypercube $\mathcal{X}=\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}$ equipped the Hamming distance, Ollivier and Villani [[OV12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx23)] have introduced the most natural interpolation $(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ between $\delta_{x}$ and $\delta_{y}$ which is defined as follows. For any $0\leq t\leq 1,$ $\mu_{t}$ is the uniform probability measure on the set of all $t$-midpoints of $x$ and $y.$ In this paper, the authors announce that it seems to be difficult to obtain displacement convexity of the entropy along these interpolations. However, they prove a Brunn-Minkowski inequality for the counting measure under the restriction $t=1/2$ which allows for explicit combinatoric computations.

#### Approximate midpoint interpolations

Bonciocat and Sturm [[BS09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx2)] have introduced $h$-approximate midpoint interpolations and a natural extension of the lower bounded curvature of geodesic spaces, again in terms of displacement convexity of the relative entropy. They show that the $h$-discretized graph of a geodesic space with a curvature lower bound $K$ has a lower $h$-curvature bound which converges to $K$ as the discretization mesh $h$ tends to zero. They also compute $h$-curvature lower bounds of some planar graphs.

#### Maas-Mielke gradient flow

Recently, Maas [[Maa11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx17)] and Mielke [[Mie11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx20)] designed a new distance $\mathcal{W}$ on $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ which, unlike $W_{2}$, allows for regarding evolution equations of reversible Markov chains on the discrete space $\mathcal{X}$ as gradient flows of the entropy $H(\cdot&#124;m)$ on $(\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{W}),$ where $m$ is the reversing measure of the Markov chain. Soon after, following the Lott-Sturm-Villani strategy, Maas and Erbar [[EM12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx5)] and Mielke [[Mie](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx19)] applied this gradient flow approach to obtain convexity properties of relative entropies in $(\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{W})$ leading to new interesting results about lower bounded curvature on discrete spaces.

The distance $\mathcal{W}$ is a Riemannian distance that is obtained by plugging discrete objects, such as a discrete gradient and a discrete divergence, into the standard Benamou-Brenier formula, see ([1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E4 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")). It is not clear that $\mathcal{W}$ and the corresponding minimizing geodesics on $(\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{W})$ are associated with an optimal transport problem related to some distance $d$ on $\mathcal{X}$. Hence, although this approach is respectful of the measure space structure $(\mathcal{X},m)$, it might not be linked to a *metric* structure $(\mathcal{X},d).$ This still needs to be clarified if, starting from this Riemannian structure on $\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$, one wishes to define a notion of Ricci type curvature on $\mathcal{X}$ (which should be related to the variation of the discrete volume $m$ along “geodesics” on $(\mathcal{X},d)$) or even if one only wishes to obtain estimates on the concentration of the measure $m$ on $\mathcal{X}$ in terms of some distance $d$.

#### Binomial interpolations

Consider the set $\left\{0,\dots,n\right\}$ with the graph structure of $\mathbb{Z}.$ The binomial interpolation between $\delta_{0}$ and $\delta_{n}$ is defined for any $0\leq t\leq 1$ and $x\in\left\{0,\dots,n\right\}$ by $\mu_{t}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}n\\ x\end{pmatrix}t^{x}(1-t)^{n-x}.$ It means that $\mu_{t}=\mathcal{B}(n,t)$ is the binomial distribution with parameters $n$ and $t$. This interpolation was introduced by Johnson [[Joh07](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx10)] on $\mathbb{N}$ to obtain displacement convexity of the entropy with motivations slightly different from the Lott-Sturm-Villani theory. Now, on a graph equipped with the standard graph distance, a binomial interpolation between $\delta_{x}$ and $\delta_{y}$ is a mixture of the binomial interpolations along the geodesic chains $x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim\cdots\sim x_{d(x,y)}=y$ that connect $x$ and $y$. These interpolations allow for displacement convexity of the entropy with respect to the counting measure $m$. It has been successfully used to prove displacement convexity of the entropy by Hillion [[Hil](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx7), [Hil10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx8), [Hil12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx9)] on some trees and Gozlan, Roberto, Samson and Tetali [[GRST](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx6)] which have worked out the examples of the complete graph, $\mathbb{Z}^{n}$ and the hypercube.

It happens that binomial interpolations are specific instances of the displacement interpolations which are built in the present paper. They correspond to the simple random walk $R$ and the volume measure $m^{o}$, see ([A.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E3 "In Simple random walk ‣ Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and ([A.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E4 "In Simple random walk ‣ Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) at the appendix, with the standard graph distance.
It will be seen at Claim [3.17](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E17 "Claim 3.17. ‣ The graph ℤ ‣ Binomial interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") that they are closely related to bridges of the Poisson process.

## 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy

To stress the analogies between displacement interpolations in discrete and continuous settings, we first recall their main properties on a Riemannian manifold. Then, we briefly introduce the main properties of an object in a discrete setting, see ([1.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E6 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), which will be defined as a displacement interpolation because of the strong analogies between its properties and the corresponding properties of the displacement interpolations in a continuous setting, see Definitions [1.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E12 "Definitions 1.12. ‣ Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. | --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where we denote $\pi_{0}(dx):=\pi(dx\times\mathcal{X})$ and $\pi_{1}(dy):=\pi(\mathcal{X}\times dy)$ the marginals of $\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}).$ The Wasserstein pseudo-distance of order 2 is defined by

|  | $W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\sqrt{2\inf\eqref{eq-74}}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

|  | $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X}):=\left\{\mu\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X});\int_{\mathcal{X}}d^{2}(x_{o},x)\,\mu(dx)<\infty\right\}$ |  |

| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

of some solution $\widehat{P}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{\textrm{ac,2}})$ of the following dynamical version of ([MK2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex1 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")),

|  | $\int_{\Omega_{\textrm{ac,2}}}C_{\mathrm{kin}}(\omega)\,P(d\omega)\rightarrow\textrm{min};\quad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{\textrm{ac,2}}):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}$ |  | (MK${}_{\textrm{dyn},2}$) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ---------------------------------- |

where the kinetic action $C_{\mathrm{kin}}$ is defined by

|  | $C_{\mathrm{kin}}(\omega):=\int_{[0,1]}\frac{1}{2}&#124;\dot{\omega}_{t}&#124;_{\omega_{t}}^{2}\,dt\in[0,\infty],\quad\omega\in\Omega_{\textrm{ac,2}}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Suppose for simplicity that any solution $\pi^{*}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ of ([MK2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex1 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) gives a zero mass to the cut-locus so that there exists a unique minimizing geodesic $\gamma^{xy}\in\Omega_{\textrm{ac,2}}$ joining $x$ and $y$ for $\pi^{*}$-almost every $x,y\in\mathcal{X}.$ Then, any solution $\widehat{P}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{\textrm{ac,2}})$ of ([MK dyn , 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex5 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) is in one-one correspondence with a solution $\widehat{\pi}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ of ([MK2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex1 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) via the relation

|  | $\widehat{P}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\delta_{\gamma^{xy}}(\cdot)\,\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega_{\textrm{ac,2}})$ |  | (1.2) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where $\delta$ stands for a Dirac probability measure.
With ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E1 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), we see that the displacement interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ satisfies

|  | $\mu_{t}=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\delta_{\gamma^{xy}_{t}}(\cdot)\,\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),\quad t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  | (1.3) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

In particular, with $\mu_{0}=\delta_{x}$ and $\mu_{1}=\delta_{y},$ we see that $[\delta_{x},\delta_{y}]=(\delta_{\gamma^{xy}_{t}})_{t\in{[0,1]}}.$ This signifies that the notion of displacement interpolation lifts the notion of action minimizing geodesic from the manifold $\mathcal{X}$ onto the Wasserstein space $\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})$.

It follows from ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E1 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and ([MK dyn , 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex5 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) that $W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ admits the Benamou-Brenier representation [[BB00](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx1)]:

|  | $W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\inf_{(\nu,v)}\left\{\int_{[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}}&#124;v_{t}(x)&#124;^{2}_{x}\,\nu_{t}(dx)dt\right\}$ |  | (1.4) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where the infimum is taken over all regular enough $(\nu,v)$ such that $\nu=(\nu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}\in\mathrm{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})^{{[0,1]}},$ $v$ is a vector field and these quantities are linked by the following current equation (in a weak sense) with boundary values:

|  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\nu+\nabla\!\cdot\!(\nu\,v)=0,\quad t\in(0,1)\\ \nu_{0}=\mu_{0},\ \nu_{1}=\mu_{1}.\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

### Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d)$

The countable set $\mathcal{X}$ of vertices is endowed with a graph structure: $x\sim y$ means that $x\neq y$ and $\left\{x,y\right\}$ is an undirected edge. Let $\Omega\subset\mathcal{X}^{{[0,1]}}$ be the natural path space for a random walk on the graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim):$ $\Omega$ is the space of all left-limited, right-continuous, piecewise constant paths $\omega=(\omega_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ on $\mathcal{X}$ with finitely many jumps such that: $\forall t\in(0,1),\omega_{t^{-}}\neq\omega_{t}\Rightarrow\omega_{t^{-}}\sim\omega_{t}.$ The distance $d$ is in accordance with the graph structure, meaning that it is required to be intrinsic in the discrete sense, that is$d(x,y)=\inf\left\{\ell(\omega);\omega\in\Omega:\omega_{0}=x,\omega_{1}=y\right\},\ x,y\in\mathcal{X}$ with

|  | $\ell(\omega):=\sum_{0<t<1}d(\omega_{t^{-}},\omega_{t}),\quad\omega\in\Omega$ |  | (1.5) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

the discrete length of the discontinuous path $\omega$. As before, we are going to define the displacement interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]=(\mu_{t})_{t\in{[0,1]}}$ by formula ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E1 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")):

|  | $\mu_{t}:=\widehat{P}_{t},\quad 0\leq t\leq 1$ |  | (1.6) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where $\widehat{P}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ is some *singled out solution* of the following order-one analogue of ([MK dyn , 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex5 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")):

|  | $\int_{\Omega}\ell(\omega)\,P(d\omega)\to\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}.$ |  | (MK${}_{\textrm{dyn}}$) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ---------------------------------- |

The random walk $\widehat{P}$ minimizes the average length while transporting the mass distribution $\mu_{0}$ on $\mathcal{X}$ onto another mass distribution $\mu_{1}.$
What is meant by “singled out solution” when talking about $\widehat{P}$ will be made precise in a while. For the moment, let us say that $\widehat{P}$ is selected among the infinitely many – see ([1.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E7 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) below – solutions of ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex8 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), as being the limit of a sequence of solutions of entropy minimizing problems associated with the slowing down procedure that was invoked when describing the lazy gas experiment. See ([1.16](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E16 "In Optimal transport appears at the limit of the slowing down procedure ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) below.

Since $d$ is assumed to be intrinsic, we shall see that the push-forward of ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex8 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) onto $\mathcal{X}^{2}$ is the Monge-Kantorovich problem

|  | $\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(x,y)\,\pi(dxdy)\rightarrow\textrm{min};\qquad\pi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):\pi_{0}=\mu_{0},\pi_{1}=\mu_{1}.$ |  | (MK) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ---- |

It leads to the Wasserstein pseudo-distance of order one

|  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\inf\eqref{MK}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

which is a distance on

|  | $\mathrm{P}_{1}(\mathcal{X}):=\left\{\mu\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X});\int_{\mathcal{X}}d(x_{o},x)\,\mu(dx)<\infty\right\}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

For each $x,y\in\mathcal{X},$ let us denote

|  | $\Gamma^{xy}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega;\omega_{0}=x,\omega_{1}=y,\ell(\omega)=d(x,y)\right\}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

the set of all *geodesics* joining $x$ and $y$.
Remark that when $x$ and $y$ are distinct, $\Gamma^{xy}$ contains infinitely many paths since a geodesic joining $x$ and $y$ is characterized by the ordered sequence of its visited states, regardless of the instants of jumps. On the other hand,
it is easily seen that for any measurable *geodesic kernel* $(Q^{xy}\in\mathrm{P}(\Gamma^{xy});x,y\in\mathcal{X})$ and for any $\pi^{*}$ solution of ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex9 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")),

|  | $P^{*}(\cdot):=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}Q^{xy}(\cdot)\,\pi^{*}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ |  | (1.7) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

solves ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex8 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")). It follows that ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex8 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) admits infinitely many solutions and also that the static and dynamical Monge-Kantorovich problems have the same optimal value:

|  | $\inf\eqref{MK}=\inf\eqref{MKdyn}.$ |  | (1.8) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

The $(X_{t})_{t\in{[0,1]}}$-push forward of the minimizer $P^{*}$ given at ([1.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E7 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) is

|  | $P^{*}_{t}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}Q^{xy}_{t}(\cdot)\,\pi^{*}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.$ |  | (1.9) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

Remark that ([1.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E7 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"))-([1.9](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E9 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. The slowing down procedure selects one singled out geodesic kernel $(G^{xy}\in\mathrm{P}(\Gamma^{xy});x,y\in\mathcal{X})$ which does not depend on the specific choice of $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$, see Theorem [2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E7 "Theorem 2.7. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. |  | $\widehat{P}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}G^{xy}(\cdot)\,\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ |  | (1.10) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

and the displacement interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ satisfies

|  | $\mu_{t}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}G^{xy}_{t}(\cdot)\,\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}),\quad t\in{[0,1]}$ |  | (1.11) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

where for each $x,y\in\mathcal{X}$, $G_{t}^{xy}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ is the $t$-marginal of $G^{xy}\in\mathrm{P}(\Gamma^{xy}).$

It will be proved that $\widehat{P}$ and every $G^{xy}$ have the Markov property.

The defining identity $\mu_{t}:=\widehat{P}_{t},$ $t\in{[0,1]},$ states that $\widehat{P}$ is a dynamical coupling of $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}].$

In particular, we see that the deterministic behaviour of $\delta_{\gamma^{xy}}$ must be replaced with a genuinely random walk $G^{xy}.$

 *The geodesic kernel $(G^{xy}\in\mathrm{P}(\Gamma^{xy});x,y\in\mathcal{X})$ encodes some geodesic dynamics of the discrete metric graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d).$*

The analogy between ([1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E2 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) entitles us to propose the following definitions.

###### Definitions 1.12.

We call $\mu=(\widehat{P}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ the $(R,d)$-displacement interpolation and $\widehat{P}$ the $(R,d)$-displacement random walk between $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}.$
We denote $\mu=[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]^{(R,d)}$ or more simply $\mu=[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]^{R}$ or $\mu=[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ when the context is clear.

It follows from $\mu_{t}:=\widehat{P}_{t},0\leq t\leq 1,$ ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex8 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and the Markov property of $\widehat{P}$ that $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ admits the Benamou-Brenier type representation

|  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\inf_{(\nu,j)}\int_{[0,1]}dt\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\nu_{t}(dz)j_{t,z}(dw)<\infty$ |  | (1.13) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

where the infimum is taken over all couples $(\nu,j)$ such that $\nu=(\nu_{t})_{t\in{[0,1]}}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})^{{[0,1]}}$ is a time-differentiable flow of probability measures on $\mathcal{X},$ $j=(j_{t,z})_{t\in{[0,1]},z\in\mathcal{X}}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})^{{[0,1]}\times\mathcal{X}}$ is a measurable jump kernel, $\nu$ and $j$ are linked by the current equation

|  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\nu_{t}(z)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}[\nu_{t}(z)j_{t,z}(dw)-\nu_{t}(dw)j_{t,w}(z)]=0,\qquad 0<t<1,\ z\in\mathcal{X}\\ \nu_{0}=\mu_{0},\ \nu_{1}=\mu_{1}\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with $\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nu_{t}(z)j_{t,z}(dw)<\infty$ for all $0<t<1,\ z\in\mathcal{X}.$ The infimum$\inf_{(\nu,j)}$ is attained at $(\mu,\widehat{J})$ where $\mu$ is the displacement interpolation and $\widehat{J}$ is the jump kernel of the Markov displacement random walk $\widehat{P}$. Hence

|  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\int_{[0,1]}dt\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu_{t}(dz)\widehat{J}_{t,z}(dw).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

This is in complete analogy with the standard Benamou-Brenier formula ([1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E4 "In McCann displacement interpolations ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).
The detailed formulation of this Benamou-Brenier formula is given at Theorem [3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E3 "Theorem 3.3 (A Benamou-Brenier type formula). ‣ A Benamou-Brenier type formula ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs").

### The discrete metric measure graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d,m)$

Let us equip $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d)$ with a positive measure $m\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}).$ We introduce a sequence $R_{k}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ of *slowed down* continuous-time Markov random walks on $\mathcal{X}$ which is respectful of the graph and the metric measure structures of $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d,m)$.

For each $k\geq 1,$ the Markov generator $L^{k}=(L^{k}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ is defined for any finitely supported function $u$ by

|  | $L^{k}_{t}u(x)=\sum_{y:y\sim x}k^{-d(x,y)}J_{t,x}(y)[u(y)-u(x)],\quad t\in{[0,1]},x\in\mathcal{X},k\geq 1.$ |  | (1.14) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

In this formula $J_{t,x}(y)>0$ is the average rate of jumps of the random walk from $x$ to $y$ at time $t$ when $k=1$. For some details about random walks, see the appendix Section [A](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1 "Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs").

As $k$ increases, the sequence of generators $(L^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ tends down to zero. It describes the dynamics of slowed down random walks which ultimately do not move anymore. We call them *lazy random walks*.
For each $k$, the random walk with generator $L^{k}$ and initial measure $m\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ is described by a positive measure

|  | $R^{k}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

on the path space $\Omega$ (the letter $R$ stands for *reference* measure).

We also assume that the graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim)$ is irreducible and that the initial positive measure $m=(m_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}$ and the jump kernel $J$ satisfy the detailed balance conditions

|  | $m_{x}J_{t,x}(y)=m_{y}J_{t,y}(x),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  | (1.15) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

Together with the irreducibility assumption, these conditions imply that $m_{x}>0,\forall x\in\mathcal{X},$ whenever $m(\mathcal{X})>0.$ A large class of $m$-reversible random walks is described at the appendix Section [A](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1 "Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), see ([A.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E5 "In A generic class of 𝑚-reversible random walks ‣ Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

Let us comment on the connection between this sequence of lazy random walks and the metric graph measure structure of $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d,m).$

1. ($\sim$)

   As for each $k,t$ and $x$, the support of the jump measure $J_{t,x}=\sum_{y:y\sim x}J_{t,x}(y)\,\delta_{y}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ is the set of neighbours of $x$, the random walker which is governed by $R^{k}$ is allowed to jump from $x$ to $y$ if and only if $x$ and $y$ are neighbours.

2. ($m$)

   Because of ([1.15](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E15 "In The discrete metric measure graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑,𝑚) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), the Markov random walks $R^{k}$ are assumed to be $m$-stationary, i.e. for all $t\in{[0,1]}$ the $t$-marginal $R^{k}_{t}$ of $R^{k}$ satisfies $R^{k}_{t}=m$. Furthermore, for all $0\leq t\leq 1$ and $k\geq 1,$ the generators $L_{t}^{k}$ are self-adjoint on $L^{2}(\mathcal{X},m).$

3. ($d$)

   The connection with the distance $d$ is less immediate. For any $x,y\in\mathcal{X},$ we shall prove at Theorem [2.9](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E9 "Corollary 2.9. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") that the sequence of bridges $R^{k,xy}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ from $x$ to $y$ (see notation ([0.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#Sx1.E3 "In Notation ‣ Introduction ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"))) converges to a probability measure which is concentrated on the set $\Gamma^{xy}$ of all geodesics joining $x$ and $y.$

Therefore, in some sense, the sequence $(R^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ of lazy random walks is respectful of the discrete metric measure graph structure of $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d,m)$.

### Optimal transport appears at the limit of the slowing down procedure

Let us briefly sketch the connection between lazy random walks and optimal transport. The main idea is to consider, as in [[Léo12a](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx13)], a sequence of entropy minimizing problems which are called Schrödinger problems. This is the mathematical implementation of the lazy gas experiment that was described in the introduction. Details about the connection between the lazy gas experiment and the Schrödinger problem are given in the author’s survey paper [[Léob](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx12), §6].

Recall that the relative entropy of a probability measure $p$ on the measurable space $Y$ with respect to some reference positive measure $r\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(Y)$ on $Y$ is roughly defined by

|  | $H(p&#124;r):=\int_{Y}\log(dp/dr)\,dp\in(-\infty,\infty],\quad p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

if $p$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $r$ and $+\infty$ otherwise. For a rigorous definition, see the appendix Section [B](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A2 "Appendix B Relative entropy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"). For each $k\geq 2,$ consider the entropy minimizing problem that is called a dynamical *Schrödinger problem*

|  | $H(P&#124;R^{k})/\log k\rightarrow\textrm{min};\qquad P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}.$ |  | (S${}_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{k}$) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | --------------------------------- |

See [[Léob](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx12)] for more details about Schrödinger’s problem.
It should be compared with ([MK dyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex8 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")). ### A Benamou-Brenier type formula

Let us have a closer look at the Benamou-Brenier type formula ([1.13](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E13 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

###### Definition 3.2 (Change of time).

A change of time $\tau$ is an *absolutely continuous* function $\tau:{[0,1]}\to{[0,1]}$ such that $\tau(0)=0$, $\tau(1)=1$ and with a nonnegative generalized derivative $0\leq\dot{\tau}\in L^{1}({[0,1]}).$

For any change of time $\tau$ and any measure $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega),$ we denote

|  | $Q^{\tau}:=(X_{\tau})_{\#}Q$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $X_{\tau}(t):=X_{\tau(t)},t\in{[0,1]}.$ For any flow $\nu$ of probability measures and any jump kernel $\mathcal{J}$, we denote $\nu\mathcal{J}_{t}(dxdy):=\nu_{t}(dx)\mathcal{J}_{t,x}(dy)$ and $\nu\mathcal{J}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\nu_{t}(dx)\mathcal{J}_{t,x}(dy)$.

The following theorem is a consequence of the Markov property of $\widehat{P}$ which was stated at Theorem [2.11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E11 "Theorem 2.11 (The dynamics of 𝑃̂). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs").

###### Theorem 3.3 (A Benamou-Brenier type formula).

Suppose that the Hypotheses [2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E1 "Hypotheses 2.1. ‣ The underlying hypotheses ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") are satisfied.

1. (1)

   We have

    |  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\inf_{\nu,j}\int_{[0,1]}dt\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\nu j_{t}(dzdw)<\infty$ |  | (3.4) |
    | --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

   where the infimum is taken over all couples $(\nu,j)$ such that $\nu=(\nu_{t})_{t\in{[0,1]}}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})^{{[0,1]}}$ is a time-differentiable flow of probability measures on $\mathcal{X},$ $j=(j_{t,z})_{t\in{[0,1]},z\in\mathcal{X}}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})^{{[0,1]}\times\mathcal{X}}$ is a measurable jump kernel, $\nu$ and $j$ are linked by

    |  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\nu_{t}(z)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}[\nu_{t}(z)j_{t,z}(dw)-\nu_{t}(dw)j_{t,w}(z)]=0,\qquad 0<t<1,\ z\in\mathcal{X}\\ \nu_{0}=\mu_{0},\ \nu_{1}=\mu_{1}\end{array}\right.$ |  | (3.5) |
    | --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

   with $\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nu_{t}(z)j_{t,z}(dw)<\infty$ for all $0<t<1,\ z\in\mathcal{X}.$

2. (2)

   The infimum $\inf_{\nu,j}$ in ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E4 "In item 1 ‣ Theorem 3.3 (A Benamou-Brenier type formula). ‣ A Benamou-Brenier type formula ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) is attained at $(\mu,\widehat{J})$ where $\mu$ is the displacement interpolation and $\widehat{J}$ is the jump kernel of $\widehat{P}$. Hence

    |  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\int_{[0,1]}dt\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu\widehat{J}_{t}(dzdw).$ |  |
    | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

3. (3)

   For any change of time $\tau$, the infimum $\inf_{\nu,j}$ in ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E4 "In item 1 ‣ Theorem 3.3 (A Benamou-Brenier type formula). ‣ A Benamou-Brenier type formula ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) is also attained at $(\mu^{\tau},\widehat{J}^{\tau})$ where $\mu^{\tau}$ is the displacement interpolation and $\widehat{J}^{\tau}$ is the jump kernel that are associated with $\widehat{P}^{\tau}.$ Moreover, $\widehat{P}^{\tau}$ is the displacement random walk associated with $R^{\tau},$ i.e. the analogue of $\widehat{P}$ when $R$ is replaced with $R^{\tau}$ and

    |  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\int_{[0,1]}dt\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu\widehat{J}^{\tau}_{t}(dzdw)$ |  |
    | --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

   where for almost every $t\in{[0,1]}$, $\mu\widehat{J}_{t}^{\tau}:=\dot{\tau}(t)\mu\widehat{J}_{\tau(t)}$ is the mass displacement distribution of $\widehat{P}^{\tau}$ at time $t$.

###### Proof.

$\bullet$ Proof of (1) and (2).
With ([1.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E8 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and Theorem [2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E7 "Theorem 2.7. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")-(3), we have

|  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\inf\eqref{MK}=\inf\eqref{MKdyn}=E_{\widehat{P}}(\ell).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

But, for any Markov random walk $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ on $(\mathcal{X},\sim)$ with jump kernel $j$ and such that $E_{P}(\ell)<\infty,$ we have

|  | $\displaystyle E_{P}(\ell)=E_{P}\int_{[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}}d(X_{t},y)\,j_{t,X_{t}}(dy)dt=\int_{[0,1]}dt\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,P_{t}(dz)j_{t,z}(dw).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

This proves that $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\inf\left\{E_{P}(\ell);P\textrm{ Markov on }(\mathcal{X},\sim):P_{0}=\mu_{0},P_{1}=\mu_{1}\right\}=E_{\widehat{P}}(\ell),$ which is the announced result since $\nu_{t}:=P_{t}$ solves the Fokker-Planck equation in ([3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E5 "In item 1 ‣ Theorem 3.3 (A Benamou-Brenier type formula). ‣ A Benamou-Brenier type formula ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

$\bullet$ Proof of (3).
We denote $P^{*}$ the displacement random walk associated to ([S dyn k](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex17 "In Optimal transport appears at the limit of the slowing down procedure ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"))k≥2 with $R^{\tau}$ instead of $R$.
As $X_{\tau}$ is injective, we have $H(P&#124;R^{k})=H(P^{\tau}&#124;R^{\tau,k})$ for all $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ and $k\geq 1$. This implies that $P^{*}=\widehat{P}^{\tau}$. Hence (3) follows from (2).
∎

### Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations

Next proposition is another consequence of the Markov property of $\widehat{P}$.

###### Proposition 3.6.

For all $0\leq s\leq t\leq 1,$ let $\widehat{P}_{st}:=(X_{s},X_{t})_{\#}\widehat{P}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ be the joint law of the positions at time $s$ and $t$ under $\widehat{P}.$ Then,

1. (1)

   $\widehat{P}_{st}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ is an optimal coupling of $\mu_{s}$ and $\mu_{t},$ meaning that $\widehat{P}_{st}$ is a solution of ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex9 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) with $\mu_{s}$ and $\mu_{t}$ as prescribed marginal constraints;

2. (2)

   $W_{1}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})=\int_{[s,t]}dr\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu\widehat{J}_{r}(dzdw).$

###### Proof.

Both statements are consequences of

- •

  the Markov property of $\widehat{P},$ see Theorem [2.11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E11 "Theorem 2.11 (The dynamics of 𝑃̂). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), which allows for surgery by gluing the bridges of $\widehat{P}_{[s,t]}$ together with the restrictions $\widehat{P}_{[0,s]}$ and $\widehat{P}_{[t,1]}$, where we denote $P_{[u,v]}:=(X_{t};u\leq t\leq v)_{\#}P$;

- •

  the fact that $\ell_{st}:=\sum_{s<r<t}d(X_{r^{-}},X_{r})$ is insensitive to changes of time: i.e. for any strictly increasing mapping $\theta:[s,t]\to{[0,1]}$ with $\theta(s)=0,$ $\theta(t)=1,$ we have $\ell_{st}=\ell_{01}(X_{\theta}).$

A standard ad absurdum reasoning leads to (1).
Statement (2) follows from (1), a change of variables formula based on any absolutely continuous change of time $\theta:[s,t]\to{[0,1]}$ and the general identity

|  | $\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{r}=\dot{\theta}(r)\mathcal{J}_{\theta(r)},\quad\textrm{for almost every }\ r\in(s,t)$ |  | (3.7) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where $(\mathcal{J}_{u};u\in{[0,1]})$ is any jump kernel and $(\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{r};r\in[s,t])$ the jump kernel resulting from the mapping $X_{\theta}.$ ∎

Proposition [3.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E6 "Proposition 3.6. ‣ Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")-(2) entitles us to define the speed of the displacement interpolation $\mu$ at time $t$ by

|  | $\mathrm{speed}(\mu)_{t}:=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu\widehat{J}_{t}(dzdw),\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,$ |  | (3.8) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

to obtain

|  | $W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\int_{[0,1]}\mathrm{speed}(\mu)_{t}\,dt.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

For any change of time $\tau:{[0,1]}\to{[0,1]},$ we see with this identity, ([3.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E7 "In Proof. ‣ Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and the change of variable formula that

|  | $W_{1}(\mu^{\tau}_{0},\mu^{\tau}_{1})=W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}).$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Hence, there are infinitely many $\mu^{\tau}$ that minimize the action in formula ([3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E4 "In item 1 ‣ Theorem 3.3 (A Benamou-Brenier type formula). ‣ A Benamou-Brenier type formula ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")). This is the content of Theorem [3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E3 "Theorem 3.3 (A Benamou-Brenier type formula). ‣ A Benamou-Brenier type formula ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")-(3). On the other hand, next result states that only one of them has a constant speed.

###### Proposition 3.9.

Under the Hypotheses [2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E1 "Hypotheses 2.1. ‣ The underlying hypotheses ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"),
there exists a unique change of time $\tau_{o}$ such that $\mu^{\tau_{o}}$ has a constant speed, i.e.

|  | $W_{1}(\mu^{\tau_{o}}_{s},\mu_{t}^{\tau_{o}})=(t-s)W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),\quad\forall 0\leq s\leq t\leq 1.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

###### Proof.

Indeed, this equation is equivalent to

|  | $\dot{\tau}(s)\psi(\tau(s))=W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),\quad\textrm{a.e.},$ |  | (3.10) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

where$\psi(t):=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu\widehat{J}_{t}(dzdw),$ a.e. Clearly, the assumption that $d$ is uniformly lower bounded and ([2.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E12 "In Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) imply that $\psi>0.$ Hence, a solution of ([3.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E10 "In Proof. ‣ Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) is given by

|  | $\tau_{o}(s)=\Psi_{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}}^{-1}(W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\,s),\quad s\in{[0,1]}$ |  | (3.11) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

where for all $t\in{[0,1]},$

|  | $0\leq\Psi_{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}}(t):=\int_{[0,t]}\psi(r)\,dr=\int_{[0,t]}dr\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu\widehat{J}_{r}(dzdw)\leq W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})<\infty.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Let us prove the uniqueness. Remark that, as a continuous strictly monotone function, $\tau_{o}$ is bijective. In addition, it is absolutely continuous. Hence, any change of time $\tau$ is equal to $\tau_{o}\circ\sigma$ for some change of time $\sigma.$ Now, instead of starting from $\mu,$ let us do a change of time $\sigma$ on $\mu^{\tau_{o}}.$ Defining $\psi_{o}(u):=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(z,w)\,\mu\widehat{J}^{\tau_{o}}_{u}(dzdw),$ a.e. instead of $\psi,$ we arrive similarly at $\dot{\sigma}(u)\psi_{o}(\sigma(u))=W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),$ a.e. But, $\psi_{o}(u)=W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})$ for all $u$. Hence, $\dot{\sigma}=1,$ from which the desired result follows.
∎

###### Definition 3.12 (Constant speed displacement interpolation).

The time changed displacement interpolation $\mu^{\tau_{o}}$ with $\tau_{o}$ given at ([3.11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E11 "In Proof. ‣ Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) is called a constant speed displacement interpolation.

As a definition, a constant speed displacement interpolation is a minimizing geodesic between $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$ in the Wasserstein space $(\mathrm{P}_{1}(\mathcal{X}),W_{1})$. But the general definition of a minimizing geodesic is misleading in the present non-strictly convex setting, since there are infinitely many *action minimizing* $W_{1}$-geodesics which do not have a constant speed.

One must be aware that, in general, the change of time $\tau_{o}$ depends on $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$. Nevertheless, we shall see below at Theorem [3.15](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E15 "Theorem 3.15 (Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement). ‣ Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") that in the special important case where the distance $d$ is the standard graph distance $d_{\sim}$ specified by

|  | $\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},\quad d_{\sim}(x,y)=1\iff x\sim y,$ |  | (3.13) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

for any $\mu_{0},\mu_{1},$ the displacement interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ has a constant speed.

Let us go back to a general discrete metric measure graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d,m)$ where $d$ might differ from the standard graph distance $d_{\sim}.$ When transferring analogically the Lott-Sturm-Villani theory to this graph setting, it is likely that one should consider a reference random walk $R$ that satisfies the detailed balance conditions ([1.15](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E15 "In The discrete metric measure graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑,𝑚) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")):

|  | $m_{x}J_{t,x}(y)=m_{y}J_{t,y}(x),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},t\in{[0,1]},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

for being respectful of the metric measure graph structure. Indeed, this implies that for all $0\leq t\leq 1$ and $k\geq 1,$ $R_{t}^{k}=m$ and also that the generators $L_{t}^{k}$, see ([1.14](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E14 "In The discrete metric measure graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑,𝑚) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), are self-adjoint on $L^{2}(\mathcal{X},m).$
Pick $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$ and consider the associated constant speed interpolation $\mu^{\tau_{o}}.$ Since, for any change of time $\tau,$ the mapping $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)\mapsto Q^{\tau}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is injective, we have $H(P^{\tau}&#124;R^{\tau})=H(P&#124;R)$ for any $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega).$ This implies that $\mu^{\tau_{o}}$ is the displacement interpolation associated with $R^{\tau_{o}}:$

|  | $\mu^{\tau_{o}}=[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]^{R}\circ\tau_{o}=[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]^{R^{\tau_{o}}}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

But, since $J^{\tau}_{t}=\dot{\tau}(t)J_{\tau(t)},$ $R^{\tau_{o}}$ inherits of the detailed balance condition satisfied by $R:$

|  | $m_{x}J^{\tau_{o}}_{t,x}(y)=m_{y}J^{\tau_{o}}_{t,y}(x),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Therefore, the constant speed displacement interpolation $\mu^{\tau_{o}}$ is really a displacement interpolation in the sense of Definition [1.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E12 "Definitions 1.12. ‣ Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") with respect to the reference random walk $R^{\tau_{o}}$ which is respectful of the discrete metric measure graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim,d,m).$

### Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement

Next result tells us that along any displacement interpolation $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}],$ the average rate of mass displacement, as defined below, doesn’t depend on time.

###### Definitions 3.14 (Rate of mass displacement).

For any Markov random walk $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ with jump kernel $(j_{t,x};t\in{[0,1]},x\in\mathcal{X})$, we denote $\nu_{t}=P_{t}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X})$ and call

|  | $\nu j_{t}(dxdy):=\nu_{t}(dx)j_{t,x}(dy),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

the *distribution of the rate of mass displacement* of $P$ at time $t$.

We also call

|  | $\nu j_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\nu_{t}(dx)j_{t,x}(dy),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

the *average rate of mass displacement* of $P$ at time $t$.

###### Theorem 3.15 (Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement).

Suppose that the Hypotheses [2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E1 "Hypotheses 2.1. ‣ The underlying hypotheses ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") are satisfied. Let $\widehat{J}$ be the jump kernel of the displacement random walk $\widehat{P}$ and $\mu$ the corresponding displacement interpolation. There exists some $K>0$ such that

|  | $\mu\widehat{J}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})=K,\quad\forall t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

In particular, when the distance $d$ is the standard discrete distance $d_{\sim}$, see ([3.13](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E13 "In Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), the displacement interpolation $\mu$ has a constant speed.

Last statement simply relies on the remark that when $d=d_{\sim},$ the speed of $\mu$ coincides with its average rate of mass displacement.

Theorem [3.15](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E15 "Theorem 3.15 (Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement). ‣ Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") is a restatement of Theorem [7.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E4 "Theorem 7.4. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") which is proved at Section [7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7 "7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs").

###### Corollary 3.16.

The constant speed displacement interpolation $\mu^{\tau^{o}}$ defined at Definition [3.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E12 "Definition 3.12 (Constant speed displacement interpolation). ‣ Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") has also a constant average rate of mass displacement.

###### Proof.

Since $\mu^{\tau^{o}}=[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]^{R^{\tau^{o}}}$ is the $R^{\tau^{o}}$-displacement interpolation, one can apply Theorem [3.15](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E15 "Theorem 3.15 (Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement). ‣ Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs").
∎

### Natural substitutes for the constant speed geodesics on a discrete metric graph

Let $R$ be given. When specifying $\mu_{0}=\delta_{x}$ and $\mu_{1}=\delta_{y},$ the displacement random walk $\widehat{P}$ is simply $G^{xy}.$ Moreover, there exists a unique change of time $\tau^{xy}$ such that

|  | $\mu^{xy}:=[\delta_{x},\delta_{y}]\circ\tau^{xy}=G^{xy}_{\tau^{xy}}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

has a constant speed. Its dynamics is given by the current equation

|  | $\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\mu^{xy}_{t}(z)-{\dot{\tau}^{xy}_{t}}\sum_{w}[\mu^{xy}_{t}(w)J^{G,y}_{\tau^{xy}_{t},w}(z)-\mu^{xy}_{t}(z)J^{G,y}_{\tau^{xy}_{t},z}(w)]=0,&0\leq t\leq 1,z\in\mathcal{X},\\ \mu^{xy}_{0}=\delta_{x},&t=0.\end{array}\right.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

The $(R,d)$-displacement interpolation $\mu^{xy}$ is a natural time-continuous averaging of the piecewise constant paths $t\mapsto\delta_{\gamma(t)}$ with $\gamma$ in the set $\Gamma^{xy}$ of all $d$-geodesics joining $x$ and $y$. It depends on the choice of the reference random walk $R.$

### Binomial interpolations

We present some easy examples of constant speed interpolations $\mu^{xy}.$ Let us consider the simplest and important setting where $R$ is the reversible simple random walk and the distance $d=d_{\sim}$ is the standard graph distance.
The jump kernel is described at ([A.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E3 "In Simple random walk ‣ Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")): $J_{x}(y)=1/n_{x},$ $\forall x\sim y,$ and with the initial “volume measure” given at ([A.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E4 "In Simple random walk ‣ Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")): $m_{x}=n_{x},$ $\forall x\in\mathcal{X}$.

Let $x$ and $y$ be fixed . We know by Theorem [3.15](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E15 "Theorem 3.15 (Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement). ‣ Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") that the displacement interpolation $\mu^{xy}=[\delta_{x},\delta_{y}]=(G^{xy}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ has a constant speed. The dynamics of $G^{xy}$ is specified at Theorem [2.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E10 "Theorem 2.10 (The dynamics of 𝐺^{𝑥⁢𝑦}). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. |  | $J^{G,y}_{t,z}(w)=\mathbf{1}_{\left\{z\neq y\right\}}n_{z}^{-1}\frac{g_{t}^{y}(w)}{g_{t}^{y}(z)}=\mathbf{1}_{\left\{z\neq y\right\}}n_{z}^{-1}\frac{R(\Gamma(t,w;1,y)\mid X_{t}=w)}{R(\Gamma(t,z;1,y)\mid X_{t}=z)}$ |  |

#### The complete graph

Let $\mathcal{X}=\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}$ with $x\sim y$ for all $x\neq y\in\mathcal{X}.$ Then, for all $x\neq y$ and all $0\leq t<1,$ we have $J^{G,y}_{t,x}(y)=1/(1-t)$ and the probability that no jump occurred before time $t$ is $\mathrm{Proba}(N_{\lambda(t)}=0)$ where $N_{\lambda}$ denotes a random variable distributed according to Poisson($\lambda$) and $\lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{1-s}\,ds=-\log(1-t).$ Therefore, $\mathrm{Proba}(N_{\lambda(t)}=0)=\exp(-\lambda(t))=1-t$ and

|  | $G^{xy}_{t}=(1-t)\delta_{x}+t\delta_{y}=\sum_{z\in\left\{x,y\right\}}t^{d(x,z)}(1-t)^{d(z,y)}\,\delta_{z},$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

for any $0\leq t\leq 1.$ This law is in one-one correspondence with the Bernoulli law $\mathcal{B}(t)$ which is the specific binomial law $\mathcal{B}(2,t).$

#### The graph $\mathbb{Z}$

We consider the simple situation where $(\mathcal{X},\sim)$ is the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ with its natural graph structure. The reference random walk $R$ is the simple walk with $J_{z}=(\delta_{z-1}+\delta_{z+1})/2,$ $z\in\mathbb{Z},$ and the counting measure as its initial measure. Take $x<y\in\mathbb{Z}.$ Then, for any $0\leq t<1$ and $x\leq z<y,$ denoting $N_{1-t}$ a random variable distributed according to the Poisson($1-t$) law and $\delta=d(z,y)=y-z$, we obtain

###### Claim 3.17.

This proves that $G^{xy}$ has the same law as $x+\widetilde{N}$ where $\widetilde{N}$ is the bridge of a Poisson process $(N_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1},$ that is $\mathrm{Proba}(G^{xy}\in\cdot)=\mathrm{Proba}(N\in\left\{\cdot\right\}-x\mid N_{1}=d(x,y)).$

#### The hypercube

Consider the hypercube $\mathcal{X}=\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}$ with its natural graph structure so that the graph distance is the Hamming distance $d(x,y)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{x_{i}\neq y_{i}\right\}}$ where $x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})$ and $y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}).$ The reference path measure $R$ is the simple random walk with the uniform measure as its initial law.
The directed tree that describes the geodesic dynamics between $x$ and $y$ has exactly $d(x,y)!$ directed chains with length $d(x,y)$ and endpoints $x$ and $y$. The law of $G^{xy}$ is the uniform mixture of the $d(x,y)!$ corresponding Poisson bridges.

In order to describe for any $0\leq t\leq 1,$ the law $G^{xy}_{t},$ let us encode each intermediate state by an ordered sequence in $\left\{\textsf{d},\textsf{s}\right\}^{d(x,y)}$ where d and s stand respectively for “different” and “same”. With this encoding, $(\textsf{d},\dots,\textsf{d})$ is $x$ since $x$ has $d(x,y)$ components that are different from $y$, $(\textsf{s},\dots,\textsf{s})$ is $y$ since $y$ has all its components which are the same as $y$ and
we see that the support $S^{xy}$ of $G^{xy}_{t}$ consists of $2^{d(x,y)}$ intermediate states at any time $0<t<1.$ A short computation shows that for each $0<t<1,$ we have

|  | $G^{xy}_{t}=\sum_{z\in S^{xy}}t^{d(x,z)}(1-t)^{d(z,y)}\,\delta_{z}.$ |  |

## 4. The Schrödinger problem

The reversing measure $m$ of the simple random walk on an infinite graph is unbounded, see ([A.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E4 "In Simple random walk ‣ Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")). Since it is the analogue of the volume measure of a Riemannian manifold, it is likely that the relative entropy with respect to $m$ should play an important role when trying to develop a Lott-Sturm-Villani theory on infinite graphs. Consequently, in this case the reference path measure $R$ is unbounded.

In order to state the Schrödinger problem, it will be necessary to have in mind some basic facts about relative entropy with respect to a possibly *unbounded* reference measure. They are collected at Section [B](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A2 "Appendix B Relative entropy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs").

### The topological path space $\Omega$

### $\Gamma$-convergence

The right notion of convergence for the sequences of minimization problems $\eqref{Skdyn}_{k\geq 2}$ and $\eqref{Sk}_{k\geq 2}$ is the $\Gamma$-convergence which is briefly over-viewed now.
Recall that $\Gamma\textrm{-}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}=f$ on the metric space $Y$ if and only if for any $y\in Y,$

1. (a)

   $\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}(y_{k})\geq f(y)$ for any convergent sequence $y_{k}\to y,$

2. (b)

   $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}(y^{o}_{k})=f(y)$ for some sequence $y^{o}_{k}\to y.$

A function $f$ is said to be coercive if for any $a\geq\inf f,$ $\left\{f\leq a\right\}$ is a compact set.

The sequence $(f^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ is said to be equi-coercive if for any real $a$, there exists some compact set $K_{a}$ such that $\cup_{k}\left\{f^{k}\leq a\right\}\subset K_{a}.$
If in addition to $\Gamma\textrm{-}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{k}=f$, the sequence $(f^{k})_{k\geq 1}$ is equi-coercive,
then:

- •

  $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\inf f^{k}=\inf f,$

- •

  if $\inf f<\infty,$ any limit point $y^{*}$ of a sequence $(y^{*}_{k})_{k\geq 1}$ of approximate minimizers i.e.: $f^{k}(y^{*}_{k})\leq\inf f^{k}+\epsilon_{k}$ with $\epsilon_{k}\geq 0$ and $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\epsilon_{k}=0,$ minimizes $f$ i.e.: $f(y^{*})=\inf f.$

For more details about $\Gamma$-convergence, see [[DM93](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx4)] for instance.

### Reciprocal path measure

The reciprocal property extends the notion of Markov property.
For more details, see [[LRZ](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx15)] and the references therein.

###### Definition 6.1.

A measure $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is said to be *reciprocal* if for any $0\leq u\leq v\leq 1,$ $Q(X_{[u,v]}\in\cdot\mid X_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]})=Q(X_{[u,v]}\in\cdot\mid X_{u},X_{v}).$

The following lemma is standard. We state it for the comfort of the reader.

###### Lemma 6.2.

1. (a)

   Any Markov measure is reciprocal (but the converse is false).

2. (b)

   Almost every bridge of a reciprocal measure is Markov.

###### Proof.

Let us prove (a). Take $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ any Markov measure. We have for any measurable subsets $A\in\Omega_{[u,v]}$ and $B\in\Omega_{[v,1]}$ with $Q(X_{[v,1]}\in B)>0,$

|  | $\displaystyle Q(X_{[u,v]}\in A\mid X_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]}\in B)$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{Q(X_{[u,v]}\in A,X_{[v,1]}\in B\mid X_{[0,u]})}{Q(X_{[v,1]}\in B\mid X_{[0,u]})}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{Q(X_{[u,v]}\in A,X_{[v,1]}\in B\mid X_{u})}{Q(X_{[v,1]}\in B\mid X_{u})}$ |  |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle Q(X_{[u,v]}\in A\mid X_{u},X_{[v,1]}\in B),$ |  |

meaning that $Q(X_{[u,v]}\in\cdot\mid X_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]})=Q(X_{[u,v]}\in\cdot\mid X_{u},X_{[v,1]}).$ And one concludes using the time-symmetry of the Markov property.

Let us prove (b). Let $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ be reciprocal. We have for any $x,y\in\mathcal{X}$ and $0\leq t\leq 1,$

|  | $\displaystyle Q^{xy}(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\mid X_{[0,t]})$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle Q(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\mid X_{0}=x,X_{[0,t]},X_{1}=y)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle Q(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\mid X_{t},X_{1}=y)=Q^{xy}(X_{[t,1]}\in\cdot\mid X_{t})$ |  |

where last equality is obtained repeating the argument with $X_{t}$ instead of $X_{[0,t]}$. This is the announced result.
∎

###### Lemma 6.3.

Let $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ be a reciprocal measure and $G\subset\Gamma$ a measurable subset of $\Omega$ consisting of geodesics. Then, the measure $Q^{\prime}:=\mathbf{1}_{G}\,Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ is still reciprocal.

###### Proof.

We use the following property of a geodesic: the restriction $\gamma_{[u,v]}$ of any geodesic $\gamma\in\Gamma,$ is still a geodesic of $\Omega_{[u,v]}$. Therefore, $X\in\Gamma^{X_{0},X_{1}}_{[0,1]}$ implies that $X_{[u,v]}\in\Gamma^{X_{u},X_{v}}_{[u,v]}$ which implies that

|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle Q(X\in G,X_{[u,v]}\in A\mid X_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]})$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{[0,u]}\in G_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]}\in G_{[v,1]}\}}Q(X_{[u,v]}\in G_{[u,v]}\cap A\mid X_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]})$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{[0,u]}\in G_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]}\in G_{[v,1]}\}}Q(X_{[u,v]}\in G_{[u,v]}\cap A\mid X_{u},X_{v})$ |  |

for any measurable set $A\subset\Omega_{[u,v]},$ where the last equality follows from the reciprocal property. Therefore, since $X_{[0,u]}\in G_{[0,u]}$ and $X_{[v,1]}\in G_{[v,1]}$, $Q^{\prime}\textrm{-a.e.},$ we have

|  | $\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(X_{[u,v]}\in A\mid X_{[0,u]},X_{[v,1]})$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle Q(X_{[u,v]}\in G_{[u,v]}\cap A\mid X_{u},X_{v})$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(X_{[u,v]}\in A\mid X_{u},X_{v}),\qquad Q^{\prime}\textrm{-a.e.}$ |  |

which is the desired result.
∎

###### Proposition 6.4.

If $R$ is reversible, then $G$ is also reversible.
The measure $G$ is reciprocal (but not Markov in general) and it concentrates on the set $\Gamma$ of all geodesics.

###### Proof.

If $R$ is reversible, the time-reversal invariances of $\int_{[0,1]}J_{X_{t}}(\mathcal{X})\,dt$ and $\Gamma$ together with the symmetry of the distance $d$ immediately imply the reversibility of $G.$

Let us show that $R_{J}:=\exp(\int_{[0,1]}J_{t,X_{t}}(\mathcal{X})\,dt)\,R$ is Markov by proving that for each $t\in{[0,1]}$ and all bounded measurable functions $a\in\sigma(X_{[0,t]})$ and $b\in\sigma(X_{[t,1]})$, we have $E_{R_{J}}(ab\mid X_{t})=E_{R_{J}}(a\mid X_{t})E_{R_{J}}(b\mid X_{t}).$ Denoting $\alpha:=\exp(\int_{[0,t]}J_{s,X_{s}}(\mathcal{X})\,ds)\in\sigma(X_{[0,t]})$ and $\beta:=\exp(\int_{[t,1]}J_{s,X_{s}}(\mathcal{X})\,ds)\in\sigma(X_{[t,1]}),$ by the Markov property of $R$, we have

|  | $\displaystyle E_{R_{J}}(ab\mid X_{t})=\frac{E_{R}(a\alpha b\beta\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(\alpha\beta\mid X_{t})}=\frac{E_{R}(a\alpha\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(\alpha\mid X_{t})}\frac{E_{R}(b\beta\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(\beta\mid X_{t})}=E_{R_{J}}(a\mid X_{t})E_{R_{J}}(b\mid X_{t})$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where last equality is obtained by plugging successively $b=1$ and $a=1$ in $E_{R_{J}}(ab\mid X_{t})=\frac{E_{R}(a\alpha\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(\alpha\mid X_{t})}\frac{E_{R}(b\beta\mid X_{t})}{E_{R}(\beta\mid X_{t})}.$ This shows that $R_{J}$ is Markov.

We conclude with Lemma [6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E3 "Lemma 6.3. ‣ Reciprocal path measure ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") that $G=\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}R_{J}$ is reciprocal.
∎

Although $G$ is reciprocal, it is not Markov. To see this, remark that the time reversed of a geodesic is also geodesic. If the geodesic walker only knows that he stands at $z$ at time $t$, having forgotten his past history and in particular that his previous state before jumping was $z^{\prime}$, he cannot decide to forbid $z^{\prime}$ to be his next state. Nevertheless, the bridges $G^{xy}$ are Markov.

###### Corollary 6.5.

For every $(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}$, the bridge $G^{xy}$ is Markov.

###### Proof.

This follows from Lemma [6.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E2 "Lemma 6.2. ‣ Reciprocal path measure ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") and Proposition [6.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E4 "Proposition 6.4. ‣ Basic properties of 𝐺. ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), remarking that under our irreducibility assumption, $R_{01}$-almost everywhere is equivalent to everywhere on $\mathcal{X}^{2}$. ∎

As $G^{xy}$ is Markov, it is sufficient to compute its jump kernel to characterize its dynamics. Recall the definition of the directed tree $(\Gamma^{xy}({[0,1]}),\to)$, between the statements of Theorems [2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E7 "Theorem 2.7. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. ### Proof of Theorem [2.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E10 "Theorem 2.10 (The dynamics of 𝐺^{𝑥⁢𝑦}). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")

For all $0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq t_{3}\leq 1,$ $z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in\mathcal{X},$ we denote

In particular, we have $\Gamma^{xy}=\Gamma(0,x;1,y).$ We also introduce the functions on $\Omega:$

|  | $\displaystyle G(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2})$ | $\displaystyle:=$ | $\displaystyle\exp\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}J_{t,X_{t}}(\mathcal{X})\,dt\right)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2})}$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  | $\displaystyle G(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2};t_{3},z_{3})$ | $\displaystyle:=$ | $\displaystyle\exp\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{3}}J_{t,X_{t}}(\mathcal{X})\,dt\right)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2};t_{3},z_{3})}.$ |  |

We see that $g^{y}_{t}(z)=E_{R}\left(G(t,z;1,y)\mid X_{t}=z\right).$
As a direct consequence of the definition of a geodesic, for all $0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq t_{3}\leq 1,$ $z_{1}\preceq z_{2}\preceq z_{3}\in\Gamma^{xy}({[0,1]}),$ we have

|  | $\Gamma(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2};t_{3},z_{3})=\Gamma(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2})\cap\Gamma(t_{2},z_{2};t_{3},z_{3})$ |  |

which implies that

|  | $G(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2};t_{3},z_{3})=G(t_{1},z_{1};t_{2},z_{2})G(t_{2},z_{2};t_{3},z_{3})\quad\textrm{on }\Gamma^{xy}.$ |  | (6.6) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

As $G^{xy}$ is Markov, to derive the infinitesimal generator of its Markov semi-group, it is enough to compute its forward stochastic derivative

|  | $L^{G,xy}_{t}u(z):=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}E_{G^{xy}}\left[u(X_{t+h})-u(X_{t})&#124;X_{t}=z\right],\quad 0\leq t<1,z\in\Gamma^{xy}({[0,1]}).$ |  |

For any $0\leq t<1,z\in\Gamma^{xy}({[0,1]})$, with ([2.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E6 "In The minimization problems ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) we see that

|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle E_{G^{xy}}(U_{t+h}-U_{t}&#124;X_{t}=z)$ |  |

### $\widehat{P}$ is Markov

It follows from ([2.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E8 "In item 4 ‣ Theorem 2.7. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), Proposition [6.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E4 "Proposition 6.4. ‣ Basic properties of 𝐺. ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") and [[LRZ](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx15), Prop. 2.8] that the limiting path measure $\widehat{P}$ is reciprocal.
We can do better, but it requires some effort.

###### Proposition 6.7.

The limiting path measure $\widehat{P}$ is Markov.

###### Proof.

By Theorem [2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E7 "Theorem 2.7. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")-(1), for each $k\geq 2,$ $\widehat{P}^{k}$ inherits the Markov property of $R.$ We show below at Lemma [6.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E12 "Lemma 6.12. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") that, as $k$ tends to infinity, $\widehat{P}^{k}$ converges in variation norm to $\widehat{P}$ and we conclude with Lemma [6.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E8 "Lemma 6.8 (Nagasawa, []). ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") that $\widehat{P}$ is Markov.
∎

Recall that the total variation norm of the signed bounded measure $q$ on $Y$ is $\&#124;q\&#124;_{\mathrm{TV}}:=&#124;q&#124;(Y)=q^{+}(Y)+q^{-}(Y)=\sup_{f:\sup&#124;f&#124;\leq 1}\int_{Y}f\,dq=\sup_{A\subset Y}(&#124;q(A)&#124;+&#124;q(A^{c})&#124;)$

###### Lemma 6.8 (Nagasawa, [[Nag93](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx21)]).

Let $(P_{k})_{k\geq 1}$ be a sequence in $\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ of Markov probability measures which converges in variation norm to $P,$ then $P$ is also Markov.

###### Proof.

We have to show that for all $0\leq t\leq 1$ and any bounded functions $a\in\sigma(X_{[0,t]})$ and $c\in\sigma(X_{[t,1]}),$ we have $E_{P}(ac)=E_{P}(aE_{P}(c&#124;X_{t})).$
For any bounded functions $b\in\sigma(X_{t})$ and $c\in\sigma(X_{[t,1]})$, we have

|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle\big&#124;E_{P}[bE_{P}(c&#124;X_{t})]-E_{P}[bE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]\big&#124;$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |  | $\displaystyle\leq$ | $\displaystyle\big&#124;E_{P}(bc)-E_{P^{k}}(bc)\big&#124;+\big&#124;E_{P^{k}}[bE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]-E_{P}[bE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]\big&#124;$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle\leq$ | $\displaystyle 2\&#124;b\&#124;\&#124;c\&#124;\&#124;P^{k}-P\&#124;_{\mathrm{TV}}\underset{k\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0$ |  |

Hence,

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}E_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})=E_{P}(c&#124;X_{t})\quad\textrm{ in }L^{1}(P).$ |  | (6.9) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

Let $a\in\sigma(X_{[0,t]}),$

|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle\big&#124;E_{P}(ac)-E_{P}[aE_{P}(c&#124;X_{t})]\big&#124;$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |  | $\displaystyle\leq$ | $\displaystyle\big&#124;E_{P}(ac)-E_{P^{k}}(ac)\big&#124;+\big&#124;E_{P^{k}}[aE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]-E_{P}[aE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]\big&#124;$ |  |
|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle\qquad+\big&#124;E_{P}[aE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]-E_{P}[aE_{P}(c&#124;X_{t})]\big&#124;$ |  |

But,

|  | $\big&#124;E_{P}(ac)-E_{P^{k}}(ac)\big&#124;+\big&#124;E_{P^{k}}[aE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]-E_{P}[aE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]\big&#124;\leq 2\&#124;a\&#124;\&#124;c\&#124;\&#124;P^{k}-P\&#124;_{\mathrm{TV}}\underset{k\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and $\big&#124;E_{P}[aE_{P^{k}}(c&#124;X_{t})]-E_{P}[aE_{P}(c&#124;X_{t})]\big&#124;\underset{k\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0$ because of ([6.9](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E9 "In Proof. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")). This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

There are counter-examples of sequences $(P_{k})_{k\geq 1}$ of Markov measures converging narrowly to a non-Markov $P$.

The following standard lemma (Scheffé’s theorem) is a preliminary result for Lemma [6.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E12 "Lemma 6.12. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")’s proof.

###### Lemma 6.10.

Let $r$ be a positive measure and $p_{k}=z_{k}\,r,$ $k\geq 1$, $p=z\,r$ be probability measures which are absolutely continuous with respect to $r.$ If $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}z_{k}=z,$ $r\textrm{-a.e.}$, then

|  | $\&#124;z\,r-z_{k}\,r\&#124;_{\mathrm{TV}}=\int&#124;z-z_{k}&#124;\,dr\underset{k\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

###### Proof.

Set $d_{k}=z-z_{k}.$ Then, $\int d_{k}\,dr=0.$ For any measurable subset $A$,

|  | $\Big&#124;\int_{A}d_{k}\,dr\Big&#124;+\Big&#124;\int_{A^{c}}d_{k}\,dr\Big&#124;\leq\int&#124;d_{k}&#124;\,dr$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with equality when $A=\left\{d_{k}\geq 0\right\}.$ Therefore, $\&#124;z\,r-z_{k}\,r\&#124;_{\mathrm{TV}}=\int&#124;d_{k}&#124;\,dr$. As $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}d_{k}^{+}=0,$ $r\textrm{-a.e.}$ and $0\leq d_{k}^{+}\leq z,$ we obtain $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int&#124;d_{k}&#124;\,dr=2\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int d_{k}^{+}\,dr=0.$ ∎

The following standard lemma will also be used during the proof of Lemma [6.12](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E12 "Lemma 6.12. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs").

###### Lemma 6.11 (Laplace principle).

Let $r$ be a positive measure on the measurable set $Y$. For any measurable function $F:Y\to[-\infty,\infty]$ which is not identically equal to $-\infty$ and any measurable subset $Y^{\prime}\subset Y$ such that $r(Y^{\prime})<\infty,$ we have

|  | $\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\epsilon\log\int_{Y^{\prime}}e^{F/\epsilon}\,dr=r\esssup_{Y^{\prime}}F\in(-\infty,\infty].$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

###### Proof.

Considering the restriction of $r$ to $Y^{\prime},$ one can assume without loss of generality that $Y^{\prime}=Y$ and $r(Y)<\infty.$ To simplify notation, let us denote $b:=r\esssup F\in(-\infty,\infty]$ and for any $\delta>0,$ $b_{\delta}:=\min[b-\delta,1/\delta]$.
Since $F\leq b,$ $r\textrm{-a.e.},$ we have $\epsilon\log\int e^{F/\epsilon}\,dr\leq b+\epsilon\log r(Y)$ and a fortiori

|  | $\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int e^{F/\epsilon}\,dr\leq b.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

On the other hand, for any $\epsilon,\delta>0,$

|  | $\epsilon\log\int e^{F/\epsilon}\,dr\geq\epsilon\log\int\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F\geq b_{\delta}\right\}}e^{F/\epsilon}\,dr\geq\epsilon\log r(F\geq b_{\delta})+b_{\delta}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

By the definition of $r\esssup F,$ for any $\delta>0$ we have $0<r(F\geq b_{\delta})\leq r(Y)<\infty.$ Therefore, taking the $\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}$ and then letting $\delta$ tend down to zero, we obtain

|  | $\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int e^{F/a_{\epsilon}}\,dr\geq b,$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

###### Lemma 6.12.

We have: $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\&#124;\widehat{P}^{k}-\widehat{P}\&#124;_{TV}=0.$

###### Proof.

By Lemma [6.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E10 "Lemma 6.10. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), it is enough to prove that $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}d\widehat{P}^{k}/dR=d\widehat{P}/dR,$ $R\textrm{-a.e.}$ For any $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ such that $P\ll R,$ we have $\frac{dP}{dR}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{0}=x,X_{1}=y\}}\frac{P_{01}(x,y)}{R_{01}(x,y)}\ \frac{dP^{xy}}{dR^{xy}}.$ We also have $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\widehat{P}_{01}^{k}(x,y)=\widehat{P}_{01}(x,y)$ and $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\widehat{P}^{k,xy}=\widehat{P}^{xy}$ for all $x,y\in\mathcal{X}.$ Therefore, it remains to show that for each $(x,y)\in\mathcal{X},$ $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}d\widehat{P}^{xy}/d\widehat{P}^{k,xy}=1,$ $R^{xy}\textrm{-a.e.}$ As, $\widehat{P}^{xy}=G^{xy}$ and $\widehat{P}^{k,xy}=R^{k,xy}$, this amounts to prove that

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{dG^{xy}}{dR^{k,xy}}=1,\quad R^{xy}\textrm{-a.e.}$ |  | (6.13) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ------ |

for all $x,y.$ By Girsanov’s formula ([4.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S4.E5 "In Girsanov’s formula ‣ Proofs of Theorem -(1) and Proposition ‣ 4. The Schrödinger problem ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), for any $0\leq t\leq 1,$

|  | $R^{k}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t})=k^{-\ell_{[t,1]}}\exp\Big(\int_{[t,1]\times\mathcal{X}}(1-k^{-d(X_{s},y)})\,J_{s,X_{s}}(dy)\,ds\Big)\,R_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t})$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $\ell_{[t,1]}:=\sum_{t\leq s\leq 1}d(X_{s^{-}},X_{s}).$ Hence, the jump measure of $R^{k,xy}$ is given for any $t\in{[0,1]},z,w\in\mathcal{X}$ by

|  |  |                                | $\displaystyle J^{k,xy}_{t,z}(w)$ |  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{R^{k}(X_{1}=y&#124;X_{t}=w)}{R^{k}(X_{1}=y&#124;X_{t}=z)}\,k^{-d(z,w)}\,J_{t,z}(w)$ |  |
|  |  | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\frac{E_{R}\left[k^{-\{d(z,w)+\ell_{[t,1]}\}}\exp\left(\int_{[t,1]\times\mathcal{X}}(1-k^{-d(X_{s},a)})\,J_{s,X_{s}}(da)ds\right)\mathbf{1}_{(X_{1}=y)}\mid X_{t}=w\right]}{E_{R}\left[k^{-\ell_{[t,1]}}\exp\left(\int_{[t,1]\times\mathcal{X}}(1-k^{-d(X_{s},a)})\,J_{s,X_{s}}(da)ds\right)\mathbf{1}_{(X_{1}=y)}\mid X_{t}=z\right]}J_{t,z}(w).$ |  |

With Lemma [6.11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E11 "Lemma 6.11 (Laplace principle). ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), we obtain that

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}J^{k,xy}_{t,z}(w)=\frac{E_{R}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{1}J_{s,X_{s}}(\mathcal{X})\,ds\right)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma(t,w;1,y)}&#124;X_{t}=w\right]}{E_{R}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{1}J_{s,X_{s}}(\mathcal{X})\,ds\right)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma(t,z;1,y)}&#124;X_{t}=z\right]}\,J_{t,z}(w)=:J^{G,y}_{t,z}(w)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

meaning, with Theorem [2.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E10 "Theorem 2.10 (The dynamics of 𝐺^{𝑥⁢𝑦}). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), that the pointwise limit of $J^{k,xy}$ as $k$ tends to infinity is the jump measure $J^{G,y}$ of $G^{xy}.$ With Girsanov’s formula, this implies that ([6.13](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E13 "In Proof. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) is true, completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

###### Remark 6.14.

Theorem [2.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E10 "Theorem 2.10 (The dynamics of 𝐺^{𝑥⁢𝑦}). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), with no reference to an entropy minimization problem.

#### A comparison with the usual continuous setting

For comparison, it is worthwhile recalling an analogous result in the usual setting of McCann’s displacement interpolations on $\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}$. The Monge-Mather shortening principle, see [[Vil09](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx29), Ch. 8], tells us that the optimal plan $\widehat{\pi}$ for the quadratic cost between $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$ is such that two distinct geodesics interpolating between couples of endpoints in its support $\supp\widehat{\pi},$ do not intersect.

In our graph setting, this would correspond to the nice situation where for any $z$ and $t<1$, $\widehat{P}(X_{1}\in\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)$ reduces to a Dirac measure. But in our discrete setting, $\widehat{P}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)$ has sample paths which live on a directed geodesic tree with top leaves (at time $1$) distributed according to a probability measure $\widehat{P}(X_{1}\in\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)$ which might not reduce to a Dirac mass in the general case.

This directed tree structure is a consequence of the *Markov property* of $\widehat{P}$.
It can also be seen as a consequence of the *shortening principle* which, in the present metric cost setting, is an elementary consequence of the triangle inequality.

As a corollary of Proposition [6.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E7 "Proposition 6.7. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), we obtain the following result.

###### Proposition 6.15.

For all $0\leq s\leq t\leq 1,$ $\widehat{P}_{st}\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ is an optimal coupling of $\mu_{s}$ and $\mu_{t},$ meaning that $\widehat{P}_{st}$ is a solution of ([MK](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex9 "In Displacement interpolations on a discrete metric graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) with $\mu_{s}$ and $\mu_{t}$ as prescribed marginal constraints.

###### Proof.

It is a consequence of

1. (1)

   the Markov property of $\widehat{P},$ see Proposition [6.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S6.E7 "Proposition 6.7. ‣ 𝑃̂ is Markov ‣ 6. Dynamics of the displacement random walk ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), which allows surgery by gluing the bridges of $\widehat{P}_{[s,t]}$ together with the restrictions $\widehat{P}_{[0,s]}$ and $\widehat{P}_{[t,1]}$;

2. (2)

   the fact that $\ell_{st}:=\sum_{s<r<t}d(X_{r^{-}},X_{r})$ is insensitive to changes of time: i.e. for any strictly increasing mapping $\theta:[s,t]\to{[0,1]}$ with $\theta(s)=0,$ $\theta(t)=1,$ we have $\ell_{st}=\ell_{01}(X\circ\theta).$

A standard ad absurdum reasoning leads to the announced property.
∎

### Proof of Theorem [2.11](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E11 "Theorem 2.11 (The dynamics of 𝑃̂). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")-(2)

Now, let us investigate the dynamics of $\widehat{P}$ in the general case where it interpolates between marginal constraints $\mu_{0}$ and $\mu_{1}$ which are not necessarily Dirac measures.
Let us denote

|  | $\widehat{p}(t,z;dy):=\widehat{P}(X_{1}\in dy&#124;X_{t}=z)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

so that the optimal coupling $\widehat{P}_{t1}$ of $\mu_{t}$ and $\mu_{1}$ disintegrates as $\widehat{P}_{t1}(dzdy)=\mu_{t}(dz)\widehat{p}(t,z;dy).$
As in Theorem [2.10](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E10 "Theorem 2.10 (The dynamics of 𝐺^{𝑥⁢𝑦}). ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), we compute a stochastic derivative. A general disintegration result tells us that

|  | $\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\widehat{P}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z,X_{1}=y)\,\widehat{P}(X_{1}\in dy&#124;X_{t}=z)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}G_{[t,1]}^{x_{o},y}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z,)\,\widehat{p}(t,z;dy)$ |  |

since, for $\mu_{0}$-almost any $x_{o},$

|  | $\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z,X_{1}=y)$ | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{[t,1]}(\cdot&#124;X_{0}=x_{o},X_{t}=z,X_{1}=y)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --- |
|  |                                | $\displaystyle=$ | $\displaystyle G_{[t,1]}^{x_{o},y}(\cdot&#124;X_{t}=z)$ |  |

where we used the Markov property of $\widehat{P}$ at the first equality and the identity $\widehat{P}^{x_{o},y}=G^{x_{o},y}$ at the second equality. Therefore, for all $0<t<t+h<1,$

|  | $E_{\widehat{P}}\Big([u(X_{t+h})-u(X_{t})]/h&#124;X_{t}=z\Big)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}E_{G^{x_{o},y}}\Big([u(X_{t+h})-u(X_{t})]/h&#124;X_{t}=z\Big)\,\widehat{p}(t,z;dy)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and letting $h$ tend down to zero, we see that the stochastic derivative of $\widehat{P}$ is given by

|  | $\widehat{L}_{t}u(z)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}L^{G,y}_{t}u(z)\,\widehat{p}(t,z;dy)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

which gives the desired expression for the jump kernel $\widehat{J}.$
The evolution equation for $[\mu_{0},\mu_{1}]$ is the usual forward Fokker-Planck equation for the time-marginal flow $t\mapsto\widehat{P}_{t}$ of the Markov measure $\widehat{P}.$ $\square$

## 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement

The main result of this section is Theorem [7.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E4 "Theorem 7.4. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"). It states that the constant average rate of mass displacement, recall Definition [3.14](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E14 "Definitions 3.14 (Rate of mass displacement). ‣ Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), is conserved along the displacement interpolation. It is a consequence of the Corollary [7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E3 "Corollary 7.3. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") of Proposition [7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E1 "Proposition 7.1. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") which also asserts that some similar quantity is conserved along the time marginal flow of the solution of the dynamical Schrödinger problem ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S4.Ex1 "In Schrödinger problem ‣ 4. The Schrödinger problem ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

### Main results of the section

The main technical result of this section is the following Proposition [7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E1 "Proposition 7.1. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") whose proof is postponed to the next subsection.

###### Proposition 7.1.

Let $P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ be a random walk. We denote $(\nu_{t})_{t\in{[0,1]}}$ its marginal flow and $(\nu j_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2}))_{0\leq t\leq 1}$ its average rate of mass displacement. Let us assume that

1. (i)

   $H(P&#124;R)<\infty;$

2. (ii)

   $\nu j_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})>0$ for all $t\in{[0,1]}$;

3. (iii)

   $1<\int_{[0,1]}\nu j_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})/\nu J_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,dt\leq\infty.$

Then, there exists a change of time $\widehat{\tau}$ which minimizes the function $\tau\mapsto H(P^{\tau}&#124;R)$ among all the changes of time $\tau$ and verifies

|  | $\nu j^{\widehat{\tau}}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})-\nu J_{\widehat{\tau}(t)}(\mathcal{X}^{2})=K,\quad\textrm{for almost every }t\in{[0,1]},$ |  | (7.2) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

for some constant $K>0.$ We have denoted by $\nu j^{\widehat{\tau}}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})=\dot{\widehat{\tau}}(t)\nu j_{\widehat{\tau}(t)}(\mathcal{X}^{2})$ the average rate of mass displacement of $P^{\widehat{\tau}}.$

Let us admit this proposition for a while and investigate its consequences.

###### Corollary 7.3.

Let ${\widetilde{P}}$ be the solution of ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S4.Ex1 "In Schrödinger problem ‣ 4. The Schrödinger problem ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and denote $\nu$ its marginal flow and ${\widetilde{J}}$ its jump kernel. Suppose that$1<\int_{[0,1]}\nu{\widetilde{J}}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})/\nu J_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,dt\leq\infty.$ Then there exists some $K>0$ such that$\nu{\widetilde{J}}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})-\nu J_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})=K,\ \textrm{for all }t\in{[0,1]}.$

###### Proof.

As ${\widetilde{P}}$ solves ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S4.Ex1 "In Schrödinger problem ‣ 4. The Schrödinger problem ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), we have $H({\widetilde{P}}&#124;R)<\infty$ and also $\nu{\widetilde{J}}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})>0$ for all $t\in{[0,1]},$ see [[Léob](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx12), Prop. 4.2]. Hence, we are allowed to apply Proposition [7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E1 "Proposition 7.1. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"). But as ${\widetilde{P}}$ solves ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S4.Ex1 "In Schrödinger problem ‣ 4. The Schrödinger problem ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), the only time change $\widetilde{\tau}$ which minimizes $\tau\mapsto H({\widetilde{P}}^{\tau}&#124;R)$ is the identity; recall that ([Sdyn](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S4.Ex1 "In Schrödinger problem ‣ 4. The Schrödinger problem ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) admits a unique minimizer. Therefore $\widetilde{\tau}(t)=t$ for all $t\in{[0,1]}$ and ([7.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E2 "In Proposition 7.1. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) becomes $\nu{\widetilde{J}}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})-\nu J_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})=K,$ almost everywhere. Finally we can remove this “almost” since this limitation comes from ([7.14](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E14 "In Lemma 7.13. ‣ Statement and proof of Lemma ‣ Proof of Proposition ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) for taking *absolutely continuous* changes of time into account and in the present case $\tau=\mathrm{Id}$ is differentiable everywhere.
∎

With Corollary [7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E3 "Corollary 7.3. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") at hand, we can prove the main result of the section.

###### Theorem 7.4.

There exists some $K>0$ such that$\mu\widehat{J}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})=K,$ for all $t\in{[0,1]}.$

In particular, when the distance $d$ is the standard discrete distance $d_{\sim}$: i.e. for all $x,y\in\mathcal{X},$ $x\sim y\iff d_{\sim}(x,y)=1,$ then the displacement interpolation $\mu$ has a constant speed.

###### Proof.

The second statement is a direct consequence of the first one with ([3.8](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S3.E8 "In Constant speed and minimizing displacement interpolations ‣ 3. Main results about the displacement interpolations ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

Let us prove the first statement.
For each $k$, let $\widehat{P}^{k}$ be the solution of ([S dyn k](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex17 "In Optimal transport appears at the limit of the slowing down procedure ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).
We denote $\mu^{k}$ and $\widehat{J}^{k}$ its marginal flow and jump kernel.
Let us first show that for all $t\in{[0,1]},$

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{[0,t]}\mu^{k}\widehat{J}^{k}_{s}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,ds=\int_{[0,t]}\mu\widehat{J}_{s}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,ds.$ |  | (7.5) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

As $\int_{[0,t]}\mu^{k}\widehat{J}^{k}_{s}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,ds=E_{\widehat{P}^{k}}N_{t}$ where $N_{t}:=\sum_{0<s<t}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{X_{s^{-}}\not=X_{s}\right\}}$ is the number of jumps during $[0,t],$ ([7.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E5 "In Proof. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) doesn’t directly follow from $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\widehat{P}^{k}=\widehat{P}$ (see Theorem [2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E7 "Theorem 2.7. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), because $N_{t}$ is unbounded. We must strengthen Theorem [2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E7 "Theorem 2.7. ‣ Results about the displacement random walks ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") to authorize the test functions $0\leq N_{t}\leq N_{1}:=N,$ $0\leq t\leq 1.$ To do so, it is enough to show that Lemma [5.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S5.E5 "Lemma 5.5. ‣ The convergences of \"()\"_{𝑘≥2} and \"()\"_{𝑘≥2} ‣ 5. Lazy random walks converge to displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") can be improved as follows: the sequence $(I^{k})_{k\geq 2}$ is equi-coercive and $\Gamma\textrm{-}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}I^{k}=I$ in $\mathrm{P}_{N}(\Omega):=\left\{P\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega);E_{P}N<\infty\right\}$ with respect to the topology weakened by the functions $C_{b}(\Omega)\cup\left\{N_{t};0\leq t\leq 1\right\}.$ For this strengthening to hold, it is sufficient that $(I^{k})_{k\geq 2}$ is equi-coercive in $\mathrm{P}_{N}(\Omega)$.
Inspecting the proof of Lemma [5.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S5.E5 "Lemma 5.5. ‣ The convergences of \"()\"_{𝑘≥2} and \"()\"_{𝑘≥2} ‣ 5. Lazy random walks converge to displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), we see that $I^{k}\geq I+c$ for all $k$ and some constant $c$. Consequently, it remains to show that the function $I$ is coercive in $\mathrm{P}_{N}(\Omega)$. But this follows from the proof of Lemma [5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S5.E3 "Lemma 5.3. ‣ The convergences of \"()\"_{𝑘≥2} and \"()\"_{𝑘≥2} ‣ 5. Lazy random walks converge to displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs"), noticing that $0\leq N_{t}\leq N\leq\ell.$ This completes the proof of ([7.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E5 "In Proof. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

We are going to apply Corollary [7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E3 "Corollary 7.3. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") to the solution $\widehat{P}^{k}$ of ([S dyn k](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.Ex17 "In Optimal transport appears at the limit of the slowing down procedure ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), for any large enough $k\geq 1$. Hence, we have to check that $\int_{[0,1]}\mu^{k}\widehat{J}^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})/\mu^{k}J^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,dt>1.$ By ([2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S2.E3 "In item 0b ‣ item (

                      R

                  ) ‣ Hypotheses 2.1. ‣ The underlying hypotheses ‣ 2. Main results about the displacement random walks ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and since it is assumed that $d(x,y)\geq 1$ for all distinct $x,y\in\mathcal{X}$,

|  | $\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\mu^{k}J^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\leq\bar{J}/k$ |  | (7.6) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where $\bar{J}:=\sup_{t,x}J_{t,x}(\mathcal{X})<\infty$ and with ([7.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E5 "In Proof. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")),

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{[0,1]}\mu^{k}\widehat{J}^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,dt=\int_{[0,1]}\mu\widehat{J}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,dt=:K>0.$ |  | (7.7) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

It follows that $\int_{[0,1]}\mu^{k}\widehat{J}^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})/\mu^{k}J^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,dt\geq Kk/(2\bar{J})>1$, for any large enough $k$.

Corollary [7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E3 "Corollary 7.3. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs") tells us that there exists $K_{k}>0$ such that$\mu^{k}\widehat{J}^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})-\mu^{k}J^{k}_{t}(\mathcal{X}^{2})=K_{k},\ \textrm{for all }t\in{[0,1]}.$ With ([7.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E6 "In Proof. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) and ([7.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E7 "In Proof. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), integrating and taking the limit leads us to

|  | $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{[0,t]}\mu^{k}\widehat{J}^{k}_{s}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,ds=Kt,\quad t\in{[0,1]}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

We conclude with ([7.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E5 "In Proof. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) that $\int_{[0,t]}\mu\widehat{J}_{s}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\,ds=Kt$ for all $0\leq t\leq 1,$ which is the announced result.
∎

### Proof of Proposition [7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S7.E1 "Proposition 7.1. ‣ Main results of the section ‣ 7. Conservation of the average rate of mass displacement ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")

A consequence of Girsanov’s formula, see [[Léo12b](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx14), Thm. 2.11] for a related result, is

|  | $H(P&#124;R)=H(P_{0}&#124;m)+\int_{[0,1]}dt\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\rho^{*}\left(\frac{dj_{t,x}}{dJ_{t,x}}(y)\right)\,P_{t}(dx)J_{t,x}(dy),$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where

|  | $\rho^{*}(a):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a\log a-a+1&\textrm{if }a>0,\\ 1&\textrm{if }a=0,\\ \infty&\textrm{if }a<0.\end{array}\right.$ |  |

## Appendix A Random walk on a graph

We give some basic informations about random walks on a graph.

### The jump kernel

A Markov random walk on the graph $(\mathcal{X},\sim~)$ is a time-continuous Markov process which is specified by its infinitesimal generator $L=(L_{t})_{0\leq t<1}$ acting on any real function $u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}$ with a finite support via the formula

|  | $L_{t}u(x)=\sum_{y:y\sim x}[u(y)-u(x)]\,j_{t,x}(y),\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},\ 0\leq t<1$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $j_{t,x}(y)\geq 0$ is the average frequency of jumps from $x$ to $y$ at time $t$.
As a convention, we set $j_{t,x}(y)=0$ as soon as $x$ and $y$ are not neighbours. The corresponding jump kernel is

|  | $j_{t,x}:=\sum_{y:y\sim x}j_{t,x}(y)\delta_{y}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}),\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},\ 0\leq t<1.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

The operator $L$ is well-defined for any bounded function $u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}$ provided that

|  | $\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}j_{t,x}(\mathcal{X})<\infty,$ |  | (A.1) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where $j_{t,x}(\mathcal{X}):=\sum_{y:y\sim x}j_{t,x}(y)$ denotes the global jump intensity at $x\in\mathcal{X}$. The bound ([A.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E1 "In The jump kernel ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) ensures that the random walk performs almost surely finitely many jumps during the unit time interval $[0,1].$ Therefore, $\Omega$ as defined above is the relevant path space to be considered.
When $j$ is assumed to satisfy ([A.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E1 "In The jump kernel ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), it uniquely specifies $Q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\Omega)$ up to its initial law $Q_{0}\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$.

In case $L$ doesn’t depend on $t$, the random walk is said to be time-homogeneous.
In this special case, its dynamics is described as follows. Once at site $x,$ the walker waits during a random time with exponential law $\mathcal{E}(j_{x}(\mathcal{X}))$, and then decides to jump at $y$ according to the probability $j_{x}(\mathcal{X})^{-1}\sum_{y:y\sim x}j_{x}(y)\delta_{y},$ and so on; all these random events being mutually independent.

### Some interesting examples of reference random walks $R$

One may require that $R$ is reversible. This means that it is time-homogeneous and that there is a (possibly unbounded) positive measure $m\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X})$ on $\mathcal{X}$ such that, not only $R$ is $m$-stationary i.e.:$R_{t}=m,\forall 0\leq t\leq 1,$ but also that $R$ is invariant with respect to time reversal i.e.: for any subinterval $[u,v]\subset{[0,1]},$

|  | $(X_{(u+v-t)^{-}};u\leq t\leq v)_{\#}R=(X_{t};u\leq t\leq v)_{\#}R.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

This happens if and only if the following time-homogeneous detailed balance condition

|  | $m_{x}J_{x}(y)=m_{y}J_{y}(x),\quad\forall x\sim y\in\mathcal{X}$ |  | (A.2) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

is satisfied; compare ([1.15](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#S1.E15 "In The discrete metric measure graph (𝒳,∼,𝑑,𝑚) ‣ 1. Defining displacement interpolations on a graph by analogy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")). As it is assumed that$J_{x}(y)>0,\forall x\sim y$ and the graph is irreducible, this implies that $m_{x}>0$ for all $x\in\mathcal{X}.$

#### Simple random walk

An important example of such a walk is the simple random walk $R^{o}\in\mathrm{P}(\Omega)$ on $\mathcal{X}.$ The dynamics of $R^{o}$ is specified by the jump kernel

|  | $J^{o}_{x}:=n_{x}^{-1}\sum_{y:y\sim x}\delta_{y},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X}.$ |  | (A.3) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

The successive waiting times are independent and identically distributed with the exponential law $\mathcal{E}(1)$ and the walker jumps from any site choosing a neighbour uniformly at random. Solving ([A.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E2 "In Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), one sees that the corresponding reversing measures are multiples of

|  | $m^{o}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}n_{x}\delta_{x}.$ |  | (A.4) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

Note that $m^{o}$ is unbounded whenever $\mathcal{X}$ is an infinite set, since the irreducibility assumption implies that $n_{x}\geq 1$ for all $x.$
As the simple random walk is analogous to the Brownian motion on a Riemannian manifold, the measure $m^{o}$ plays the role of the volume measure on the graph.

#### Counting random walk

It corresponds to$J_{x}=\sum_{y:y\sim x}\delta_{y},x\in\mathcal{X}$ whose reversing measure is the counting measure $m=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\delta_{x}.$

#### A generic class of $m$-reversible random walks

Take some measure $m=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}m_{x}\delta_{x}$ on $\mathcal{X}$ with $m_{x}>0,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}$ and consider the jump kernel

|  | $J_{x}:=\sum_{y:y\sim x}\frac{s(x,y)}{\sqrt{n_{x}n_{y}}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{y}}{m_{x}}}\,\delta_{y},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},$ |  | (A.5) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

where $s$ is a symmetric function.
Assume that
there exists some constant $1\leq c<\infty$ such that

|  | $m_{y}/n_{y}\leq c\ m_{x}/n_{x},\quad\forall x\sim y$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

and some $0<\sigma<\infty$ such that the symmetric function $s$ satisfies

|  | $0<s(x,y)=s(y,x)\leq\sigma,\qquad\forall x\sim y\in\mathcal{X}.$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

Then, $J$ verifies ([A.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E1 "In The jump kernel ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), so that $R$ is a measure on $\Omega.$ As $J$ clearly verifies ([A.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A1.E2 "In Some interesting examples of reference random walks 𝑅 ‣ Appendix A Random walk on a graph ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")), $R$ is $m$-reversible. Moreover, $R$ is equivalent to the simple random walk $R^{o}$, i.e. for any measurable $A\subset\Omega,$ $R(A)>0\iff R^{o}(A)>0$.

## Appendix B Relative entropy

This section is a short version of [[Léoa](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#bib.bibx11), § 2] which we refer to for more details.
Let $r$ be some $\sigma$-finite positive measure on some space $Y$. The relative entropy of the probability measure $p$ with respect to $r$ is loosely defined by

|  | $H(p&#124;r):=\int_{Y}\log(dp/dr)\,dp\in(-\infty,\infty],\qquad p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

if $p\ll r$ and $H(p&#124;r)=\infty$ otherwise.

More precisely, when $r$ is a probability measure, we have

|  | $H(p&#124;r)=\int_{Y}h(dp/dr)\,dr\in[0,\infty],\qquad p,r\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with $h(a)=a\log a-a+1\geq 0$ for all $a\geq 0,$ (set $h(0)=1).$ Hence, the above definition is meaningful. It follows from the strict convexity of $h$ that $H(\cdot&#124;r)$ is also strictly convex. In addition, since $h(a)=\inf h=0\iff a=1,$ we also have for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y),$

|  | $H(p&#124;r)=\inf H(\cdot&#124;r)=0\iff p=r.$ |  | (B.1) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

If $r$ is unbounded, one must restrict the definition of $H(\cdot&#124;r)$ to some subset of $\mathrm{P}(Y)$ as follows. As $r$ is assumed to be $\sigma$-finite, there exists a measurable function $W:Y\to[1,\infty)$ such that

|  | $z_{W}:=\int_{Y}e^{-W}\,dr<\infty.$ |  | (B.2) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

Define the probability measure $r_{W}:=z_{W}^{-1}e^{-W}\,r$ so that $\log(dp/dr)=\log(dp/dr_{W})-W-\log z_{W}.$ It follows that for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ satisfying $\int_{Y}W\,dp<\infty,$ the formula

|  | $H(p&#124;r):=H(p&#124;r_{W})-\int_{Y}W\,dp-\log z_{W}\in(-\infty,\infty]$ |  | (B.3) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

is a meaningful definition of the relative entropy which is coherent in the following sense. If $\int_{Y}W^{\prime}\,dp<\infty$ for another measurable function $W^{\prime}:Y\to[0,\infty)$ such that $z_{W^{\prime}}<\infty,$ then $H(p&#124;r_{W})-\int_{Y}W\,dp-\log z_{W}=H(p&#124;r_{W^{\prime}})-\int_{Y}W^{\prime}\,dp-\log z_{W^{\prime}}\in(-\infty,\infty]$.

Therefore, $H(p&#124;r)$ is well-defined for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ such that $\int_{Y}W\,dp<\infty$ for some measurable nonnegative function $W$ verifying ([B.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A2.E2 "In Appendix B Relative entropy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")).

It follows from the strict convexity of $H(\cdot&#124;r_{W})$ and ([B.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A2.E3 "In Appendix B Relative entropy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) that $H(\cdot&#124;r)$ is also strictly convex.

Let $Y$ and $Z$ be two Polish spaces equipped with their Borel $\sigma$-fields. For any measurable function $\phi:Y\to Z$ and any measure $q\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(Y)$ we have the disintegration formula

|  | $q(dy)=\int_{Z}q(dy&#124;\phi=z)\,\phi_{\#}q(dz)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

where $z\in Z\mapsto q(\cdot&#124;\phi=z)\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ is measurable, and the following additivity property

|  | $H(p&#124;r)=H(\phi_{\#}p&#124;\phi_{\#}r)+\int_{Z}H\Big(p(\cdot\mid\phi=z)&#124;r(\cdot\mid\phi=z)\Big)\,\phi_{\#}p(dz),$ |  | (B.4) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |

is valid for any $p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ and any $\sigma$-finite $r\in\mathrm{M}_{+}(Y).$ In particular, as $r(\cdot\mid\phi=z)$ is a probability measure for each $z$, with ([B.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1308.0226v1#A2.E1 "In Appendix B Relative entropy ‣ Lazy random walks and optimal transport on graphs")) we see that

|  | $H(\phi_{\#}p&#124;\phi_{\#}r)\leq H(p&#124;r),\quad\forall p\in\mathrm{P}(Y)$ |  |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- |

with equality if and only if

|  | $p(\cdot\mid\phi=z)=r(\cdot\mid\phi=z),\quad\forall z,\phi_{\#}p\textrm{-a.s.}$ |  | (B.5) |
| --- | ------------------------------ | --- | ----- |
